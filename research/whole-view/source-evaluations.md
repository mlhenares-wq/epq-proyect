## Source evaluations

Short evaluations in the same format as the Kharroubi example: what the paper examines, why its provenance is or is not reliable, why I chose it, and why I should not rely on it too much.

1. ***Maritime Chokepoints and the Global Economy: Evidence from the Strait of Hormuz* by Enisse Kharroubi (Bank for International Settlements, Working Paper 1378, 2026).** The paper examines how disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz, a major global shipping route, affect the global economy, using ship-traffic data to show that disruptions push up energy prices and then lower world output and raise inflation. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it was published by the BIS, a respected international financial institution owned by central banks. I chose it because it gives me a very in-depth view of one of my case studies, allowing me to gain a greater knowledge of it. However, I should not be over-reliant on it: it is a working paper that has not been peer-reviewed, its cover is dated September 2026 although the abstract says August, and depending on one study could make my work biased.

2. ***Research note on the economic effects of a Strait of Hormuz closure* by Lutz Kilian, Michael Plante and Alexander Richter (Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, March 2026).** The note models what a closure removing close to a fifth of world oil supply would do to oil prices and world growth, under closures lasting one, two or three quarters. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it comes from a US central bank, and Kilian is a leading expert on oil markets. I chose it because its scenarios let me compare what experts expected with what actually happened at Hormuz. However, I should not treat it as evidence of what happened: it was written in March, before anyone knew how long the closure would last, and its headline $98 oil price was soon overtaken when Brent reached $118.

3. ***Macroeconomic Risks from Maritime Trade Disruptions* by Vipin P. Veetil and Fathimath S. Vemmarath (arXiv working paper 2607.09951, 2026).** The paper builds a model of how chokepoint disruptions spread through economies and tests it on the 2026 Hormuz closure. Its provenance is weaker because it is a preprint posted online without peer review, and I could only confirm the authors through an economics database. I chose it because it shows that many tankers switched off their tracking signals during the crisis, which helps me explain why transit counts disagree. However, I should not use it for headline figures, because "dark" ships are not the same as lost traffic and its results have not been checked by other experts.

4. ***I mercati dell'energia sotto pressione: lo shock di Hormuz del 2026* by Alessandro D'Orazio, Simone Emiliozzi, Fabrizio Ferriani, Andrea Gazzani, Marco Taboga and Giovanni Veronese (Banca d'Italia, Questioni di Economia e Finanza No. 1067, October 2026).** The paper examines how the Hormuz closure affected oil, refined-product and gas markets, and what cushioned the shock, such as stocks, emergency reserves and rerouted supply. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it was published by Italy's central bank and written by six named economists, and its main points match the World Bank, the IEA and the EIA. I chose it because it explains why prices rose less than expected and why Asia was hit hardest, which deepens my severity analysis. However, it is written in Italian, only covers events up to mid-2026, and gives a European central bank's perspective, so I should balance it with other regions' views.

5. ***War in MENA: Economic Spillovers and Policy Challenges* by the International Monetary Fund (2026).** The note examines how the war spread economic damage across the Middle East and beyond, especially to food importers and oil exporters. The IMF is a highly reliable producer, but the copy I found is hosted on a bank's website rather than the IMF's own, so I cannot be sure it is the final official version. I chose it because it shows the regional cost of the Hormuz crisis, which my other sources mostly measure globally. However, I should find the original on imf.org before citing it, and remember that the IMF writes to guide governments, so it focuses on policy rather than on causes.

6. ***Shipping Disruptions in the Red Sea: Local Shock, Global Impact* by Jason Dunn and F. Leibovici (VoxEU/CEPR, 2024).** The column uses a model of world trade and shipping to show how losing shipping capacity in the Red Sea raises freight costs and prices and slows the world economy. It is reliable in terms of provenance because its authors are researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis and VoxEU is a respected platform for economists. I chose it because it explains how a local disruption spreads globally, which supports my severity analysis for the Red Sea case. However, its model assumes the shock fades within six months, while the crisis lasted nearly three years, so it probably understates the long-term harm.

7. ***Sailing through Storms: The Fallout of Red Sea Disruptions on Global Trade and Inflation* by Lorenz Emter and co-authors (VoxEU/CEPR, June 2024).** The column examines how the Red Sea attacks affected world trade, oil prices and inflation, and finds the effects were small because shipping had spare capacity and demand was weak. It is reliable in terms of provenance because its authors are economists linked to the European Central Bank, and its findings match the ECB's own estimate and the IMF's trade data. I chose it because it gives balanced evidence that the Red Sea crisis was less severe than it looked. However, it was written in mid-2024, before the 2025 lull and the 2026 blockade, so it cannot describe the whole crisis.

8. ***Distance, Trade, and Income: The 1967 to 1975 Closing of the Suez Canal as a Natural Experiment* by James Feyrer (NBER Working Paper 15557, 2009; published in the *Journal of Development Economics*, 2021).** The paper uses the eight-year closure of the Suez Canal to measure how much longer sea routes reduce trade and income. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it comes from a Dartmouth economist through the National Bureau of Economic Research, and a later version passed peer review in a leading journal. I chose it because it is the main academic study of the severity of my historical Suez case, and the NBER version is free to read. However, another study (CEPII, 2016) finds a smaller effect, so the size of the shock is debated, and I should quote figures from the published 2021 version where possible.

9. ***Strait of Hormuz: Insurance Market* by the Robert S. Strauss Center for International Security and Law, University of Texas (undated).** The page examines how war-risk insurance, insurers' losses and oil prices behaved during the 1980s Tanker War. It is fairly reliable in terms of provenance because it comes from a university research centre and its figures match UPI reports and official oil-price data from the time. I chose it because good economic sources on the Tanker War are rare, and it fills a gap in my historical Hormuz case. However, it has no named author or date, and it is a short summary rather than a full study, so I should check its figures against the original records.

10. ***The Economics of Piracy in South East Asia* by Karsten von Hoesslin (Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime, May 2016).** The report examines how piracy in Southeast Asia works as an organised business, especially the theft of fuel from tankers. Its provenance is only fairly reliable because it is an NGO report by a single researcher, and one of its figures (over 120,000 ships a year in the Malacca and Singapore straits) does not match official traffic counts. I chose it because it explains the motives and methods behind Malacca piracy, which helps me understand why patrols and arrests can deter it. However, it covers the 2014–15 hijacking wave, which falls between my two Malacca periods, so I should use it for background rather than for data.

11. ***Systemic Impacts of Disruptions at Maritime Chokepoints* by Jasper Verschuur, J. Lumma and Jim W. Hall (*Nature Communications*, 2025).** The paper estimates how much trade is at risk at 24 chokepoints worldwide and how much disruption costs each year. It is highly reliable in terms of provenance because it was peer-reviewed and published in a leading scientific journal by University of Oxford researchers, and it is free to read. I chose it because it puts all three of my case studies on one scale and shows which countries are most exposed. However, it estimates average yearly losses from a model, so it smooths out rare, extreme events like the 2026 Hormuz closure, and I should not use it to describe any single crisis.
