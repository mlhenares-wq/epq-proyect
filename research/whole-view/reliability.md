## Reliability analysis

Each paper's main claims were checked against the project's own graded sources: the data books, the provenance files and the judgement analysis. Grades use the same Admiralty Code (A–F for the producer, 1–6 for the content). As before, only search-result text could be seen, not full papers, so no content grade is better than 2 unless independent sources confirm the claim.

### 1. Kharroubi (2026), BIS Working Paper 1378 — B2

**What it says.** It uses IMF/Oxford PortWatch traffic data and finds that Hormuz disruptions act as a stagflationary force. Energy and other commodity prices rise after a traffic shock, followed by lower world output and higher consumer prices.

**Cross-check.** The direction matches the observed data. Brent rose 65%, from $71.36 to $118 (FRED, A1; EIA, A1). The World Bank called it the largest oil supply shock on record (World Bank, April 2026, A2). Exports through the strait fell below 10% of normal (IEA, 11 March 2026, A1).

**Reliability.** The BIS is a highly reputable producer, but working papers are not peer-reviewed. The search snippets confirm only the stagflation finding and the data source. The output, inflation and bond-spread results come from one summary and need checking in the PDF. The paper is dated two ways: September 2026 on the cover and August 2026 on the abstract, so cite it as September 2026. The free PDF is [here](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-1378-maritime-chokepoints-and-global-economy-evidence-strait-hormuz.pdf).

### 2. Kilian, Plante and Richter (2026), Dallas Fed — B2 as a model, B3 as a forecast

**What it says.** In its main scenario, a closure removing close to 20% of world oil supply in the second quarter of 2026 pushes WTI to about $98 a barrel and cuts annualised world growth by 2.9 points in that quarter. Its other scenarios run from $68 (reopening after one quarter) to $132 by year-end (closed for three quarters). The authors stress that these are scenarios, not forecasts.

**Cross-check.**
- **Supply loss: agrees.** About 20 mb/d used the strait before the war (IEA, 11 March 2026, A1), roughly a fifth of world oil use.
- **Prices: below what happened.** WTI was already above $97 when the note came out on 20 March (Bloomberg, B2). Brent reached $118 on 31 March (EIA, A1) and possibly about $126 in late April (C–D sources). The closure lasted far longer than one quarter (7.7 months by 19 October), so the relevant scenario is the longest one, not the headline $98.
- **Later context.** The IMF said in July that oil prices had stayed in a moderate range thanks to stock releases and spare supply. That fits the IEA release (A1) and Bordoff and O'Sullivan (B2).

**Reliability.** The producer is strong, and Kilian is a leading oil-market economist. The model outputs are reported the same way by several outlets, so what the model says is not in doubt. But a March scenario cannot be read as a measurement of what happened, so grade it 3 when used as a prediction.

### 3. Veetil and Vemmarath (2026), "Macroeconomic Risks from Maritime Trade Disruptions" — C3

**What it says.** It models how chokepoint disruptions spread through economies and tests the model on the 2026 closure, using PortWatch and IEA data. It reports that 62–70% of tanker transits went "dark" (ship-tracking signals switched off) in the acute phases.

**Cross-check.** This is not the same as traffic lost: the IEA (A1) and UNCTAD (A1) put the fall at 90–95%. It does support the project's warning, in the Hormuz attribution file, that ship-tracking and US military counts disagreed widely (about 650 against about 1,000 transits: gCaptain/Bloomberg, B2). Tracker-based transit counts in 2026 should therefore be treated with care.

**Reliability.** The authors are named only on RePEc. arXiv preprints are not peer-reviewed, and economics on arXiv is less established than on SSRN or NBER. Use it for the "dark ships" caveat, not for headline figures.

### 4. D'Orazio, Emiliozzi, Ferriani, Gazzani, Taboga and Veronese (2026), Banca d'Italia QEF 1067 — A2

**What it says.** Titled "I mercati dell'energia sotto pressione: lo shock di Hormuz del 2026", it calls the crisis the largest supply interruption ever recorded in the oil market. It finds that price rises were cushioned by high stocks, emergency reserves, rerouted flows and falling demand. Asia felt the direct effects most, while Europe was hit mainly through prices and competition for alternative supplies.

**Cross-check.** It agrees with independent sources on four points:
- **Scale:** the World Bank's "largest oil supply shock on record" (A2).
- **Cushions:** the IEA's 400-million-barrel release (A1), and Bordoff and O'Sullivan's credit to oversupply and pipelines (Foreign Affairs, B2).
- **Asia:** oil flows through Malacca fell from 23.2 to 16.6 mb/d because of the Hormuz closure (EIA, A1; Kompas, C2).
- **Bypasses:** pipelines can carry only about 9 of the 20 mb/d (Al Jazeera, B2).

**Reliability.** A central bank's published series with six named economists. Four independent agreements would justify A1, but only the abstract was seen, so it stays at A2. It is in Italian, and covers events only up to mid-2026.

### 5. IMF (2026), War in MENA: Economic Spillovers and Policy Challenges — A3 until confirmed

**What it says.** The IMF's regional assessment of the war's spillovers.

**Cross-check.** It is consistent with the IMF's April regional briefing (as reported by News First, C2): food-importing economies were hit hardest, and five of eight conflict-affected oil exporters were expected to shrink in 2026. It is also consistent with Chapter 1 of the October *World Economic Outlook*.

**Reliability.** The IMF is an A-grade producer, but this PDF sits on a Lebanese bank's blog, not on imf.org. The text cannot be confirmed as the IMF's final version. Find the original on imf.org before citing; until then treat it as A3.

### 6. Dunn and Leibovici (2024), "Shipping disruptions in the Red Sea: local shock, global impact" — B2

**What it says.** A multi-country model of trade and shipping, estimated on pre-crisis data, finds that losing shipping capacity cuts trade volumes, raises shipping and traded-goods prices and slows world activity. It notes that average global shipping costs more than doubled.

**Cross-check.**
- **Direction:** agrees with UNCTAD (A1), which found Suez transits down 42% by February 2024.
- **Cost figure: conservative.** The Asia–Europe container rate rose 246% by January 2024 (Drewry WCI, B2), more than tripling. A world average would rise less than this single route, so "more than doubled" fits.
- **Duration: assumed too short.** The scenario assumes shocks fade within six months. In reality the crisis had lasted 35 months by October 2026, and transits were still about 60% down 100 days after the attacks stopped (Container News citing BIMCO, C2). The model therefore understates long-run harm.

**Reliability.** Written by St. Louis Fed researchers (Jason Dunn is a senior research associate there), from a working paper the search could not open. Strong on mechanism, weak on duration.

### 7. Emter and co-authors (2024), "Sailing through storms" — B2

**What it says.** It finds muted effects on trade and inflation, thanks to spare shipping capacity, uncongested ports, weak demand and good inventories. It reports Bab el-Mandeb transits down 70% on December 2023 (PortWatch, to 31 March 2024), and Brent back below its level after the first attacks.

**Cross-check.** It agrees closely with the project's data:
- **Brent:** rose only about 6% (Engine and World Oil, C2).
- **Inflation:** the ECB put the extra euro-area inflation at only about 0.25 points even in an escalation scenario (ECB, A2).
- **Trade:** the 70% fall at Bab el-Mandeb sits between the IMF's 50% fall in Suez trade volume (A1) and UNCTAD's 70% fall in tonnage (A1).

**Reliability.** Written by ECB-linked economists (Lorenz Emter's CEPR profile lists the column; check the other names on the page). It is dated June 2024, so it does not cover the 84% fall in attacks in 2025 (ACLED, B2) or the Saudi blockade of July 2026 (AP, B1). Good for 2024, out of date after that.

### 8. Feyrer (2009), NBER Working Paper 15557 — A2

**What it says.** A 10% shorter sea route raises trade by about 5%, and each extra dollar of trade raises income by about 25 cents. Distance matters about half as much as typical estimates suggest.

**Cross-check.** It is the same study as Feyrer (2021) in the *Journal of Development Economics*, graded A2 in the provenance files. The judgement analysis already uses the "half as much" finding. CEPII (2016, B2) finds an even smaller effect, so the size of the 1967 trade shock is contested.

**Reliability.** Strong: an academic working paper that later passed peer review. Quote numbers from the 2021 version where you can, because figures can change between working paper and journal.

### 9. Strauss Center, "Strait of Hormuz: Insurance Market" — B2

**What it says.** Kharg Island cover roughly doubled when Iraq declared an exclusion zone. Marine insurers lost heavily, while some war-risk underwriters made large profits. Iran cut its oil price to offset premiums, and real oil prices fell through the 1980s. Of 239 tankers attacked, 55 (23%) were sunk or written off.

**Cross-check.** All three main claims are confirmed:
- **Kharg cover:** rose from 0.75% to 1.5% of hull value in May 1984 (UPI, 15 May 1984, B2).
- **Oil prices:** Brent rose only 6.9% in mid-1987 (FRED, A1).
- **Losses:** "fewer than a quarter of the ships hit were sunk or disabled" is already in the project from the same centre.

The centre's two traffic figures (a 25% fall in shipping, and under 2% of ships hit) look contradictory but measure different things: one is traffic that stayed away, the other is ships attacked.

**Reliability.** A university centre with no named author or date, but its figures are confirmed by records from the time.

### 10. von Hoesslin (2016), "The Economics of Piracy in South East Asia" — C2

**What it says.** Indonesian-style piracy is organised, profitable theft of fuel and cargo, often with inside help and little violence. More than 120,000 ships a year pass through the Straits of Malacca and Singapore.

**Cross-check.**
- **Traffic figure: too high.** The Malaysian traffic system counted 63,636 transits in 2004 (IALA, B2) and a record 102,525 in 2025 (Seatrade, C2). A figure of 120,000 for 2016 is not credible as transits; it probably counts something broader.
- **Type of crime: fits.** Its picture of low-violence theft matches the project's evidence for 2025: no Asian incident reached ReCAAP's most serious category, and 53% were petty theft (ReCAAP, A1).

**Reliability.** An NGO report by one consultant-researcher, written about the 2014–15 tanker-hijacking wave, which falls between the EPQ's Malacca periods. Use it for background on how piracy works, not for figures.

### 11. Verschuur, Lumma and Hall (2025), *Nature Communications* — A2

**What it says.** Across 24 chokepoints, about $192bn of trade is at risk each year, with about $10.7bn of direct losses and $3.4bn from higher freight costs. The risk is concentrated at the Taiwan Strait, Suez and Bab el-Mandeb.

**Cross-check.** It agrees with the Red Sea case: Bab el-Mandeb and Suez carry the largest share of losses, and Egypt is among the most exposed countries. Canal revenue fell from $10.25bn to $3.99bn (Egypt Today, D2), and Sisi claimed about $7bn lost (Reuters, B2). Its annual averages are far below the 2026 Hormuz shock, because a modelled average smooths out rare, extreme events. That gap is a useful point for the evaluation section.

**Reliability.** Peer-reviewed, open access and already in the Top 80. The strongest source in this set.

### Overall verdict

- **Most reliable:** Verschuur et al., Feyrer and Banca d'Italia, each confirmed by independent A- and B-graded data.
- **Reliable for mechanisms, weaker on figures:** Kharroubi, Dunn and Leibovici, and the Strauss Center.
- **Use with care:** the Dallas Fed and the 2024 Red Sea column (both scenarios or snapshots overtaken by events); the IMF note (until found on imf.org); the arXiv preprint and the Global Initiative report (not peer-reviewed, and one figure that does not fit).
- **The papers support the EPQ's severity findings.** Hormuz 2026 was by far the most severe, the Red Sea's economic effects were muted, and the 1967 closure's trade effect was real but smaller than once thought. None of them tests whether multilateral cooperation reduced risk, so they strengthen the severity side of the analysis, not the cooperation verdict.
