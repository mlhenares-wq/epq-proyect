## Strait of Malacca: the piracy peak, the fall, and 2025–26

Multilateral cooperation was a major reason why risk fell in the Strait of Malacca, but not the main one. Risk stayed low throughout, because piracy never stopped or slowed trade. Cooperation mainly changed how insurers judged the strait and, in 2025, helped national police act.

### Likelihood

The IMB series for the Malacca Strait itself shows a sharp rise and an even sharper fall.

| Year | IMB attacks, Malacca Strait | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1999 | 2 | ICC-IMB, 2001, A1 |
| 2000 | 75 | ICC-IMB, 2001, A1 |
| 2002 | 16 | Al Jazeera, 2003, B2* |
| 2004 | 37 or 38 | NUS/Beckman, 2012, B2 (37); ICC-IMB, 2006, A2 (38) |
| 2005 | 12 (one report says 18) | ICC-IMB, 2006, A2; Gulf News, 2006, C3 |
| 2008 | 2 | ICC-IMB, 2009, A2* |

The steepest fall, from 75 to 16 (−79%, derived), came in 2000–02. That was before any multilateral patrol existed.

Today the problem sits elsewhere. ReCAAP counted 108 incidents in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore in 2025, the most since 2007 (ReCAAP ISC Annual Report 2025, A1). But 107 were in the Singapore Strait and only 1 in the Malacca Strait (same source).

The IMB, which draws its areas differently, recorded 80 in the "Singapore Straits" (ICC-IMB, 2026, A2). The surge then reversed. ReCAAP logged 21 incidents in the first half of 2026, down 74% on 80 (ReCAAP ISC Half-Yearly Report 2026, A1).

The type of crime matters as much as the count. In 2025 no Asian incident reached CAT 1, the worst level, and 53% were CAT 4 petty theft (ReCAAP ISC Annual Report 2025, A1). These shares cover all of Asia; no breakdown for the straits alone was found.

Thieves usually took engine spares, scrap or crew belongings, and often nothing (PortNews, 2026, C2). The peak was different: at least 20 sailors were kidnapped in May–December 2004 (Reuters via The Straits Times, 2004, B2*).

### Severity

Severity was low in every period. In 2004, 63,636 ships reported to the Malacca traffic system (IALA, n.d., B2*). In 2025 a record 102,525 passed through (Seatrade Maritime, 2026, C2).

The two series measure slightly different things, but both rise. ReCAAP said the 2025 rise "does not indicate a corresponding increase in threat to maritime trade" (Free Malaysia Today, 2026, C1).

The only measurable cost came from insurers. Lloyd's Joint War Committee (JWC) listed the strait as a war-risk area in June 2005 (RSIS, 2005, B2). Premiums reached "nearly 0.4%" of hull value, about US$150,000 per port call (Maritime Executive, 2005, C2).

Estimates conflict: other reports say about 1% of cargo value (Maritime Executive, 2005, C2), RSIS about US$5,000 per trip (RSIS, 2006, B2*). The listing ended on 7 August 2006, after about 14 months (inforMARE, 2006, C1). Ships never abandoned the route, and no source shows any trade loss.

### What multilateral cooperation did

MALSINDO sea patrols by Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore began on 20 July 2004, with about 5–7 ships per navy (YaleGlobal, 2004, B1). Each navy stayed in its own waters, with no hot pursuit across borders (The Diplomat, 2015, C1). "Eyes in the Sky" air patrols began on 13 September 2005 (MINDEF, 2005, B1).

Their reach was limited. About 8 sorties a week were flown against about 70 needed, in daytime only, while pirates struck at night (Marine Policy, 2026, A3). Thailand became a full member of the patrols on 18 September 2008 (MINDEF, 2008, B1).

ReCAAP, Asia's first government-to-government anti-piracy treaty, came into force on 4 September 2006 (ReCAAP Agreement, 2004, A1). Its Information Sharing Centre opened in Singapore on 29 November 2006 (Today, 2006, C1). Yet Malaysia and Indonesia, the two biggest coastal states, are not parties (ReCAAP Agreement, 2004, A1).

Sovereignty shaped this design. In 2004 the US floated its Regional Maritime Security Initiative (RMSI), with special forces on fast boats (RSIS/IDSS, 2004, B2). Malaysia replied that "security should not be an excuse to compromise on sovereignty" (The Straits Times, 2004, C1).

Indonesia also saw the RMSI as too military and designed without it (Febrica, 2014, A2). The RMSI failed, while ReCAAP's information-sharing model did better (Marine Policy, 2023, A2). Outside pressure thus pushed the coastal states into their own, looser system.

Cooperation's best-documented success was with insurers. The JWC kept the strait listed in January 2006 despite lobbying (Business Times, 2006, C2). It delisted only after its consultant reviewed the coastal states' measures, including seven air patrols a week (Business Insurance, 2006, C1).

In 2025, shared information supported national police. ReCAAP warned on 3 February 2025 of 11 incidents since 1 January (ReCAAP Incident Alert 01/2025, A1). On 9 July 2025, Indonesian police in the Riau Islands arrested 11 members of a gang (Maritime Executive, 2025, C1*).

The arrests used CCTV footage supplied by ships (ReCAAP ISC Annual Report 2025, A1). Singapore Strait robberies then fell from 14 in July to 2 in August and 1 in September (MINDEF, 2025, B2).

### What else explains the change

The tsunami hit Aceh on 26 December 2004. An IMB official said: "The tsunami seems to have really succeeded where the joint patrols could not" (Al Jazeera, 2005, B2). The effect was short, as attacks resumed by March 2005 (ORF, 2005, F6*).

The Aceh peace deal followed on 15 August 2005 (Peace Accords Matrix, n.d., B1*). Some rebels were suspected of piracy, and one report links the fall off Belawan to the deal (Gulf News, 2006, C3). It probably explains why attacks stayed low, not why they first fell.

Indonesia's economy recovered after the Asian financial crisis. Poverty fell from about 23–24% in 1999 to about 13% in 2010 (SMERU, n.d., B2*). But Indonesian incidents rose from 15 in 2009 to 106 in 2013 as the economy grew (CIMSEC, 2014, C2).

The ISPS Code brought new ship security rules from 1 July 2004. Yet the IMB saw "no dramatic decline in attacks" afterwards (ICC-IMB, 2005, A2*). Industry precautions mattered more in 2026, when ReCAAP's director credited them alongside enforcement (Splash247, 2026, C1).

Connected crises are a weak factor. One unchecked report says analysts linked part of the 2025 rise to ships diverted from the Red Sea (Malay Mail, 2025, C6).

### Judgement

Multilateral cooperation was a major reason among several, not the main reason. Most of the fall came before MALSINDO, so the timing does not fit. The project's attribution test gives the patrols 15.8%, tied with the tsunami (A3 attribution test, derived).

Adding ReCAAP lifts the multilateral share to 23.7%, exactly 1.5 times the tsunami's (A3 attribution test, derived). That passes the "main reason" rule only on a knife-edge. Worse, cooperation was at its strongest in 2025, the year with the most incidents since 2007.

Cooperation did little for severity, because severity was never high. Its real gain was on likelihood as insurers saw it, and on feeding national police the information they needed.

The definitional point could change the verdict. MALSINDO was designed so that each navy patrols its own waters. If those national patrols count as cooperation, the multilateral share rises to 36.8%, 2.3 times the tsunami's (A3 attribution test, derived).

On that reading, cooperation becomes the main reason. But the 2000–02 fall happened with no coordination, and the 2025 surge happened inside the same system.

So cooperation is best seen as a necessary condition for keeping risk low, locking in a fall other forces started. Confidence is medium: the IMB and ReCAAP count differently, many figures come from search snippets, and attribution scores are judgements.
