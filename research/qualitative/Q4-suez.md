## Suez Canal and Red Sea: the 1967–75 closure vs the 2023–26 Houthi crisis

Multilateral cooperation was much stronger in 2023–26 than in 1967–75, yet it explains only a small part of the change. It cut the harm done by each attack. It did little to stop attacks or bring ships back.

*Source note: nearly every figure here was seen only in search-result snippets, not full pages. Grades follow the project compendium. "Ungraded" means a web-search figure the compendium does not list.*

### Likelihood

In June 1967 Egypt closed the canal itself. It was "blocked at both ends by scuttled Egyptian ships" (Time, 1967, B2). Disruption was therefore certain, not a matter of chance.

It happened because the canal became a front line after the Six-Day War. UN observers watched a ceasefire along it, starting with 43 observers at 9 posts (UN S/8053/Add.4, 1967, ungraded). No ships sailed, so there were no attacks to count and no premiums to price.

In 2023–26 disruption was likely, but never certain. The Houthis attacked selected ships linked to Israel, the US or the UK, and later Saudi Arabia (AP, 2026, B1). Counts range from 99 attacks by early 2026 (Container News citing Lloyd's List, 2026, C2) to over 100 ships by December 2024 (Seatrade, 2025, C2).

Attacks were likely because the Gaza war gave a motive. Cheap weapons and the narrow Bab el-Mandeb gave the means (Arellanes, 2025, A2).

Attacks were not certain for any one ship. China negotiated passage directly (Reuters via Arab News, 2026, B2), and attacks fell 84% in 2025 (ACLED, 2025, B2).

### Severity

| Measure | 1967–75 | 2023–26 |
|---|---|---|
| Canal traffic | Zero transits (Time, 1967, B2) | ~26,400 (2023) → ~13,200 (2024) (Ahram Online citing the SCA, 2025, D2) |
| Canal revenue | No figure found; a UN study put lost tolls at $250m a year (AP, 2021, B1) | $10.25bn → $3.99bn (Egypt Today citing the SCA, 2025, D2); $10.2bn → ~$4bn (Ahram Online, 2025, D2) |
| Duration | 8 years, to 5 June 1975 (AP, 1975, B1) | About 3 years, still ongoing (AP, 2026, B1) |
| Freight | Tanker rates "doubled" by July 1967 (NYT via a blog, ungraded) | Shanghai–Rotterdam +246% by 18 January 2024 (Hellenic Shipping News citing Drewry, 2024, D1) |
| War-risk premium | No data: no transits | ~0.01% implied (Lloyd's List, 2024, B2) or ~0.5% (Reuters, 2024, B2) before; ~1% in January 2024 (Lloyd's List, 2024, B2); up to 7% for Saudi-linked ships in 2026 (Reuters, 2026, B2) |

Both Egyptian revenue figures come from the canal authority, so they are not independent. The Drewry figure comes from one weekly release.

In 1967, 15 ships were trapped for eight years (Time, c. 1969, B2), though other sources say 14 or 16. By July 2025 four ships had sunk (AP via CP24, 2025, ungraded; Bloomberg, 2025, B1). Deaths to July 2025 range from 6 (Iran International) to 8 (Dawn), both ungraded snippets.

Most ships rerouted round the Cape, adding over 10 days (IMF, 2024, A1). Cape arrivals rose 89% by mid-2024 (UNCTAD, 2024, A1). Yet euro-area inflation rose by 0.25 points at most (ECB, 2024, A2).

For 1967–75, Feyrer finds a 10% shorter sea route raises trade by about 5% (Feyrer, 2009, B2). Each extra dollar of trade adds about 25 cents of income (Feyrer, 2009, B2). CEPII finds a much smaller effect (CEPII, 2016, B2), so the size is contested.

Overall, 1967–75 was more severe for trade and duration. 2023–26 was worse for lives and insurance costs.

### What multilateral cooperation did

**1967–75.** UN Resolutions 242 (1967) and 338 (1973) set a framework but had no enforcement. UNEF II, with up to 7,000 troops, policed the 1974 buffer zone (UN Peacekeeping, ungraded). US, UK and French units then cleared the canal from 11 April to 15 December 1974 (CTF-65, 1975, B1).

The clearance removed 686,000 mines (Wikipedia, D2), or over 700,000 explosives (Time, 1974, B4). Nimrod Spar raised 10 wrecks (Boyd, 1976, B2), with the US paying about $15m (Time, 1974, B2). But clearance began seven years late, after the 1973 war and a US-brokered deal (Time, 1975, B2).

**2023–26.** Prosperity Guardian launched 29 days after the first attack (CNN, 2023, B1). Resolution 2722 passed 11–0–4, with Russia and China abstaining (UN Security Council, 2024, A1).

EU Aspides escorted over 720 ships by August 2026 (EEAS via Lovguiden, 2026, ungraded). Atalanta was extended into the Red Sea (Council of the EU, 2024, A1).

After US/UK strikes on about 60 targets (CSIS, 2024, B2), premiums tripled within a week (Lloyd's List, 2024, B2). The 2025 truce was a bilateral US–Houthi deal brokered by Oman (CBS News, 2025, B1). Two ships sank two months later (Bloomberg, 2025, B1).

Coverage had gaps. Egypt did not publicly join Prosperity Guardian (Mada Masr, 2023, C1). Eritrea rejects outside navies (Shabait, 2025, D3), and Djibouti refused US strikes from its base (gCaptain, n.d., C2, snippet only).

Spain declined Prosperity Guardian, joining only EU, NATO or UN missions (Última Hora, 2023, C1). It also stayed out of Aspides (The Objective, 2024, C1). China joined neither (Al Arabiya and Breaking Defense, 2023–24, C2).

The operation "prevented the loss of civilian lives" but "has not deterred Houthi aggression" (Forces News citing IISS, n.d., C2). Tallis argues the US won tactically but failed strategically (Tallis, 2025, C2).

### What else explains the change

**Type of threat.** A state closure stops all traffic, whatever navies do. A selective threat lets each ship decide. In late 2025 container-ship transits were 86% below 2023, but product tankers only 19% (Container News citing BIMCO, 2026, C2).

**Connected crises.** Attacks paused with Gaza ceasefires and restarted when fighting resumed (Times of Israel and Al Jazeera, 2025, B2). In 1973 a war first prolonged, then ended, the closure (Time, 1973, B3).

The 2026 Hormuz closure pushed Saudi oil into the Red Sea. The Houthis then declared a blockade on Saudi Arabia on 20 July 2026 (AP, 2026, B1).

**Technology.** Cheap drones gave a militia reach; a drone boat hit the *Tutor* (Al Jazeera, 2024, B1). The 1967 closure had encouraged supertankers too big for the canal. *The Spectator* called the 1975 reopening an "anti-climax" (Samuelson, 1975, C2).

**Supply-chain adaptation.** Carriers added ships to keep port calls steady (Yap and Yang, 2024, A2, snippet only). Transport-chain costs to Europe rose only 3–13% (Bedoya-Maya et al., 2025, A2). The canal chief credited falling premiums for ships returning (Ahram Online, 2026, D2).

**SUMED.** This pipeline opened in 1977, so it did not exist in 1967 (SUMED website, ungraded). In 2026 it carried Saudi crude around the blockade (Lloyd's List Intelligence, 2026, B2).

### Judgement

Cooperation reduced the severity of disruption a little, but not its likelihood. My cooperation score rose from 5 to 13, yet overall risk barely moved, from 14.7 to 14.4 (own calculation, file A2). Cooperation ranked sixth of eight causes, with an 8.5% share.

The strongest counter-argument is that the 1974 clearance ended the closure. Navies also saved lives that my index does not measure. But the clearance followed a political settlement, and shipping in 2023–26 followed Gaza and insurance prices.

Verdict: cooperation reduced disruption only to a limited extent. Confidence is medium. Most figures are snippets, the Egyptian data are D-graded, 1967 premiums are missing, and the crisis continues.
