## Strait of Hormuz: the Tanker War, 1980–88 (1–17)

These 17 cover the attacks on shipping from 1984, war-risk insurance, the US reflagging and Earnest Will convoys, the European minesweepers, UN Resolutions 552 and 598, and the 1988 ceasefire. Six are news reports written at the time.

1. **Navias, M.S. and Hooton, E.R. (1996). Tanker Wars: The Assault on Merchant Shipping during the Iran–Iraq Conflict, 1980–1988.** I.B. Tauris (reviewed in *Iranian Studies*, Cambridge). [link](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/iranian-studies/article/abs/tanker-wars-the-assault-on-merchant-shipping-during-the-iraniraq-conflict-19801988-martin-s-navias-and-e-r-horton-london-and-new-york-ib-tauris-publishers-1996-ix-244-pp-including-appendix-notes-index-of-ships-and-general-index-cloth/555128C86253A41B2A6257F098EC20C2) — **Grade A2** · Type: Academic book

     Summary and analysis: The standard scholarly history of the Tanker War, 244 pages with an appendix and an index of every ship attacked. It shows that Iraq began attacking shipping in 1981 and that Iran retaliated from 1984, so the "war on shipping" lasted far longer than the US convoy phase of 1987–88. A figure of over 400 seafarers killed is attached to it (via *History Today*), which conflicts with the US Naval Institute's 116 for 1984–87 (entry 3), so say which count you use. Use it as the backbone for the incidents-per-year measure and the length of disruption, and as the neutral reference against which to check wire-service claims. It also describes the European and US naval responses, so it helps weigh joint operations against the belligerents' own choices.

     Reliability: Academic book reviewed in journals, but dated (1996), needs library access, and may duplicate plan link 8.

2. **Walker, G.K. (2000). The Tanker War, 1980–88: Law and Policy.** US Naval War College, *International Law Studies*, vol. 74. [link](https://digital-commons.usnwc.edu/ils/vol74/iss1/) — **Grade A2** · Type: Academic book (legal)

     Summary and analysis: A free, full-text legal study of the Tanker War. It covers the law of neutral shipping, the right of belligerents to attack merchant ships, the legality of reflagging Kuwaiti tankers under the US flag, convoy and escort rules, and the UN Security Council resolutions, including 598. It is the best source for the formal-frameworks and law-enforcement factors in the 1980s: it explains what the rules allowed, why reflagging gave the US a legal reason to protect ships, and why Resolution 552 (entry 6) had no enforcement mechanism. Use it to judge whether cooperation in 1987–88 was truly multilateral or a US–Kuwait deal wrapped in international law, and to explain why only US-flagged ships were protected while other neutral ships kept being hit.

     Reliability: Authoritative legal study, but published by a US Navy institution, so it takes a US legal viewpoint.

3. **O'Rourke, R. (1988). The Tanker War.** *US Naval Institute Proceedings*, 114(5), May. [link](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1988/may/tanker-war) — **Grade B2** (graded here) · Type: Professional journal (written at the time)

     Summary and analysis: Written in spring 1988 by a naval analyst who later spent decades at the Congressional Research Service, this article calls the Tanker War "a campaign of economic attrition and political intimidation" and reviews the US, European and Soviet naval forces in the Gulf. Its tables are the best year-by-year data: ships attacked rose from 71 (1984) and 47 (1985) to 111 (1986) and 179–181 (1987), with Iraq responsible for 283 and Iran for 168 attacks in 1981–87. Another table gives 116 merchant seafarers killed, 167 wounded and 37+ missing in 1984–87. Use it as the main source for incidents per year and casualties, and to show that attacks peaked in 1987, the year cooperation began, which complicates any claim that escorts lowered likelihood.

     Reliability: An expert professional journal written during the events, but its attack table is sourced to the Washington Post with "other sources vary", and only the archive OCR text and search summary were seen.

4. **Mobley, R.A. (2022). Deterrence Without Escalation: Fresh Insights into U.S. Decisionmaking During Operation Earnest Will.** *Joint Force Quarterly*, 106 (NDU Press). [link](https://ndupress.ndu.edu/Media/News/News-Article-View/Article/3106465/) — **Grade B1** · Type: Professional military journal (archive-based)

     Summary and analysis: Using declassified documents, Mobley shows that US planners assumed mines were not a serious threat and sent no minesweepers ahead of the first convoy, so the lead tanker, *Bridgeton*, was mined. It sets out the problems the US faced: reading Iranian decision-making, producing persuasive intelligence, signalling to Tehran, "achieving deterrence without unintentional escalation" and finding diplomatic off-ramps, and it notes that analysts and commanders disagreed about how Iran would react. Use it for three factors at once: information sharing (intelligence existed but was contested), response speed (minesweepers arrived only after the mining) and joint operations. It also supports the counter-argument that escort cooperation raised short-term risk before it reduced it.

     Reliability: US defence-university journal by a former US intelligence analyst, so it reflects a US view with no Iranian sources.

5. **UN Security Council (1987). Resolution 598 (1987).** UN Digital Library, 20 July 1987. [link](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/137345) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official / primary (UN resolution)

     Summary and analysis: The binding Iran–Iraq ceasefire resolution, adopted 15–0 at the 2750th meeting under Articles 39–40 of the UN Charter. It demands an immediate ceasefire and withdrawal to recognised borders, and it deplores attacks on neutral shipping. It passed just two days before the first Earnest Will convoy, so naval and diplomatic cooperation ran side by side. Iran accepted it only in July 1988, and the ceasefire began on 20 August 1988, after which attacks on shipping stopped. Use it as the main evidence for formal frameworks and great-power agreement, and to test the counter-argument: the year-long gap between adoption and acceptance suggests the resolution worked only once Iran's own military position collapsed (see entries 7 and 14).

     Reliability: Original UN legal text reflecting great-power agreement; it shows diplomacy helped end the Tanker War but not how much.

6. **UN Security Council (1984). Resolution 552 (1984).** UN Security Council, via Refworld, 1 June 1984. [link](https://www.refworld.org/node/80497) — **Grade A1** (graded here) · Type: Official / primary (UN resolution)

     Summary and analysis: Adopted 13–0–2 (Nicaragua and Zimbabwe abstaining) after a complaint of 21 May 1984 from Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the UAE. It condemned recent attacks on commercial ships, demanded that Iran stop attacking ships sailing to and from Kuwaiti and Saudi ports, and affirmed free navigation for shipping bound for non-belligerent states. The Secretary-General's report of December 1984 (S/16877) then recorded a "serious escalation" of attacks, with a list of incidents from June to December 1984. Use it as the first formal multilateral framework of the Tanker War and as evidence that it failed: attacks went on and peaked in 1987 (entry 3). It also targeted only Iran, although Iraq made most 1984 attacks.

     Reliability: Original UN legal text, confirmed by the UN Digital Library and the Secretary-General's own follow-up report, though it records what states demanded, not what happened.

7. **US Naval History and Heritage Command (2018). H-Gram 018: 30th Anniversary of Operation Praying Mantis.** NHHC, Director's Corner, April 2018. [link](https://history.navy.mil/about-us/leadership/director/directors-corner/h-grams/h-gram-018.html) — **Grade B1** (graded here) · Type: Official / primary (US Navy historical record)

     Summary and analysis: The US Navy's official history office explains the road to Praying Mantis: USS *Samuel B. Roberts* hit an Iranian mine on 14 April 1988, and four days later US forces destroyed two Iranian surveillance platforms, sank the frigate *Sahand* and spared the damaged *Sabalan* at the last moment. Iran lost over 60 dead and about 100 wounded in one day. The project data book also cites it for 116 merchant seafarers killed in 1984–87, matching entry 3. Use it for enforcement and response speed: a four-day retaliation that crippled Iran's navy three months before Tehran accepted Resolution 598. Pair it with entries 13 and 14 to ask whether coercion, not cooperation, ended the war.

     Reliability: Official US Navy history whose facts match UPI and Washington Post reports from the time, but written to commemorate the operation, so it presents US action favourably.

8. **Central Intelligence Agency (1987). Iran-Iraq: Lull in the Gulf Anti-Shipping War.** CIA Directorate of Intelligence memorandum NESA M 87-20063, 17 June 1987 (CIA Reading Room). [link](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/node/1976865) — **Grade B2** (graded here) · Type: Official / primary (declassified intelligence memo)

     Summary and analysis: A four-page assessment by the CIA's Iran/Iraq Branch, written five weeks before the first convoy. It judged that both Iran and Iraq saw short-term advantages in the lull in ship attacks, that Iran hoped to turn it into a permanent ceasefire in Gulf waters, and that renewed Iraqi attacks would anger the superpowers. Search summaries add that it expected Iran to resume strikes on unescorted ships, mine waters near Kuwait and possibly target US-flagged ships once escorts began. The *Bridgeton* mining (entry 11) bore this out. Use it for the information-sharing factor: the warning existed but did not produce minesweepers in time, which shows intelligence without a fast response does not cut likelihood.

     Reliability: An authentic declassified government document, but parts are redacted, its judgements are forecasts, and only catalogue entries and excerpts were seen.

9. **UPI (1984). Attack on Persian Gulf tanker "dangerous escalation".** *UPI Archives*, 15 May 1984. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1984/05/15/Attack-on-Persian-Gulf-tanker-dangerous-escalation/6583453441600/) — **Grade B2** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

     Summary and analysis: Covers Iran's first retaliatory strikes on Kuwaiti and Saudi tankers in May 1984. Its key figures are the war-risk premiums: Lloyd's had doubled seven-day cover for Kharg Island from 0.75% to 1.5% of hull value, and after the attack on a Kuwaiti tanker rates rose to 3% of ship value plus 2% of cargo value. The Strauss Center adds that Kharg hull rates reached 7.5% after the *Yanbu Pride* was hit that month. This is the best primary evidence for the insurance (likelihood) measure in the 1980s. Use it with Resolution 552 (entry 6), passed two weeks later, to show that the Gulf states turned to the UN as costs soared, and that premiums stayed high because the resolution changed little.

     Reliability: Wire report from the time; excellent insurance evidence, but check which May story gives each figure.

10. **UPI (1987). The Pentagon raised the toll today to 37 sailors.** *UPI Archives*, 19 May 1987. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1987/05/19/The-Pentagon-raised-the-toll-today-to-37-sailors/3451548395200/) — **Grade B1** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

     Summary and analysis: Reports that two Exocet missiles fired by an Iraqi Mirage F-1 on 17 May 1987 killed 37 sailors on the frigate USS *Stark*, and that the US accepted Saddam Hussein's apology. A related UPI story the same day says the ship's radar failed to pick up the threat, and it never fired back. This is a key counter-argument source: the deadliest attack on a US ship in the Tanker War came from Iraq, the side Washington was tilting towards, which shows the absence of deconfliction and shared air pictures between the US and the belligerents. Use it to argue that information sharing was the weakest cooperation factor in 1987, and that the *Stark* attack hardened US commitment to the Kuwaiti reflagging plan.

     Reliability: Wire report written at the time using Pentagon figures; the death toll is confirmed by many sources.

11. **UPI (1987). The supertanker Bridgeton hit an underwater mine Friday morning.** *UPI Archives*, 24 July 1987. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1987/07/24/The-supertanker-Bridgeton-hit-an-underwater-mine-Friday-morning/4397554097600/) — **Grade B1** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

     Summary and analysis: A same-day report that the reflagged supertanker *Bridgeton* (401,382 tons) hit a mine shortly before 7 a.m. about 18 miles west of Farsi Island, on the very first Earnest Will convoy. None of the 26 aboard were hurt and it reached Kuwait under its own power; divers later found a 30-by-10-foot hole, and the Navy was "forced to hide its ships behind the wounded Bridgeton". This is the strongest counter-argument to escorts: one cheap mine beat three warships within days of the reflagging. Use it for the type of threat and response speed, and set it against the CIA warning of June 1987 (entry 8) and the European minesweeper decision 18 days later (entry 12).

     Reliability: Same-day wire report, widely confirmed, though blaming Iran was a Pentagon claim at the time.

12. **UPI (1987). Britain, France sending mine sweepers to Persian, Oman gulfs.** *UPI Archives*, 11 August 1987. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1987/08/11/Britain-France-sending-mine-sweepers-to-Persian-Oman-gulfs/5251555652800/) — **Grade B1** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

     Summary and analysis: After the *Bridgeton* mining, Britain announced four minesweepers and a support ship to reinforce its Armilla Patrol, and France joined. Italy said it could send ships quickly but needed parliamentary approval; Belgium, the Netherlands and Italy followed, coordinated loosely through the Western European Union rather than NATO (MERIP 1988; the CVCE calls the later Operation Cleansweep one of the WEU's first joint actions). This is the core evidence for European burden-sharing and response speed: about 18 days from mine strike to decision, with Royal Navy sweepers working in the Gulf by late September. Use it alongside UPI's 1989 verdict that the Europeans cleared "only a few dozen mines", which suggests presence and deterrence mattered more than mines removed.

     Reliability: Wire report of government announcements, not results; confirmed by later analysis.

13. **UPI (1988). President Reagan said downing of Iranian airliner was "understandable accident".** *UPI Archives*, 4 July 1988. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1988/07/04/President-Reagan-said-downing-of-Iranian-airliner-was-understandable-accident/3452583992000/) — **Grade B1** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

     Summary and analysis: Reports that USS *Vincennes* shot down Iran Air Flight 655 (Bandar Abbas to Dubai) on 3 July 1988, killing all 290 people aboard, after the crew mistook the Airbus A300 for an Iranian F-14; the Pentagon first claimed it had downed a fighter. Reagan called it an "understandable accident", while Iran called it premeditated and the UN Security Council met. Use it for severity (human cost) and as a counter-argument on information sharing: there was no common air picture between navies and civil aviation. It also matters for the end of the war, because Iran accepted Resolution 598 two weeks later, so ask whether fear of further US action, rather than cooperation, drove the ceasefire.

     Reliability: Wire report from the next day reflecting the US official line; the snippet wrongly said A320.

14. **UPI (1988). Iran accepts unconditional cease-fire.** *UPI Archives*, 18 July 1988. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1988/07/18/Iran-accepts-unconditional-cease-fire/6562585201600) — **Grade B1** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

     Summary and analysis: Reports that Secretary-General Pérez de Cuéllar received a letter from President Khamenei accepting Resolution 598, almost a year after it passed; Iran had earlier insisted that Iraq first be named the aggressor. The ceasefire took effect on 20 August 1988, and on 26 September the US ended formal convoys (Washington Post). This is the clearest evidence for the length of disruption (1984–88) and for the view that the Tanker War ended through Iran's own decision and UN diplomacy, not through escorts or minesweeping. Use it to weigh cooperation against alternative factors: Iran's battlefield losses, Praying Mantis (entry 7) and the Iran Air disaster (entry 13) all came in the months before acceptance.

     Reliability: Wire report from the time; the letter was dated 17 July and announced 18 July.

15. **Robert S. Strauss Center, University of Texas (n.d.). Strait of Hormuz – Tanker War.** Strauss Center for International Security and Law. [link](https://www.strausscenter.org/strait-of-hormuz-tanker-war/) — **Grade B2** · Type: Retrospective (university research summary)

     Summary and analysis: A neutral reference summary of the Tanker War. It says the conflict cut commercial shipping in the Gulf by about 25% and raised crude prices sharply early on, but that at its peak the campaign disrupted no more than about 2% of ships passing through the Gulf, and that under a quarter of the tankers hit were sunk or disabled. It is the main source here for the trade-fall measure and the low hit rate. Use it to show that severity was modest despite hundreds of attacks, then test the price claim against FRED Brent data (only $18.58 to $19.86 between May and July 1987) and *The National* (entry 16), which says flows were never "seriously disrupted".

     Reliability: Neutral university research summary, but undated, and its price claim is disputed by other sources.

16. ***The National* (2020). How the Iran-Iraq war shaped oil markets regionally and globally.** *The National* (Abu Dhabi, UAE). [link](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/how-the-iran-iraq-war-shaped-oil-markets-regionally-and-globally-1.1084279) — **Grade C2** · Type: Regional retrospective (Gulf press, 40th anniversary)

     Summary and analysis: A Gulf retrospective published for the war's 40th anniversary. Related search text says that at no point in the Tanker War was the flow of oil through Hormuz "seriously disrupted". This gives a regional view on the severity measures (oil flows and prices): the 1980s market had spare capacity, the 1986 price collapse and bypass pipelines, so attacks barely moved supply. Use it for the alternative-factors argument, that low severity in 1984–88 owed more to market conditions and to neither side trying to close the strait than to escorts or minesweepers. Contrast it with the Strauss Center's 25% fall in shipping (entry 15) and the USIP estimate of oil flows falling from over 7 to under 4 mb/d.

     Reliability: A UAE royal-owned paper giving a Gulf-state view, and its "never seriously disrupted" claim conflicts with some other sources.

17. ***The National* (2020). Iraq-Iran war: a legacy of carnage that shaped the Middle East for 40 years.** *The National* (Abu Dhabi, UAE). [link](https://www.thenationalnews.com/world/iraq-iran-war-a-legacy-of-carnage-that-shaped-the-middle-east-for-40-years-1.1081287) — **Grade C1** · Type: Regional retrospective (Gulf press, 40th anniversary)

     Summary and analysis: A regional look back at the whole war, including the attacks on shipping. Search text says more than 450 ships from 15 countries were attacked, about two-thirds of them by Iraq, and that Kuwait's request for help led to the US reflagging its tankers (Operation Earnest Will, 1987–88). The total matches the US Naval Institute's 451 (entry 3) and the Lloyd's count of 447 by December 1987 reported by UPI, so it corroborates the incident count. Use it to bring a Gulf perspective on why Kuwait sought outside protection, and to support the counter-argument that the belligerents' own choices drove likelihood: Iraq, which no convoy restrained, made most attacks, so escorts of Kuwaiti ships could not reduce the overall total much.

     Reliability: A UAE royal-owned paper, but its figures are consistent with other histories, including the US Naval Institute and Lloyd's counts.
