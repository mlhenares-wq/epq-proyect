# Top 80 Accessible Sources for the EPQ: Multilateral Cooperation and Chokepoint Disruption

Oct 20, 2026 · @Manuela López-Henares

## How the 80 were chosen

This list started as 100 sources, with the same number for each case study's historical disruption as for its present day. On 24 October 2026, 20 sources that cannot be read for free online were removed: paywalled journal articles and trade press (Elsevier and Wiley journals, Lloyd's List, Foreign Affairs, The Washington Post, Business Insurance), members-only magazine archives (US Naval Institute *Proceedings*), library-only books, a paid standard (ISO 31000), a catalogue record with no full text, and Singapore newspaper archives that open only inside the National Library. Every source left has a free link. The sections are no longer exactly balanced, and the sources are renumbered 1–80. 66 of the 80 are graded A or B.

**Rules used for every section:**

1. **Right period.** Historical sections hold only sources written during the period or mainly about it. Sources that compare both periods sit in the present-day section.
2. **A mix of types.** Each section combines academic work, official and primary documents, news reports, think-tank analysis and regional views. The historical sections include news written at the time (UPI, AP, Time, The Spectator) and government records (UN resolutions, US Navy reports, CIA and US State Department documents).
3. **Reliability first.** Grades A and B are preferred. C sources are kept only where they add something unique. The two D sources, Press TV (Iran) and Al-Ahram (Egypt), show only how a government framed events.
4. **Coverage.** Each section covers likelihood, severity, the five cooperation factors and the main counter-arguments for that period.

## Strait of Hormuz: the Tanker War, 1980–88 (1–17)

These 15 cover the attacks on shipping from 1984, war-risk insurance, the US reflagging and Earnest Will convoys, the European minesweepers, UN Resolutions 552 and 598, and the 1988 ceasefire. Six are news reports written at the time.

1. **Walker, G.K. (2000). The Tanker War, 1980–88: Law and Policy.** US Naval War College, *International Law Studies*, vol. 74. [link](https://digital-commons.usnwc.edu/ils/vol74/iss1/) — **Grade A2** · Type: Academic book (legal)

   Summary and analysis: A free, full-text legal study of the Tanker War. It covers the law of neutral shipping, the right of belligerents to attack merchant ships, the legality of reflagging Kuwaiti tankers under the US flag, convoy and escort rules, and the UN Security Council resolutions, including 598. It is the best source for the formal-frameworks and law-enforcement factors in the 1980s: it explains what the rules allowed, why reflagging gave the US a legal reason to protect ships, and why Resolution 552 (entry 6) had no enforcement mechanism. Use it to judge whether cooperation in 1987–88 was truly multilateral or a US–Kuwait deal wrapped in international law, and to explain why only US-flagged ships were protected while other neutral ships kept being hit.

   Reliability: Authoritative legal study, but published by a US Navy institution, so it takes a US legal viewpoint.
2. **Mobley, R.A. (2022). Deterrence Without Escalation: Fresh Insights into U.S. Decisionmaking During Operation Earnest Will.** *Joint Force Quarterly*, 106 (NDU Press). [link](https://ndupress.ndu.edu/Media/News/News-Article-View/Article/3106465/) — **Grade B1** · Type: Professional military journal (archive-based)

   Summary and analysis: Using declassified documents, Mobley shows that US planners assumed mines were not a serious threat and sent no minesweepers ahead of the first convoy, so the lead tanker, *Bridgeton*, was mined. It sets out the problems the US faced: reading Iranian decision-making, producing persuasive intelligence, signalling to Tehran, "achieving deterrence without unintentional escalation" and finding diplomatic off-ramps, and it notes that analysts and commanders disagreed about how Iran would react. Use it for three factors at once: information sharing (intelligence existed but was contested), response speed (minesweepers arrived only after the mining) and joint operations. It also supports the counter-argument that escort cooperation raised short-term risk before it reduced it.

   Reliability: US defence-university journal by a former US intelligence analyst, so it reflects a US view with no Iranian sources.
3. **UN Security Council (1987). Resolution 598 (1987).** UN Digital Library, 20 July 1987. [link](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/137345) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official / primary (UN resolution)

   Summary and analysis: The binding Iran–Iraq ceasefire resolution, adopted 15–0 at the 2750th meeting under Articles 39–40 of the UN Charter. It demands an immediate ceasefire and withdrawal to recognised borders, and it deplores attacks on neutral shipping. It passed just two days before the first Earnest Will convoy, so naval and diplomatic cooperation ran side by side. Iran accepted it only in July 1988, and the ceasefire began on 20 August 1988, after which attacks on shipping stopped. Use it as the main evidence for formal frameworks and great-power agreement, and to test the counter-argument: the year-long gap between adoption and acceptance suggests the resolution worked only once Iran's own military position collapsed (see entries 7 and 14).

   Reliability: Original UN legal text reflecting great-power agreement; it shows diplomacy helped end the Tanker War but not how much.
4. **UN Security Council (1984). Resolution 552 (1984).** UN Security Council, via Refworld, 1 June 1984. [link](https://www.refworld.org/node/80497) — **Grade A1** (graded here) · Type: Official / primary (UN resolution)

   Summary and analysis: Adopted 13–0–2 (Nicaragua and Zimbabwe abstaining) after a complaint of 21 May 1984 from Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the UAE. It condemned recent attacks on commercial ships, demanded that Iran stop attacking ships sailing to and from Kuwaiti and Saudi ports, and affirmed free navigation for shipping bound for non-belligerent states. The Secretary-General's report of December 1984 (S/16877) then recorded a "serious escalation" of attacks, with a list of incidents from June to December 1984. Use it as the first formal multilateral framework of the Tanker War and as evidence that it failed: attacks went on and peaked in 1987 (entry 3). It also targeted only Iran, although Iraq made most 1984 attacks.

   Reliability: Original UN legal text, confirmed by the UN Digital Library and the Secretary-General's own follow-up report, though it records what states demanded, not what happened.
5. **US Naval History and Heritage Command (2018). H-Gram 018: 30th Anniversary of Operation Praying Mantis.** NHHC, Director's Corner, April 2018. [link](https://history.navy.mil/about-us/leadership/director/directors-corner/h-grams/h-gram-018.html) — **Grade B1** (graded here) · Type: Official / primary (US Navy historical record)

   Summary and analysis: The US Navy's official history office explains the road to Praying Mantis: USS *Samuel B. Roberts* hit an Iranian mine on 14 April 1988, and four days later US forces destroyed two Iranian surveillance platforms, sank the frigate *Sahand* and spared the damaged *Sabalan* at the last moment. Iran lost over 60 dead and about 100 wounded in one day. The project data book also cites it for 116 merchant seafarers killed in 1984–87, matching entry 3. Use it for enforcement and response speed: a four-day retaliation that crippled Iran's navy three months before Tehran accepted Resolution 598. Pair it with entries 13 and 14 to ask whether coercion, not cooperation, ended the war.

   Reliability: Official US Navy history whose facts match UPI and Washington Post reports from the time, but written to commemorate the operation, so it presents US action favourably.
6. **Central Intelligence Agency (1987). Iran-Iraq: Lull in the Gulf Anti-Shipping War.** CIA Directorate of Intelligence memorandum NESA M 87-20063, 17 June 1987 (CIA Reading Room). [link](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/node/1976865) — **Grade B2** (graded here) · Type: Official / primary (declassified intelligence memo)

   Summary and analysis: A four-page assessment by the CIA's Iran/Iraq Branch, written five weeks before the first convoy. It judged that both Iran and Iraq saw short-term advantages in the lull in ship attacks, that Iran hoped to turn it into a permanent ceasefire in Gulf waters, and that renewed Iraqi attacks would anger the superpowers. Search summaries add that it expected Iran to resume strikes on unescorted ships, mine waters near Kuwait and possibly target US-flagged ships once escorts began. The *Bridgeton* mining (entry 11) bore this out. Use it for the information-sharing factor: the warning existed but did not produce minesweepers in time, which shows intelligence without a fast response does not cut likelihood.

   Reliability: An authentic declassified government document, but parts are redacted, its judgements are forecasts, and only catalogue entries and excerpts were seen.
7. **UPI (1984). Attack on Persian Gulf tanker "dangerous escalation".** *UPI Archives*, 15 May 1984. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1984/05/15/Attack-on-Persian-Gulf-tanker-dangerous-escalation/6583453441600/) — **Grade B2** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

   Summary and analysis: Covers Iran's first retaliatory strikes on Kuwaiti and Saudi tankers in May 1984. Its key figures are the war-risk premiums: Lloyd's had doubled seven-day cover for Kharg Island from 0.75% to 1.5% of hull value, and after the attack on a Kuwaiti tanker rates rose to 3% of ship value plus 2% of cargo value. The Strauss Center adds that Kharg hull rates reached 7.5% after the *Yanbu Pride* was hit that month. This is the best primary evidence for the insurance (likelihood) measure in the 1980s. Use it with Resolution 552 (entry 6), passed two weeks later, to show that the Gulf states turned to the UN as costs soared, and that premiums stayed high because the resolution changed little.

   Reliability: Wire report from the time; excellent insurance evidence, but check which May story gives each figure.
8. **UPI (1987). The Pentagon raised the toll today to 37 sailors.** *UPI Archives*, 19 May 1987. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1987/05/19/The-Pentagon-raised-the-toll-today-to-37-sailors/3451548395200/) — **Grade B1** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

   Summary and analysis: Reports that two Exocet missiles fired by an Iraqi Mirage F-1 on 17 May 1987 killed 37 sailors on the frigate USS *Stark*, and that the US accepted Saddam Hussein's apology. A related UPI story the same day says the ship's radar failed to pick up the threat, and it never fired back. This is a key counter-argument source: the deadliest attack on a US ship in the Tanker War came from Iraq, the side Washington was tilting towards, which shows the absence of deconfliction and shared air pictures between the US and the belligerents. Use it to argue that information sharing was the weakest cooperation factor in 1987, and that the *Stark* attack hardened US commitment to the Kuwaiti reflagging plan.

   Reliability: Wire report written at the time using Pentagon figures; the death toll is confirmed by many sources.
9. **UPI (1987). The supertanker Bridgeton hit an underwater mine Friday morning.** *UPI Archives*, 24 July 1987. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1987/07/24/The-supertanker-Bridgeton-hit-an-underwater-mine-Friday-morning/4397554097600/) — **Grade B1** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

   Summary and analysis: A same-day report that the reflagged supertanker *Bridgeton* (401,382 tons) hit a mine shortly before 7 a.m. about 18 miles west of Farsi Island, on the very first Earnest Will convoy. None of the 26 aboard were hurt and it reached Kuwait under its own power; divers later found a 30-by-10-foot hole, and the Navy was "forced to hide its ships behind the wounded Bridgeton". This is the strongest counter-argument to escorts: one cheap mine beat three warships within days of the reflagging. Use it for the type of threat and response speed, and set it against the CIA warning of June 1987 (entry 8) and the European minesweeper decision 18 days later (entry 12).

   Reliability: Same-day wire report, widely confirmed, though blaming Iran was a Pentagon claim at the time.
10. **UPI (1987). Britain, France sending mine sweepers to Persian, Oman gulfs.** *UPI Archives*, 11 August 1987. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1987/08/11/Britain-France-sending-mine-sweepers-to-Persian-Oman-gulfs/5251555652800/) — **Grade B1** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

    Summary and analysis: After the *Bridgeton* mining, Britain announced four minesweepers and a support ship to reinforce its Armilla Patrol, and France joined. Italy said it could send ships quickly but needed parliamentary approval; Belgium, the Netherlands and Italy followed, coordinated loosely through the Western European Union rather than NATO (MERIP 1988; the CVCE calls the later Operation Cleansweep one of the WEU's first joint actions). This is the core evidence for European burden-sharing and response speed: about 18 days from mine strike to decision, with Royal Navy sweepers working in the Gulf by late September. Use it alongside UPI's 1989 verdict that the Europeans cleared "only a few dozen mines", which suggests presence and deterrence mattered more than mines removed.

    Reliability: Wire report of government announcements, not results; confirmed by later analysis.
11. **UPI (1988). President Reagan said downing of Iranian airliner was "understandable accident".** *UPI Archives*, 4 July 1988. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1988/07/04/President-Reagan-said-downing-of-Iranian-airliner-was-understandable-accident/3452583992000/) — **Grade B1** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

    Summary and analysis: Reports that USS *Vincennes* shot down Iran Air Flight 655 (Bandar Abbas to Dubai) on 3 July 1988, killing all 290 people aboard, after the crew mistook the Airbus A300 for an Iranian F-14; the Pentagon first claimed it had downed a fighter. Reagan called it an "understandable accident", while Iran called it premeditated and the UN Security Council met. Use it for severity (human cost) and as a counter-argument on information sharing: there was no common air picture between navies and civil aviation. It also matters for the end of the war, because Iran accepted Resolution 598 two weeks later, so ask whether fear of further US action, rather than cooperation, drove the ceasefire.

    Reliability: Wire report from the next day reflecting the US official line; the snippet wrongly said A320.
12. **UPI (1988). Iran accepts unconditional cease-fire.** *UPI Archives*, 18 July 1988. [link](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1988/07/18/Iran-accepts-unconditional-cease-fire/6562585201600) — **Grade B1** · Type: News report (wire, at the time)

    Summary and analysis: Reports that Secretary-General Pérez de Cuéllar received a letter from President Khamenei accepting Resolution 598, almost a year after it passed; Iran had earlier insisted that Iraq first be named the aggressor. The ceasefire took effect on 20 August 1988, and on 26 September the US ended formal convoys (Washington Post). This is the clearest evidence for the length of disruption (1984–88) and for the view that the Tanker War ended through Iran's own decision and UN diplomacy, not through escorts or minesweeping. Use it to weigh cooperation against alternative factors: Iran's battlefield losses, Praying Mantis (entry 7) and the Iran Air disaster (entry 13) all came in the months before acceptance.

    Reliability: Wire report from the time; the letter was dated 17 July and announced 18 July.
13. **Robert S. Strauss Center, University of Texas (n.d.). Strait of Hormuz – Tanker War.** Strauss Center for International Security and Law. [link](https://www.strausscenter.org/strait-of-hormuz-tanker-war/) — **Grade B2** · Type: Retrospective (university research summary)

    Summary and analysis: A neutral reference summary of the Tanker War. It says the conflict cut commercial shipping in the Gulf by about 25% and raised crude prices sharply early on, but that at its peak the campaign disrupted no more than about 2% of ships passing through the Gulf, and that under a quarter of the tankers hit were sunk or disabled. It is the main source here for the trade-fall measure and the low hit rate. Use it to show that severity was modest despite hundreds of attacks, then test the price claim against FRED Brent data (only $18.58 to $19.86 between May and July 1987) and *The National* (entry 16), which says flows were never "seriously disrupted".

    Reliability: Neutral university research summary, but undated, and its price claim is disputed by other sources.
14. ***The National* (2020). How the Iran-Iraq war shaped oil markets regionally and globally.** *The National* (Abu Dhabi, UAE). [link](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/how-the-iran-iraq-war-shaped-oil-markets-regionally-and-globally-1.1084279) — **Grade C2** · Type: Regional retrospective (Gulf press, 40th anniversary)

    Summary and analysis: A Gulf retrospective published for the war's 40th anniversary. Related search text says that at no point in the Tanker War was the flow of oil through Hormuz "seriously disrupted". This gives a regional view on the severity measures (oil flows and prices): the 1980s market had spare capacity, the 1986 price collapse and bypass pipelines, so attacks barely moved supply. Use it for the alternative-factors argument, that low severity in 1984–88 owed more to market conditions and to neither side trying to close the strait than to escorts or minesweepers. Contrast it with the Strauss Center's 25% fall in shipping (entry 15) and the USIP estimate of oil flows falling from over 7 to under 4 mb/d.

    Reliability: A UAE royal-owned paper giving a Gulf-state view, and its "never seriously disrupted" claim conflicts with some other sources.
15. ***The National* (2020). Iraq-Iran war: a legacy of carnage that shaped the Middle East for 40 years.** *The National* (Abu Dhabi, UAE). [link](https://www.thenationalnews.com/world/iraq-iran-war-a-legacy-of-carnage-that-shaped-the-middle-east-for-40-years-1.1081287) — **Grade C1** · Type: Regional retrospective (Gulf press, 40th anniversary)

    Summary and analysis: A regional look back at the whole war, including the attacks on shipping. Search text says more than 450 ships from 15 countries were attacked, about two-thirds of them by Iraq, and that Kuwait's request for help led to the US reflagging its tankers (Operation Earnest Will, 1987–88). The total matches the US Naval Institute's 451 (entry 3) and the Lloyd's count of 447 by December 1987 reported by UPI, so it corroborates the incident count. Use it to bring a Gulf perspective on why Kuwait sought outside protection, and to support the counter-argument that the belligerents' own choices drove likelihood: Iraq, which no convoy restrained, made most attacks, so escorts of Kuwaiti ships could not reduce the overall total much.

    Reliability: A UAE royal-owned paper, but its figures are consistent with other histories, including the US Naval Institute and Lloyd's counts.

## Strait of Hormuz: the 2026 closure (18–34)

These 13 cover the closure from February 2026, the re-closures, the collapse in transits, the oil-price shock and the multilateral response. The Lloyd's List reports on record insurance and freight costs were removed because they are paywalled; those figures are cited in the judgement analysis. Press TV is included only as evidence of how Iran framed events.

16. **Kharroubi, E. (2026). Maritime chokepoints and the global economy: evidence from the Strait of Hormuz.** BIS Working Papers No. 1378. [link](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-1378-maritime-chokepoints-and-the-global-economy-evidence-strait-hormuz) — **Grade B2** · Type: Academic (central-bank working paper)

    Summary and analysis: Uses real-time ship-traffic data to measure unusual falls in chokepoint transits, separating supply-driven from demand-driven drops. It finds that Hormuz disruption raises energy and fertiliser prices, especially when supply-driven, then weakens global industrial production and raises consumer prices after about 4–6 months, with effects lasting up to a year, while corporate bond spreads widen. Of all the chokepoints studied, Hormuz gives the clearest signal of global supply stress. This is the most rigorous evidence for the price-rise and trade-reduction measures in 2026, and for severity beyond oil. Use it as context on why trade concentration matters and why a long closure with repeated re-closures does lasting damage. It corroborates OECD and World Bank forecasts but says nothing directly about cooperation.

    Reliability: A respected central-bank research body, but a working paper that has not been peer-reviewed and was seen only through search snippets.
17. **Talmadge, C. (2008). Closing Time: Assessing the Iranian Threat to the Strait of Hormuz.** *International Security* 33(1), pp. 82–117. [link](https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/closing-time-assessing-iranian-threat-strait-hormuz) — **Grade A2** · Type: Academic (peer-reviewed journal article; pre-war benchmark for 2026)

    Summary and analysis: Argues that Iran probably could not keep Hormuz fully closed for long, but that mines, missiles and small boats could disrupt shipping enough to drag the US into a long, costly campaign; Iran's stocks of these weapons were already larger than during the Tanker War. It is included here not as a 1980s source but as the pre-war benchmark that 2026 tested. Seven months of repeated closures partly confirmed it: Iran never sealed the strait completely, yet transits stayed far below normal and the IMO reported the central lanes unusable because of mines. Use it to frame the type-of-threat and technology factors, and as a counter-argument: if a determined state with cheap weapons can impose disruption, the ceiling on what cooperation can achieve is low.

    Reliability: Peer-reviewed article in a top security journal and free to read, though written in 2008 from a US point of view.
18. **Meza, A., Ari, I., Al Sada, M. and Koç, M. (2026). Implications of interrupting the Hormuz Strait in the LNG trade.** *Journal of Transportation Security*. [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12198-026-00350-1) — **Grade A2** · Type: Academic (peer-reviewed journal article)

    Summary and analysis: An agent-based simulation of the LNG market finds that a one-month Hormuz closure could be absorbed by drawing on Pacific suppliers, but a closure lasting six months or more leaves LNG demand unmet. That threshold matters for 2026, when the strait stayed largely shut for over seven months and Mitsui OSK's chairman called carrying LNG through it "almost impossible" in September. It is strong evidence that duration is the decisive severity measure, and it weakens the pipeline counter-argument for gas: LNG cannot be piped around the strait, so Qatar had no bypass at all. Use it alongside Kharroubi (item 18) to show severity growing with time, and against Bordoff and O'Sullivan (item 25), whose pipeline argument applies to crude only.

    Reliability: Peer-reviewed, but from a Qatari state-funded university writing about Qatar's own export route, and based on a model, not real 2026 data.
19. **International Energy Agency (2026). IEA Member countries to carry out largest ever oil stock release amid market disruptions from Middle East conflict.** IEA press release, 11 March. [link](https://www.iea.org/news/iea-member-countries-to-carry-out-largest-ever-oil-stock-release-amid-market-disruptions-from-middle-east-conflict/) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official / primary (intergovernmental)

    Summary and analysis: After an emergency meeting on 10 March, all 32 IEA members unanimously agreed to release 400 million barrels from emergency stocks, the largest coordinated action in the agency's history and only its sixth. The trigger was Hormuz exports falling below 10% of pre-conflict levels; the IEA's own April Oil Market Report later put loadings at about 3.8 mb/d against over 20 mb/d in February. This is the strongest 2026 evidence for formal frameworks and response speed (11 days after the first strikes), and a clear contrast with the Tanker War, when no collective release happened. Test it against the price record: Brent closed above $100 the next day (CNBC), and only about 330m barrels had been released by mid-October.

    Reliability: The official announcement from the body that took the decision, widely confirmed, though it is written to calm markets and does not show the effect.
20. **UN Security Council (2026). Resolution 2817.** Security Council Report, 11 March 2026. [link](https://www.securitycouncilreport.org/un-documents/document/s-res-2817.php) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official / primary (UN resolution)

    Summary and analysis: Adopted 13–0 with China and Russia abstaining, and drafted by Bahrain for the GCC plus Jordan, it condemns Iran's actions to close or obstruct Hormuz and affirms that impeding lawful transit passage is a serious threat to international peace and security. It is the main 2026 evidence for formal frameworks, and it passed within two weeks of the war starting, a fast response. Yet it authorised no force and did not reopen the strait, and a softer draft that only "encouraged" escorts was vetoed on 7 April (11–2–2). Use it as evidence that frameworks without enforcement did little to cut duration: Gulf states were still demanding its implementation in October. Contrast it with the unanimity behind Resolution 598 in 1987, and with Iran's rival "authority" (item 33).

    Reliability: Original UN text drafted by Bahrain for the Gulf states, who were parties to the crisis; it is declaratory and did not reopen the strait.
21. **Joint Maritime Information Center (2025–26). JMIC Advisory Notes and threat levels.** JMIC / UKMTO (via Seatrade). [link](https://www.seatrade-maritime.com/security/jmic-raises-strait-of-hormuz-threat-level-to-severe) — **Grade B2** · Type: Official / primary (military information centre)

    Summary and analysis: Advisory notes from the Joint Maritime Information Center, run by the Combined Maritime Forces with UKMTO. They rated the Hormuz threat "critical" in March 2026, "substantial" on 17 June after the US–Iran memorandum and "severe" on 7 July, which gives a ready-made likelihood timeline that tracks the re-closures. This is the main evidence for the information-sharing factor, the one area where cooperation clearly grew compared with 1987–88, when no shared air picture existed. Use it to show that cooperation improved knowledge of risk without reducing incidents: in mid-October gCaptain reported JMIC still rating the threat SEVERE, and finding no evidence for IRGC mine claims, while CENTCOM claimed control. That gap also bears on the US-role counter-argument.

    Reliability: Warnings from a US-led coalition body that is itself a party to the conflict, and its criteria are not published in detail.
22. **UN News (2026). Strait of Hormuz: UN evacuates 2,500 seafarers before attack freezes rescue operation.** UN News, June. [link](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/06/1167818) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official / primary (UN and IMO)

    Summary and analysis: Reports that an IMO-led evacuation got 115 ships and about 2,500 seafarers out of the Gulf before pausing after a container ship was attacked. By June at least 14 seafarers had been killed and more than 40 merchant ships attacked. This is the best official count for the 2026 incidents measure, since no cumulative total exists, and a concrete case of multilateral humanitarian cooperation that a single attack could halt. Use it to support the argument that cooperation reduced human costs but could not secure the route. Track the toll over time: the IMO estimated about 20,000 seafarers stranded in March, and its Secretary-General still put about 6,000 trapped in October, describing a "double blockade" by Iran and the US.

## Suez Canal: the 1967–75 closure (35–49)

These 12 cover why Egypt closed the canal, the eight years of lost trade and stranded ships, UN diplomacy, and the 1974 multinational clearance. Five are news reports written at the time; Al-Ahram is included only as evidence of how Egypt framed events.

29. **Time (1967). Middle East: The Quickest War.** *Time*, June. [link](https://time.com/archive/6890621/middle-east-the-quickest-war/) — **Grade B2** · Type: News report (contemporary magazine)

    Summary and analysis: Time's report on the Six-Day War of 5–10 June 1967 says the canal was "blocked at both ends by scuttled Egyptian ships". This is contemporary evidence for why the canal closed: the canal state decided to close it during a war. No non-state group was attacking passing ships. That matters for the type-of-threat factor. No naval escort could keep a canal open against the state that owned it, so once war began, disruption was close to certain. Use it with the CIA's August 1967 briefing note, which says the canal carried 237 million tons of cargo in 1966, about 15% of world seaborne trade, to show the scale of what was lost. Check which Time page holds the quotation before citing it.

    Reliability: Contemporary reporting by a leading US weekly, but the issue date is unconfirmed and the quotation may come from the companion essay "Running From Defeat".
30. **UN Security Council (1967). Resolution 242 (1967), S/RES/242.** United Nations, 22 November. [link](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/90717) — **Grade A1** (graded here) · Type: Official / primary (UN resolution)

    Summary and analysis: Adopted unanimously under Chapter VI five months after the war, Resolution 242 sets out principles for peace. These include Israeli withdrawal from occupied territories and the "necessity… for guaranteeing freedom of navigation through international waterways in the area". It is the core evidence for the formal-frameworks factor in 1967–75. The UN affirmed free navigation quickly, yet the canal stayed shut for another seven and a half years, because the resolution could not be enforced and the parties disagreed about what it meant. Resolution 338 (October 1973) only repeated the call to implement 242 while setting up the ceasefire that led to UNEF II. Use it to argue that a framework without enforcement or a political settlement did not shorten the disruption.

    Reliability: The original UN text, adopted unanimously, though its deliberately vague wording was the price of agreement and it has no enforcement power.
31. **Time (1969). World: The Suez Canal's Bleak Centennial.** *Time*, c. November. [link](https://time.com/archive/6635732/world-the-suez-canals-bleak-centennial/) — **Grade B2** · Type: News report (contemporary magazine)

    Summary and analysis: Written for the canal's hundredth anniversary in November 1969, this report finds that the only ships left in the canal were 15 vessels trapped since June 1967. Fourteen were bunched together in the Great Bitter Lake, kept afloat by skeleton crews rotated every three months, who passed the time with lifeboat races, football and films. This "Yellow Fleet" is the clearest human evidence for the duration measure. Thirty months in, there was still no sign of a reopening, or of any international effort to free the ships. Use it to show the severity of a total closure by a state, and contrast it with 2023–26, when ships could choose to divert. Ship counts vary between sources (14, 15 or 16).

    Reliability: A leading US weekly writing at the time, but the issue date is inferred from the "30 months" figure and has not been checked on the page.
32. **CEPII (2016). Trade costs and the Suez and Panama Canals, Working Paper 2016-29.** CEPII, Paris. [link](https://www.cepii.fr/PDF_PUB/wp/2016/wp2016-29.pdf) — **Grade B2** · Type: Academic (working paper)

    Summary and analysis: This CEPII working paper uses canal closures and openings, including the 1967–75 Suez closure, to estimate how sea distance affects trade. It finds a much smaller effect of distance on trade (an elasticity of about −0.15) than Feyrer (38). It is the main academic counterweight on severity. If distance matters less, the closure cost less trade than the headline figures suggest, because shippers absorbed the longer voyages round the Cape. Use it to show that the size of the 1967 trade shock is contested, and say so in the EPQ rather than quoting one figure. Together, 38 and 39 suggest that rerouting softened the closure, a point that matters when comparing it with the partial diversion of 2023–26.

    Reliability: A French government-funded research centre with strong trade economists, but this is an unrefereed working paper seen only through search summaries, so check for a later journal version.

    Summary and analysis: Written during the 2021 *Ever Given* blockage and carried by an Israeli paper, this AP explainer recalls the eight-year closure after 1967 and the "Yellow Fleet" stranded in the Great Bitter Lake. It also recalls Sadat reopening the canal in 1975 after talks with Israel. Trace the UN study (probably UNCTAD, 40) before citing the figures, and compare them with the $10 billion total that Time quoted in June 1975.

## Red Sea: the Houthi crisis, 2023–26 (50–64)

These 12 cover the attacks, the fall in Suez traffic and revenue, freight costs, and the naval coalitions. The Lloyd's List report that war-risk premiums roughly tripled after the January 2024 strikes was removed because it is paywalled; the figure is cited in the judgement analysis.

    Summary and analysis: The resolution condemns at least two dozen Houthi attacks since 19 November 2023, demands that they stop and that the *Galaxy Leader* and its crew be freed, and backs states' right to defend their ships. ## Theory, method and cross-case (91–100)

These 6 frame the essay, justify the risk equation and put all three straits on one scale.

75. **Marin-Ferrer, M., Vernaccini, L. and Poljansek, K. (2017). Index for Risk Management INFORM: Concept and Methodology, Version 2017.** EU Publications Office (EUR 28655 EN). [link](https://doi.org/10.2760/094023) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official methodology report

    Summary and analysis: This is the blueprint for the EPQ's equation. INFORM combines three dimensions, Hazard & Exposure, Vulnerability and Lack of Coping Capacity, by multiplying them and raising each to the power 1/3, a geometric mean. It keeps coping capacity separate because international actors can change it. The student's R = (L × S × K)^(1/3) copies this structure directly, with multilateral cooperation treated as institutional coping capacity (K = 5 − C/5). Cite it at the start of the methodology chapter to justify both the form of the equation and the decision to give cooperation its own term. Pair it with ISO 31000 for the likelihood × severity core, OECD/JRC (2008) for normalisation and sensitivity testing, and Cox (2008a) for the limits of scoring.

    Reliability: The official methodology of the EU Joint Research Centre, so it is authoritative for index construction, though it was designed for humanitarian and disaster risk rather than maritime security.
76. **Bueger, C. and Edmunds, T. (2017). Beyond seablindness: a new agenda for maritime security studies.** International Affairs 93(6). [link](https://academic.oup.com/ia/article/93/6/1293/4111108) — **Grade A1** · Type: Journal article (theory)

    Summary and analysis: Bueger and Edmunds argue that politicians and scholars long neglected the sea ("seablindness") and set out a research agenda drawn from counter-piracy, stressing information sharing, capacity-building and networked governance. This gives the literature review its core vocabulary and a theoretical reason to expect cooperation to lower risk, which is the hypothesis the EPQ tests. The student should use it to frame the five cooperation factors (frameworks, joint operations, information sharing, response speed, enforcement) as recognised mechanisms, then test them against the three cases. Its optimism should be weighed against Shortland and Vothknecht (2011), who find displacement, and refined with Bueger and Edmunds (2024), whose threat-type split explains why the agenda fits Malacca piracy better than Iran or the Houthis.

    Reliability: Copenhagen and Bristol academics in a top, highly cited international relations journal, but it is theory rather than data and was written before 2023.
77. **Verschuur, J., Lumma, J. and Hall, J.W. (2025). Systemic impacts of disruptions at maritime chokepoints.** Nature Communications 16. [link](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-65403-w) — **Grade A1** · Type: Journal article (quantitative)

    Summary and analysis: This model estimates that about US$192 billion of trade a year is at risk at 24 chokepoints, with roughly US$10.7 billion a year in losses plus US$3.4 billion in extra freight, counting delays, rerouting, insurance and freight costs. It is the only peer-reviewed source that puts Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb/Suez and Malacca on one scale, so it suits the introduction and the cross-case comparison. It also justifies a key method choice: because it treats freight costs as losses, the student moved freight rates from L to S. Use it alongside EIA (exposure) and UNCTAD (2024) (observed Red Sea losses), and stress that its figures are expected values, not measured outcomes.

    Reliability: An Oxford and TU Delft team in a top open-access journal, confirmed by several sources, though the US$192 billion is a modelled expected value produced before the 2026 Hormuz closure.
78. **UNCTAD (2024). Review of Maritime Transport 2024: Navigating Maritime Chokepoints.** United Nations. [link](https://unctad.org/publication/review-maritime-transport-2024) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official UN report

    Summary and analysis: The whole 2024 edition is about chokepoints. By mid-2024, tonnage crossing the Gulf of Aden was down 76% and through Suez down 70%, while Cape of Good Hope arrivals rose 89%; small island states and the least developed countries suffered most from higher freight costs. These are ready-made figures for the trade-reduction indicator in S for the Suez/Red Sea case, and they show rerouting as a physical alternative to cooperation. Use it with PortWatch-based estimates and Verschuur et al. (2025) to cross-check severity, and with EIA for Hormuz and Malacca exposure. Note its developing-country lens when comparing who bears the costs.

    Reliability: An official, free UN report whose figures match IMF and Clarksons data, though its mandate leads it to stress costs to poorer states and its mid-2024 data miss 2025–26.
79. **United Nations (1982). UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), Part III, Articles 34–45 (transit passage).** UN Division for Ocean Affairs. [link](https://www.un.org/Depts/los/convention_agreements/texts/unclos/part3.htm) — **Grade A1** · Type: Treaty (primary legal text)

    Summary and analysis: Part III sets the legal regime for international straits, including the right of transit passage that coastal states may not suspend, and Article 43, which says user and coastal states "should by agreement co-operate" on navigation and safety. It is the shared legal baseline for all three cases and scores the "formal frameworks" factor in C. Use it to contrast cases: Malacca's coastal states operate within it, while Iran signed but never ratified it and in 2026 asserted its own strait authority and tolls. Article 43 is directly on the EPQ's theme, but pair it with evidence of compliance, since law on paper is not enforcement.

    Reliability: The primary treaty text, though Iran signed but never ratified it and a legal text does not prove compliance.
80. **EIA (2024, updated 2026). World Oil Transit Chokepoints.** US Energy Information Administration. [link](https://www.eia.gov/international/analysis/special-topics/World_Oil_Transit_Chokepoints) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official data report

    Summary and analysis: The standard reference for oil volumes through each chokepoint. Malacca carries the most oil (about 23.7 million barrels a day in 2023), Hormuz about 21 million, and Hormuz has very limited bypass pipelines while Malacca is the easiest to route around. This gives the EPQ its measure of structural exposure and physical alternatives, which the methodology holds fixed within each strait but must discuss when comparing between straits. Use it in the case introductions and as a possible optional exposure score in S. Combine it with Verschuur et al. (2025) for trade value and UNCTAD (2024) for observed Red Sea disruption.

    Reliability: Official, free US government data, though it relies on a commercial tanker-tracking firm and its core figures are 2023 data from before the 2026 war.
