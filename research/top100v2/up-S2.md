## Red Sea: the Houthi crisis, 2023–26 (50–64)

These 15 cover the attacks, the fall in Suez traffic and revenue, freight and insurance costs, and the naval coalitions. They include the strongest counter-argument: war-risk premiums roughly tripled after the January 2024 strikes.

50. **UN Security Council (2024). Resolution 2722 (2024), S/RES/2722.** United Nations, 10 January. [link](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4033392/files/S_RES_2722_%282024%29-EN.pdf) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official / primary (UN resolution)

     Summary and analysis: The resolution condemns at least two dozen Houthi attacks since 19 November 2023, demands that they stop and that the *Galaxy Leader* and its crew be freed, and backs states' right to defend their ships. It passed 11–0 with four abstentions (Russia, China, Algeria, Mozambique), and Russian amendments linking the attacks to Gaza were rejected. Use it as the core evidence for the formal-frameworks factor and for response speed (seven weeks after the first attack), and note that the US–UK strikes followed within two days. Compare it with Resolution 242 (1967), which affirmed free navigation but was followed by eight years of closure: both lacked enforcement, and ACLED (58) and Tallis (59) show that attacks and diversions carried on.

     Reliability: The original UN text, but its wording reflects great-power bargaining, it legitimises the sponsors' own response and it has no enforcement power.

51. **Council of the EU (2024–26). Decisions (CFSP) 2024/583, 2024/632, 2025/334 and 2026/439 on EUNAVFOR ASPIDES.** *EUR-Lex*. [link](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dec/2024/583/oj/eng) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official / primary (EU legal acts)

     Summary and analysis: These acts establish (8 February 2024) and launch (19 February 2024) Aspides as a strictly defensive EU mission, extend it to 28 February 2027 with about €15 million in common costs (down from about €17 million), and in 2025 add tasks to gather information on arms trafficking and shadow fleets for sharing with Interpol, Europol, UNODC and the IMO. They are primary evidence for three cooperation factors (formal frameworks, joint operations and information sharing) and for response speed: launch came about three months after the first attack. Contrast the defensive mandate with the US-led Prosperity Guardian and strikes (59, 60), and use ECFR (57) to show that ships and money did not match the task. The texts say nothing about effectiveness.

     Reliability: Original EU legal acts that show what Aspides may do, not whether it worked, and the budget figure needs checking in the text.

52. **UNCTAD (2024). Navigating Troubled Waters: Impact to Global Trade of Disruption of Shipping Routes in the Red Sea, Black Sea and Panama Canal.** UNCTAD Rapid Assessment, 22 February. [link](https://unctad.org/publication/navigating-troubled-waters-impact-global-trade-disruption-shipping-routes-red-sea-black) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official / data (UN agency)

     Summary and analysis: UNCTAD's rapid assessment uses its own data to show Suez Canal transits down 42% from their peak and weekly container-ship transits down 67% by early 2024, and it separates the Panama drought from the effects of the attacks. It is the headline official figure for the trade-reduction measure and shows that the 2023–26 disruption was partial, not the total stoppage of 1967–75. Use it with IMF PortWatch (53), which independently confirms the scale, and with Yap and Yang (54) for the year-long fall. Note the gap between container falls (−67%, later −82% to −86%) and much smaller tanker falls, which supports the type-of-threat and cargo-mix explanations more than cooperation. Check the often-quoted 82% figure in the PDF.

     Reliability: A UN body publishing its own data, though it is a short early-2024 snapshot, not an annual figure, and it is not peer-reviewed.

53. **IMF (2024). Red Sea attacks disrupt global trade.** *IMF Blog* (Chart of the Week), 7 March. [link](https://www.imf.org/Blogs/Articles/2024/03/07/Red-Sea-Attacks-Disrupt-Global-Trade) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official / data (IMF PortWatch)

     Summary and analysis: Using PortWatch ship-tracking data, IMF staff show Suez Canal trade volume down about 50% year on year in January–February 2024, while traffic round the Cape of Good Hope rose about 74%, adding more than ten days to deliveries. It is evidence for the trade-reduction measure and, above all, for the Cape-route alternative factor: trade was diverted rather than lost, unlike the total stoppage of 1967–75. It independently confirms UNCTAD (52), and the PortWatch dataset lets the student draw their own transit graph for the whole crisis. Compare the extra ten days with Feyrer's (2021) finding, for the 1967–75 closure, that longer distances cut trade only moderately, which helps explain why severity stayed contained.

     Reliability: IMF staff using original data, confirmed by UNCTAD, but a short blog covering two months only, and the authors still need adding.

54. **Yap, W.Y. and Yang, D. (2024). Geopolitical tension and shipping network disruption: analysis of the Red Sea crisis on container port calls.** *Journal of Transport Geography* 121. [link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtrangeo.2024.104004) — **Grade A2** · Type: Peer-reviewed journal article

     Summary and analysis: Using ship-tracking (AIS) data, the authors show that container carriers switched to the Cape and added ships to keep port calls steady, while average daily Suez transits fell from about 75 to about 30 between December 2023 and December 2024. It is evidence for the trade-reduction measure (a 60% fall) and, more importantly, for the supply-chain factor: industry adapted without state help, and kept doing so after Prosperity Guardian and Aspides were at sea. Use it to argue that carrier behaviour, not navies, limited severity, which matches Bedoya-Maya et al. (55). Its transit fall agrees with UNCTAD (52) and with Suez Canal Authority figures showing annual transits roughly halving, from about 26,400 in 2023 to about 13,200 in 2024.

     Reliability: Hong Kong PolyU researchers in a strong journal, but it covers year one only and the 75→30 transit figure comes from a snippet.

55. **Bedoya-Maya, F. et al. (2025). Container port competitiveness amid disruptions: insights from the European maritime network during the Red Sea crisis.** *Journal of Transport Geography* 128. [link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtrangeo.2025.104304) — **Grade A2** · Type: Peer-reviewed journal article

     Summary and analysis: Modelling the European port network in the first half of 2024, this Antwerp team finds that Red Sea diversions raised transport-chain costs into Europe by only 3–13%, depending on the port: hubs near Gibraltar coped best, while feeder ports near Suez lost connections. It is evidence for the cost side of severity and for the supply-chain factor: the network reorganised around the Cape route instead of collapsing. Use it to qualify spot-rate spikes such as Freightos' $8,369 peak (61), since headline rates overstate the cost to whole supply chains. It agrees with Yap and Yang (54), and helps explain why the ECB's March 2024 staff projections put the euro-area inflation effect at only about 0.25 percentage points.

     Reliability: A strong University of Antwerp port-economics team with a free PDF and a confirmed abstract, but it covers only the first half of 2024 and is Europe-focused.

56. **Arellanes Arellanes, J. (2025). Los hutíes en el entramado geopolítico de Medio Oriente: la Crisis del Mar Rojo como disrupción de la seguridad marítima internacional.** *Revista Mexicana de Ciencias Políticas y Sociales* 70(255). [link](https://www.revistas.unam.mx/index.php/rmcpys/article/view/93957) — **Grade A2** · Type: Peer-reviewed journal article

     Summary and analysis: This UNAM article treats the Houthis as a "hybrid actor" rather than a simple Iranian proxy, drawing on more than 18,000 ACLED events from 2017 to 2025. It argues that Yemen's collapse, cheap military technology and the group's position on Bab el-Mandeb let a local actor disrupt international maritime security. It is the best peer-reviewed evidence for two alternative factors, type of threat and technology, and helps explain why naval coalitions struggled to deter the group (59). Use it to contrast the 1967 threat (a state closing the canal with scuttled ships and mines) with the selective attacks of 2023–26, and to put the Houthis' own framing of the campaign in context (64). It also adds a Latin American scholarly view.

     Reliability: A peer-reviewed UNAM journal indexed in SciELO, but seen in search summaries only.

57. **ECFR (2026). The strait and narrow: Yemen, the Red Sea and Europe's path forward.** European Council on Foreign Relations. [link](https://ecfr.eu/article/the-strait-and-narrow-yemen-the-red-sea-and-europes-path-forward/) — **Grade B2** · Type: Think tank analysis

     Summary and analysis: ECFR argues that Europe should quickly add resources to Aspides and Atalanta, deepen Aspides' cooperation with the Saudi-led Multinational Maritime Defence Alliance, and add minesweeping and counter-drone cover; a related ECFR brief calls Aspides, Atalanta and EMASoH "fragmented and under-resourced". It is direct evidence for evaluating the joint-operations factor: the EU mission had a legal mandate (51) but not the ships to match it. Use it with ANSA's September 2026 report that Kaja Kallas said more than ten ships were needed, and with CIMSEC's estimate of about four escorted transits a day against the 8–10 that industry wanted. Remember its advocacy purpose when weighing the claim.

     Reliability: A pro-EU think tank funded by foundations and governments; it is advocacy, so it has a reason to stress under-resourcing.

58. **ACLED (2025). Regional power struggles fuel simmering tensions across Red Sea.** ACLED, December. [link](https://acleddata.com/report/regional-power-struggles-fuel-simmering-tensions-across-red-sea) — **Grade B2** · Type: Think tank / conflict-data report

     Summary and analysis: ACLED's year-end review, built on its transparent conflict-event dataset, finds 84% fewer Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping in 2025 than in 2024. It is the best time-series evidence for the incidents measure and lets the student test timing: the fall followed the January 2025 Gaza ceasefire and the Oman-brokered US–Houthi truce of May 2025, a bilateral deal, not a multilateral step. That truce excluded Israel-linked shipping, and the *Magic Seas* and *Eternity C* were sunk in July 2025. Use it for the "Gaza as on/off switch" counter-argument, and with BIMCO data showing Suez transits still about 60% down 100 days after the last attack. Do not mix ACLED "events" with counts of ships attacked.

     Reliability: A widely used conflict-event dataset with a transparent method, partly government-funded, though coding rules shape the counts and this figure was not cross-checked.

59. **Tallis, J. (2025). How the Biden Administration Won Tactically but Failed Strategically in the Red Sea.** *War on the Rocks*, 2 April. [link](https://warontherocks.com/2025/04/how-the-biden-administration-won-tactically-but-failed-strategically-in-the-red-sea/) — **Grade C2** · Type: Think tank / policy analysis

     Summary and analysis: Tallis, a US naval analyst, argues that the US Navy fought "nearly flawless" engagements against Houthi missiles and drones, but that judged against the real aim of restoring commercial traffic, Operation Prosperity Guardian failed: few partners joined, and shipping lines made their own cost calculations and kept using the Cape. It is included despite its C grade because no other candidate judges the coalition after the event against traffic outcomes, giving the clearest version of the "won the battles, lost the route" counter-argument. Use it for the joint-operations and enforcement factors, and to separate severity (lives and ships saved) from likelihood. It matches the IISS-based Forces News report and ECFR (57), but treat it as argument, not data.

     Reliability: A US naval analyst writing to persuade policy readers, so it is an argument, not data.

60. **Lloyd's List (2024). Red Sea war risk rates see huge jump in wake of Yemen airstrikes.** *Lloyd's List*, c. 16 January. [link](https://www.lloydslist.com/LL1147965/Red-Sea-war-risk-rates-see-huge-jump-in-wake-of-Yemen-airstrikes) — **Grade B2** · Type: Trade press

     Summary and analysis: Lloyd's List reports that war-risk cover for Red Sea transits roughly tripled within a week of the US–UK strikes of 11–12 January 2024 on about 60 Houthi targets, from about 0.3% of hull value to 0.75% and then 1%, which it puts at about 100 times the pre-November 2023 level. It is the key evidence for the war-risk premium measure and for the escalation counter-argument: forceful enforcement raised the risk that insurers priced, at least in the short term. Set it against Reuters' March 2024 report that rates held near 1% even after the *Rubymar* became the first ship sunk. Use it to show that likelihood, as judged by the insurance market, did not fall when cooperation became more coercive.

     Reliability: The leading shipping paper, but the figures are broker quotes and the "100 times" comparison depends on the baseline chosen.

61. **Freightos (2024). FBX Index July 2024: market summary.** *Baltic Exchange* (guest column), August. [link](https://www.balticexchange.com/en/news-and-events/news/guest-column/2024/fbx-index-july-24-market-summary.html) — **Grade B2** · Type: Trade press / data (index owner)

     Summary and analysis: The owner of the FBX index reports that Asia–North Europe container rates peaked at $8,369 per 40-ft box in July 2024 (+17% in a month), with Asia–Mediterranean at $7,796, driven by Red Sea diversions, congestion at transhipment hubs and shippers moving peak-season cargo early. By October 2024 rates were about 60% below the peak, at $3,489. It is the clearest evidence for the freight-rate measure and shows the spike faded while diversions went on, which points to supply-chain adjustment rather than cooperation. Compare it with Drewry's index, up 61% in two weeks to 4 January 2024, and note that no comparable index exists for 1967–75, when tanker rates reportedly "doubled".

     Reliability: The company that owns the index commenting on its own product, with a commercial interest in volatility, and the date is unconfirmed.

62. **Lloyd's List Intelligence (2026). Red Sea Brief: 6 August 2026.** *Lloyd's List Intelligence* blog. [link](https://www.lloydslistintelligence.com/resources/blog/red-sea-brief-6-august-2026) — **Grade B2** · Type: Trade press / data brief

     Summary and analysis: LLI tracks transits after the Houthis' 20 July 2026 blockade on Saudi-linked shipping: Bab el-Mandeb traffic fell 24% and then levelled off, about 275 ships transited Suez in the week to 2 August, very large crude carriers' transits quadrupled as some lightened their loads, and some Saudi crude moved through Egypt's SUMED pipeline. It is the best 2026 traffic source for the trade-reduction measure and the only candidate showing SUMED, a physical alternative that did not exist in 1967–75 (it opened in 1977). It also shows the crisis re-escalating, linked to the Hormuz war, while Aspides was still at sea. Note Infobae's conflicting −34%; either way, the 2026 fall was smaller and more selective than in 2024.

     Reliability: The leading shipping-data firm, though this blog also markets its services, and its −24% differs from Infobae's −34%.

63. **Reuters (2024). Egypt's Sisi says regional challenges cost Egypt around $7 bln of Suez Canal revenues in 2024.** *CNBC Africa*, December. [link](https://cnbcafrica.com/wire/655389) — **Grade B2** · Type: Regional perspective (Egypt, via wire)

     Summary and analysis: Reuters reports President Sisi's claim that Red Sea disruption cost Egypt about $7 billion in canal revenue in 2024, a fall of more than 60% on 2023. It gives the canal state's view of severity, which matters because Egypt did not join Prosperity Guardian, and in October 2026 it joined Sudan, Eritrea and Somalia in declaring Red Sea security the "exclusive responsibility of the littoral states". Compare the claim with the IMF's estimate of about $6 billion and with Suez Canal Authority figures (about $4 billion in 2024 against $10.2 billion in 2023), noting that calendar-year and fiscal-year figures differ. Set it against the UN estimate of $250 million a year in lost tolls in 1967–75.

     Reliability: Reuters reporting a presidential claim that differs from the IMF's estimate of about $6 billion, and the statement did not name the Houthis.

64. **Reuters (2023). Israel's Eilat Port has 85% drop in activity amid Red Sea Houthi attacks.** *Jerusalem Post* (Israel), 21 December. [link](https://www.jpost.com/middle-east/article-779002) — **Grade B1** · Type: Regional perspective (Israel, via wire)

     Summary and analysis: Eilat's chief executive told Reuters that activity had fallen 85% since the Houthis began attacking ships; the port mainly handles car imports and potash exports. It is the most-cited trade-reduction figure for Israel and shows how selective the threat was: the port the Houthis openly targeted suffered near-total loss, while Suez traffic fell by about half (52, 53). Later Israeli reports (16 ship calls in 2024 against 132 in 2023) confirm that the collapse lasted despite Prosperity Guardian and Aspides. Read it beside the Houthi agency Saba's November 2023 statement, cited only as framing, which called all Israel-linked ships "legitimate targets" in support of Gaza. Treat the CEO's figure as an upper estimate.

     Reliability: Reuters copy in a free Israeli paper, but the CEO was lobbying for state aid, so treat it as an upper estimate.
