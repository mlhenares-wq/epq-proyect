     Reliability: Scholarly and written during the events, so it ends before ReCAAP and Eyes in the Sky took effect, and the summary comes from search results only.

     Summary and analysis: This article explains why the US-backed Regional Maritime Security Initiative of 2004 failed: Indonesia and Malaysia saw outside patrols as a threat to their sovereignty and did not rank piracy as a priority. It calls the RMSI a pioneering failure whose lessons ReCAAP applied, and argues that ReCAAP worked better because it was inclusive and built on information sharing rather than joint patrols, even though Malaysia and Indonesia never signed it. It is the key academic source on formal frameworks and on why Malacca cooperation became "coordinated, not joint". Use it to frame the 2004 sovereignty row reported by the Straits Times (76) and the Indonesian position (77), then test its optimism against the timing problem in the ReCAAP text (70).

     Reliability: A peer-reviewed journal with the abstract confirmed, but the authors are unknown, it is paywalled and it may favour the ReCAAP model.

68. **[Author to check] (2026). Complexities of governance and sovereignty in maritime security. Vol. 187 (May 2026).** *Marine Policy*. [link](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0308597X26000369) — **Grade A3** · Type: Peer-reviewed journal article

     Summary and analysis: This recent article gives the sharpest critique of the 2005 Eyes in the Sky air patrols. 70. **ReCAAP member states (2004). Regional Cooperation Agreement on Combating Piracy and Armed Robbery against Ships in Asia.** NUS Centre for International Law treaty database.      Summary and analysis: Adopted in Tokyo on 11 November 2004 and in force on 4 September 2006, this is Asia's first government-to-government anti-piracy agreement. It set up the Information Sharing Centre in Singapore, launched in November 2006. The text shows that ReCAAP's core is information sharing and capacity building, not enforcement or joint patrols, so it is the primary source for the formal-frameworks and information-sharing factors. Use it to expose the timing problem: ReCAAP came into force after Malacca attacks had already fallen to 11–12 a year (69) and a month after the war-risk delisting (72), so it cannot explain the fall. It also shows the key gap: Malaysia and Indonesia are not parties. Read it with Marine Policy (67), which explains why its inclusiveness mattered.

     Reliability: A primary legal text, but its main limit is that Malaysia and Indonesia, the two biggest coastal states, are not parties.

71. **Ministry of Defence, Singapore (2005). Joint news release: launch of Eyes in the Sky (EiS) initiative.** National Archives of Singapore. [link](https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/data/pdfdoc/20050913984.htm) — **Grade B1** · Type: Official (four-state government release)

     Summary and analysis: This joint release by Malaysia, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand records the launch of Eyes in the Sky on 13 September 2005. Each state flies up to two maritime air patrols a week, with an officer from each country on board, under a clause protecting each state's sovereignty. It is the founding document for the joint-operations and information-sharing factors late in the peak. It also dates the response: more than five years after the 2000 surge (69) and about three months after the Lloyd's war-risk listing of June 2005, which suggests market pressure sped it up. Use it for what governments committed to, not for results. Marine Policy (68) questions whether so few daytime flights could matter, while Business Insurance (75) shows insurers took them seriously.

     Reliability: An official four-state release, solid on what governments did but silent on whether it worked.

72. **inforMARE (2006). Lloyd's Joint War Committee removes the Malacca Strait from its listed areas.** inforMARE, backed by London P&I Club circular 7083.      Summary and analysis: This Italian shipping report, backed by London P&I Club circular 7083, records that Lloyd's Joint War Committee removed the Malacca Strait from its war-risk listed areas from 00:01 BST on 7 August 2006, about 14 months after the June 2005 listing. Ports in north-east Sumatra stayed listed. It is the clearest dated evidence that a measured cost fell: war-risk premiums, reported elsewhere at about 0.4% of hull value or 1% of cargo value. Use it to fix when the premium ended, and pair it with Business Insurance (75) for why insurers acted.      Reliability: Italian shipping press backed by a London P&I Club circular, but the delisting is insurers' judgement, not proof that the patrols caused it.
