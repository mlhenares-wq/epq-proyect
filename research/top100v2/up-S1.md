## Suez Canal: the 1967–75 closure (35–49)

These 15 cover why Egypt closed the canal, the eight years of lost trade and stranded ships, UN diplomacy, and the 1974 multinational clearance. Five are news reports written at the time; Al-Ahram is included only as evidence of how Egypt framed events.

35. **Time (1967). Middle East: The Quickest War.** *Time*, June. [link](https://time.com/archive/6890621/middle-east-the-quickest-war/) — **Grade B2** · Type: News report (contemporary magazine)

     Summary and analysis: Time's report on the Six-Day War of 5–10 June 1967 says the canal was "blocked at both ends by scuttled Egyptian ships". This is contemporary evidence for why the canal closed: the canal state decided to close it during a war. No non-state group was attacking passing ships. That matters for the type-of-threat factor. No naval escort could keep a canal open against the state that owned it, so once war began, disruption was close to certain. Use it with the CIA's August 1967 briefing note, which says the canal carried 237 million tons of cargo in 1966, about 15% of world seaborne trade, to show the scale of what was lost. Check which Time page holds the quotation before citing it.

     Reliability: Contemporary reporting by a leading US weekly, but the issue date is unconfirmed and the quotation may come from the companion essay "Running From Defeat".

36. **UN Security Council (1967). Resolution 242 (1967), S/RES/242.** United Nations, 22 November. [link](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/90717) — **Grade A1** (graded here) · Type: Official / primary (UN resolution)

     Summary and analysis: Adopted unanimously under Chapter VI five months after the war, Resolution 242 sets out principles for peace. These include Israeli withdrawal from occupied territories and the "necessity… for guaranteeing freedom of navigation through international waterways in the area". It is the core evidence for the formal-frameworks factor in 1967–75. The UN affirmed free navigation quickly, yet the canal stayed shut for another seven and a half years, because the resolution could not be enforced and the parties disagreed about what it meant. Resolution 338 (October 1973) only repeated the call to implement 242 while setting up the ceasefire that led to UNEF II. Use it to argue that a framework without enforcement or a political settlement did not shorten the disruption.

     Reliability: The original UN text, adopted unanimously, though its deliberately vague wording was the price of agreement and it has no enforcement power.

37. **Time (1969). World: The Suez Canal's Bleak Centennial.** *Time*, c. November. [link](https://time.com/archive/6635732/world-the-suez-canals-bleak-centennial/) — **Grade B2** · Type: News report (contemporary magazine)

     Summary and analysis: Written for the canal's hundredth anniversary in November 1969, this report finds that the only ships left in the canal were 15 vessels trapped since June 1967. Fourteen were bunched together in the Great Bitter Lake, kept afloat by skeleton crews rotated every three months, who passed the time with lifeboat races, football and films. This "Yellow Fleet" is the clearest human evidence for the duration measure. Thirty months in, there was still no sign of a reopening, or of any international effort to free the ships. Use it to show the severity of a total closure by a state, and contrast it with 2023–26, when ships could choose to divert. Ship counts vary between sources (14, 15 or 16).

     Reliability: A leading US weekly writing at the time, but the issue date is inferred from the "30 months" figure and has not been checked on the page.

38. **Feyrer, J. (2021). Distance, trade, and income – The 1967 to 1975 closing of the Suez Canal as a natural experiment.** *Journal of Development Economics* 153, 102708. [link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2021.102708) — **Grade A2** · Type: Academic (peer-reviewed journal article)

     Summary and analysis: Feyrer treats the eight-year closure as a natural experiment, because it suddenly lengthened sea routes for some pairs of countries, such as Europe and Asia, but not for others. He finds that distance cuts trade only about half as much as standard estimates suggest. His VoxEU summary says a 10% fall in sea distance goes with about 5% more trade. He also finds that each extra dollar of trade adds about 25 cents of income, mainly through trade in goods. It is the strongest quantitative evidence for the trade-reduction measure in 1967–75. It shows that the closure did real damage, but that ships rerouting round the Cape softened it. A free version exists as NBER Working Paper 15557. Contrast its estimate with CEPII's (39).

     Reliability: A peer-reviewed study by a Dartmouth economist in a leading field journal, but only the abstract and summary were seen and the size of the effect is contested.

39. **CEPII (2016). Trade costs and the Suez and Panama Canals, Working Paper 2016-29.** CEPII, Paris. [link](https://www.cepii.fr/PDF_PUB/wp/2016/wp2016-29.pdf) — **Grade B2** · Type: Academic (working paper)

     Summary and analysis: This CEPII working paper uses canal closures and openings, including the 1967–75 Suez closure, to estimate how sea distance affects trade. It finds a much smaller effect of distance on trade (an elasticity of about −0.15) than Feyrer (38). It is the main academic counterweight on severity. If distance matters less, the closure cost less trade than the headline figures suggest, because shippers absorbed the longer voyages round the Cape. Use it to show that the size of the 1967 trade shock is contested, and say so in the EPQ rather than quoting one figure. Together, 38 and 39 suggest that rerouting softened the closure, a point that matters when comparing it with the partial diversion of 2023–26.

     Reliability: A French government-funded research centre with strong trade economists, but this is an unrefereed working paper seen only through search summaries, so check for a later journal version.

40. **UNCTAD Secretariat (1973). Economic Effects of the Closure of the Suez Canal (TD/B/C.4/104).** UNCTAD, Geneva (TRID record). [link](https://trid.trb.org/View/15787) — **Grade A3** (graded here) · Type: Academic / research study (UN agency)

     Summary and analysis: Published while the canal was still shut, this 77-page study with 15 statistical tables assesses the effects of the closure on tankers, liner trades and dry bulk cargoes. It also covers the extra costs of running ships, and the consequences for ports and for international trade in general. It is the only official attempt made at the time to measure severity across the whole shipping system, and the UN cost estimates later quoted by Time and AP (41) probably came from it. Its figures were not visible in the search record. B. J. Abrahamson's 1973 critical review argued that they were exaggerated and offered lower estimates, so treat it as the upper end of a contested range, alongside Feyrer (38) and CEPII (39).

     Reliability: An official UN study whose mandate stresses costs to developing countries, but only the bibliographic abstract was seen and its figures were challenged at the time, so credibility stays at 3.

41. **Associated Press (2021). Not only grounded ships: A look at past crises that closed Suez.** *Times of Israel*, March. [link](https://www.timesofisrael.com/not-only-grounded-ships-a-look-at-past-crises-that-closed-suez/) — **Grade B1** · Type: Regional / retrospective view (Israeli outlet, AP wire)

     Summary and analysis: Written during the 2021 *Ever Given* blockage and carried by an Israeli paper, this AP explainer recalls the eight-year closure after 1967 and the "Yellow Fleet" stranded in the Great Bitter Lake. It also recalls Sadat reopening the canal in 1975 after talks with Israel. It cites a UN study that put the cost at $1.7 billion a year in lost trade and higher shipping costs, plus $250 million a year in lost Egyptian tolls. These are rare annual figures for severity, including the canal state's own losses. The account presents diplomacy, not naval power, as what ended the closure. Trace the UN study (probably UNCTAD, 40) before citing the figures, and compare them with the $10 billion total that Time quoted in June 1975.

     Reliability: Wire copy from a major agency, but written in 2021 rather than at the time, so trace the UN study before citing its figures.

42. **Egypt and Israel (1974). Agreement on Disengagement of Forces (Kilometre 101), annexed to UN document S/11198.** United Nations (UNISPAL), 18 January. [link](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-181723) — **Grade A1** (graded here) · Type: Official / primary (treaty text)

     Summary and analysis: Witnessed by the UNEF commander and passed to the Security Council by the Secretary-General on 18 January 1974, the agreement commits Egypt and Israel to the ceasefire. It moves Egyptian forces west of "Line A" and Israeli forces, including those west of the canal and the Bitter Lakes, east of "Line B", and places UNEF II in the zone between them. The UN reported disengagement complete by 4 March 1974, and clearance began on 11 April (45). This is the key primary evidence for the counter-argument. The canal could not be cleared while two armies faced each other across it, so it took this US-brokered political deal to make cooperation possible. The UN witnessed the deal and policed the buffer zone, but did not reopen the canal.

     Reliability: The original treaty text circulated by the UN, so it is authoritative on the terms, though its map was missing when it was issued and the reopening itself was handled in side letters (43).

43. **Nixon, R. (1974). Letter from President Nixon to Egyptian President Sadat, 17 January 1974 (FRUS 1969–1976, vol. XXVI, doc. 13).** Office of the Historian, US Department of State. [link](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v26/d13) — **Grade A2** (graded here) · Type: Official / primary (diplomatic document)

     Summary and analysis: Sent as the disengagement deal (42) was concluded, and drafted by Kissinger according to his memoirs, this letter expects Egypt to begin work towards an early return of the canal to full operation. It assures Sadat that Israel will not take military action against the civilian cities along the canal if Egypt goes ahead. A companion letter to Golda Meir (doc. 12) records that Sadat gave matching assurances. This is the strongest primary evidence that the reopening was a political bargain between two states, brokered by the US. Rebuilding the canal cities and reopening the waterway gave Egypt a stake in keeping the peace. Use it to argue that cooperation followed politics: the multinational clearance carried out this deal rather than causing it.

     Reliability: An official US document published by the State Department's historians, though only search summaries were seen and it shows only the American side of the bargain.

44. **Time (1974). Egypt: Clear the Canal / Egypt: Salvaging Suez (issue of 6 May 1974).** *Time*. [link](https://time.com/archive/6611227/egypt-clear-the-canal/) — **Grade B2** · Type: News report (contemporary magazine)

     Summary and analysis: Time reports the start of the clearance. US Navy Sea Stallion helicopters worked with British minesweepers to find "thousands of shells, rockets and bombs from the 1967 and 1973 wars". They swept for magnetic, acoustic and pressure mines under US Rear Admiral Brian McCauley, and the US share of the cost was put at about $15 million. It is independent evidence from the time for the joint-operations factor (the US, Britain, France and Egypt) and for technology. Imperial War Museum film records show that the British part, Operation Rheostat, began work on 1 May 1974, after Egypt accepted a British offer in February. Use it to back up the CTF-65 report (45) from outside the Navy, and note that it followed the disengagement deal (42).

     Reliability: Contemporary weekly reporting, but it is unclear which page holds which figure and the $15 million is not confirmed elsewhere.

45. **Commander Task Force 65 (1975). CTF Sixty-Five Final Report: Suez Canal Clearance Operations Nimbus Star, Nimbus Moon, Nimrod Spar.** DTIC AD-A010 261. [link](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA010261.pdf) — **Grade B1** · Type: Official / primary (US Navy after-action report)

     Summary and analysis: The US Navy's official after-action report records the clearance from 11 April to 15 December 1974 in three operations. Nimbus Star was minesweeping, mainly by US helicopters, with British and French units. Nimbus Moon trained Egyptian personnel, and Nimrod Spar removed wrecks. The task force chose to sweep mines from helicopters, partly because of reports that an Israeli aircraft had dropped a Mk 36 Destructor mine. It is the authoritative primary source for joint operations and response speed. About seven years passed before clearance began, but once the January 1974 disengagement (42) was in place the work took eight months. It therefore supports the view that cooperation worked, but only once politics allowed it.

     Reliability: The official record by the team that did the work, but it is a US self-assessment that says little about British, French or Egyptian roles, and only its front matter and contents were seen.

46. **Boyd, J.H., Jr. (1976). Nimrod Spar: Clearing the Suez Canal.** *Proceedings* (US Naval Institute) 102/2/876, February. [link](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1976/february/nimrod-spar-clearing-suez-canal) — **Grade B2** · Type: Academic / practitioner journal article (primary account)

     Summary and analysis: Written by Captain J. Huntly Boyd Jr., the US Navy Supervisor of Salvage who ran it, this article describes Nimrod Spar, the last phase of the clearance. That phase removed ten designated wrecks. The contractor Murphy Pacific Marine Salvage did the work after the US agreed, in talks with Egypt, to pay for it. The article explains why mines and unexploded explosives had to be cleared before the wrecks could be lifted and the channel dredged, which helps explain how long the work took. It is evidence for joint operations and for burden-sharing: the clearance depended on one great power's money and expertise as much as on any multilateral framework. Use the publisher's date (February 1976), not January.

     Reliability: A first-hand account by the official in charge, valuable for detail but probably self-congratulatory, and the online text is a scan that may contain errors.

47. **Associated Press (1975). [Sadat reopens the Suez Canal].** *The Michigan Daily*, 6 June. [link](https://digital.bentley.umich.edu/midaily/mdp.39015071754639/265) — **Grade B1** · Type: News report (contemporary wire)

     Summary and analysis: Same-day AP copy reports that, "leading a convoy of Egyptian ships dwarfed by a big American cruiser", Sadat reopened the canal on 5 June 1975, sailing on the destroyer *October 6*, eight years to the day after it closed. The US cruiser *Little Rock* is a visible sign of the American role in the clearance (45) and the diplomacy (43). It gives a dated report from the time for the duration measure (eight years of total closure), and Aramco World's eyewitness account confirms it. Note the six-month gap between the end of clearance in December 1974 and the reopening. It came from political timing, tied to the Sinai talks and the anniversary, not from technical delay, which supports the counter-argument that politics set the pace.

     Reliability: AP wire copy reprinted in a student newspaper's free archive, reliable for the event but brief, and the exact headline is unconfirmed.

48. **Samuelson, M. (1975). Suez Canal.** *The Spectator*, 7 June, p. 11. [link](https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/7th-june-1975/11/suez-canal) — **Grade C2** · Type: News commentary (contemporary opinion)

     Summary and analysis: Written two days after the reopening, this British piece calls it "one of the more significant anti-climaxes of our time". It argues that the reopening mattered more as an Egyptian political victory than as a shipping event, because the industry had adapted: supertankers too big for the canal now carried Gulf oil round the Cape. It also notes that Egypt had barred both Israeli ships and other countries' ships carrying cargo to or from Israel, and still let only Israeli cargoes, not Israeli ships, through. It is the best evidence from the time that alternative factors (technology and adapted supply chains) reduced severity, and it shows the limits of the settlement. Maritime Reporter later found that by 1978 only 17% of tankers could transit fully laden.

     Reliability: An opinion piece from a right-leaning British weekly, useful as a judgement made at the time but not a source of data.

49. **Al-Ahram Weekly (n.d.). The Suez Canal: A waterway to die for.** *Ahram Online*. [link](https://english.ahram.org.eg/NewsContentP/50/408198/AlAhram-Weekly/The-Suez-Canal-A-waterway-to-die-for.aspx) — **Grade D1** · Type: Regional / retrospective view (Egyptian state media)

     Summary and analysis: This history feature in Egypt's leading state-owned paper recalls that the canal stayed closed for eight years after 1967. Sunken ships were then cleared, and Sadat reopened it on 5 June 1975, the same date it had closed. The feature presents the canal as a symbol of national sovereignty and sacrifice. It gives the canal state's view, which the Western sources lack: the closure was Egypt's own act of war, and the reopening a national achievement rather than a gift from foreign navies. Use it with Egypt Today's 2021 timeline, which presents the reopening as a peace gesture after talks with Israel. Together they show that Egyptian accounts credit diplomacy and Egypt's own decisions, not the multinational clearance.

     Reliability: Egyptian state-owned media with a nationalist framing and no confirmed publication date, so its dates are reliable but its interpretation is the official story.
