## What each review argues or finds

| 1 | Li, Chua and Yuen (2024) | Maritime disruptions can be sorted into types, and each type needs its own management strategy. | It groups disruptions into categories, maps their impacts on shipping, and sets out the strategies used to manage them, from a database search of the literature. |
| 2 | Sikal et al. | 3 | Hormuz thematic review (2026) | Chokepoint shocks spread through supply chains, and Hormuz is the most important test case. | Based on 22 peer-reviewed articles, it treats Hormuz as a stress test for global oil and LNG supply chains and draws policy lessons on vulnerability. |
| 4 | López et al. (2025) | Geopolitical disruptions come in distinct types, and firms' responses can be matched to them. | From 80 articles, it identifies six types of geopolitical disruption and finds that financial management, collaboration and resilience are broadly effective responses. |
| 5 | Maritime logistics in conflict (2025) | Recent conflicts, including the Red Sea crisis, have become a major source of shipping disruption. | From 44 studies (2015–24), it finds higher freight costs, rerouting and port delays, and groups the responses: digital tracking, port diversification and other resilience measures. |

## Reliability analysis

Each review's claims were checked against the project's own graded sources. Grades use the Admiralty Code (A–F for the producer, 1–6 for the content).

**1. Li, Chua and Yuen (2024), A2.** *Transport Policy* is a respected peer-reviewed journal, and Yuen is an established maritime researcher at Nanyang Technological University. It agrees in general terms with the project's evidence that disruptions differ in type and need different responses, which is the same logic as the EPQ's argument by type of threat. However, it is a general review of shipping disruption, so it says little about the three chokepoints, and it is paywalled.

**2. Sikal et al. (2025), B3.** Its central finding, that the effect of a chokepoint shock depends on alternative routes and governance, matches the EPQ closely: the Hormuz closure did far more harm than the Red Sea attacks because pipelines could carry only about 9 of the 20 mb/d (Al Jazeera, 27 March 2026, B2), while Red Sea ships could reroute round the Cape (UNCTAD, A1). However, the journal it appeared in could not be confirmed and only a summary was seen, so the content grade is 3.

**3. Hormuz thematic review (2026), B3.** Its starting point, that Hormuz carries a large share of world oil and LNG, matches the IEA (about 20 mb/d, roughly 25% of seaborne oil, A1). It is useful because it gathers 22 studies on one of the EPQ's cases. However, it is very new, its authors were not visible, and it is unclear how rigorously it was peer-reviewed, so treat it as a lead to other studies rather than evidence in itself.

**4. López et al. (2025), A2.** *Transportation Research Part E* is a leading transport journal, and the review's systematic method (80 articles) is strong. Its finding that collaboration is an effective response fits the EPQ's evidence that information sharing cut harm per incident. However, it studies how firms respond to geopolitical shocks, not how states cooperate, so it cannot test the EPQ's question about multilateral cooperation directly.

**5. Maritime logistics in conflict (2025), B2.** Its findings agree with the project's Red Sea data: freight rates rose sharply (Drewry WCI +246% by January 2024, B2), ships rerouted round the Cape (Cape arrivals +89% by mid-2024, UNCTAD, A1), and port calls were disrupted. It follows the PRISMA method for systematic reviews. However, the journal is newer and less established than the others, so its grade stays at B.

**6. Bueger (2014), A2.** Bueger is one of the leading maritime security researchers, also author of *Understanding Maritime Security* (Oxford University Press, 2024), and *Cooperation and Conflict* is a peer-reviewed international relations journal. Its three pillars are consistent with how the EPQ is built: incidents (how piracy works), cooperation (organisational responses) and context (history). However, it is from 2014, before the 2025 Singapore Strait surge, and it maps the field rather than giving new data.

**7. GSDRC report, B2.** GSDRC is a research service funded by the UK government, run by the University of Birmingham and partners. Its five causes fit the Malacca evidence: geography (the narrow, busy strait), weak law enforcement (rare prosecutions: Kontorovich and Art, 2010, A2), and economic dislocation (Indonesian poverty around 23–24% in 1999: SMERU, B2). However, it is a short desk summary that relies on other authors, so the original studies it cites should be checked.

**8. Menzel (2022), A2.** *Politics and Governance* is a peer-reviewed, open-access journal, and the study uses real incident data from 2001–21. Its conclusion, that Asia's overlapping schemes work better than critics expect, partly challenges the EPQ: the project's process tracing gives cooperation only 15.8–23.7% of the fall to 2008, behind or tied with the 2004 tsunami (Al Jazeera, 2005, B2). The two can be reconciled: Menzel shows cooperation helped, the EPQ shows it was not the main cause. It is the most important counter-argument in this set and should appear in the evaluation chapter.

**9. Bibliometric piracy review, C3.** It shows that research interest followed real piracy, peaking around the Somali crisis. That is useful context for why most piracy research is about Somalia rather than Malacca. However, its authors and journal could not be confirmed, and counting publications says nothing about whether cooperation worked.

**Overall verdict.** The most reliable reviews are López et al., Li, Chua and Yuen, Bueger and Menzel, all peer-reviewed in established journals. Sikal et al. and the Hormuz review are useful but need their details checked. Together they support two of the EPQ's main points: that alternatives and governance decide how severe a disruption is, and that cooperation helps against piracy. Menzel pushes back on how small the EPQ judges cooperation's role at Malacca, which makes it a valuable counter-argument.

## Source evaluations

1. ***A Review on Maritime Disruption Management: Categories, Impacts, and Strategies* by Li, Chua and Yuen (*Transport Policy*, 2024).** The review sorts maritime disruptions into types and maps their impacts and how they are managed. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it was peer-reviewed in a respected transport journal by established researchers. I chose it because it gives me a framework for comparing very different disruptions, such as a state closure and a pirate raid. However, it is general rather than about my three straits, and it is paywalled, so I should use it for the framework, not for evidence about my cases.

2. **Review of maritime chokepoint research, 2000–2025, by Sikal et al. (2025).** The review traces how research on chokepoints has developed and finds that the harm from a shock depends on alternative routes and governance. Its provenance is less certain because I could not confirm the journal, and I only saw a summary. I chose it because its main finding supports my argument that Hormuz was more severe than the Red Sea because it had fewer alternatives. However, I should check the full paper and its journal before relying on it.

3. **The thematic review of chokepoint shocks using Hormuz as its case (2026; title to check).** The review brings together 22 peer-reviewed studies on how chokepoint shocks spread through oil and gas supply chains. Its provenance is uncertain because it is very recent and I could not confirm its authors or how it was reviewed. I chose it because it gathers research on one of my case studies in one place. However, I should use it mainly to find the studies it reviews, and cite those directly where possible.

4. ***Modeling Supply Chain Disruptions due to Geopolitical Reasons: A Systematic Literature Review* by López, Morales-Contreras, Langella and Alonso-Monge (*Transportation Research Part E*, 2025).** The review analyses 80 studies and identifies six types of geopolitical disruption and the responses that work against them. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it was published in a leading transport journal and uses a systematic method. I chose it because it shows that collaboration is an effective response to geopolitical shocks, which relates to my question about cooperation. However, it studies firms rather than states, so I cannot use it as direct evidence that multilateral cooperation between governments reduced risk.

5. **Systematic review of maritime logistics in times of conflict (*Journal of Disaster Risk Reduction and Resilience*, 2025).** The review analyses 44 studies from 2015–24 and finds that conflicts such as the Red Sea crisis raised freight costs, caused rerouting and delayed ports. It is fairly reliable because it follows the standard PRISMA method, and its findings match official data from UNCTAD and Drewry. I chose it because it confirms the severity of my Red Sea case using many studies at once. However, the journal is new and less established, so I should support its claims with stronger sources.

6. ***Piracy Studies: Academic Responses to the Return of an Ancient Menace* by Christian Bueger (*Cooperation and Conflict*, 2014).** The review maps piracy research into three areas: how piracy works, how states respond, and history and theory. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it was peer-reviewed and written by one of the leading experts on maritime security, and a free copy is available. I chose it because it helps me structure my Malacca case and my literature review. However, it is over ten years old and does not cover recent events such as the 2025 Singapore Strait surge.

7. ***Piracy in the Horn of Africa, West Africa and the Strait of Malacca* by GSDRC.** The report summarises research on the causes of piracy and compares three regions, including Malacca. It is fairly reliable because GSDRC is a research service funded by the UK government and based at a university, but it is a short summary rather than original research. I chose it because its five causes of piracy help me explain why piracy rose and fell at Malacca, and its comparison shows what is special about Southeast Asia. However, I should check the original studies it cites rather than relying on its summary.

8. **Menzel's study of Asian counter-piracy cooperation (*Politics and Governance*, 2022; check the exact title).** The article tests whether Asia's overlapping counter-piracy schemes reduced piracy, using incident data from 2001–21, and finds they worked better than critics expected. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it was peer-reviewed and uses real data, and it is free to read. I chose it because it gives the strongest counter-argument to my finding that cooperation was only a minor cause at Malacca. However, the author admits the effect is hard to measure, so I should present it as a challenge to my verdict rather than as proof that I am wrong.

9. **A bibliometric systematic review of maritime piracy research (Cambridge Core).** The review counts how much research on piracy has been published over time and finds a peak around 2013, during the Somali piracy crisis. Its provenance is uncertain because I could not confirm its authors or journal. I chose it because it explains why most piracy research focuses on Somalia rather than Malacca, which is a limitation of the evidence for my case. However, it measures research activity, not piracy or cooperation, so it gives only background.
