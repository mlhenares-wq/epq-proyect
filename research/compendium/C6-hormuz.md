### Peer-reviewed and academic

- **Lott, A. and Kawagishi, S. (2022). The legal regime of the Strait of Hormuz and attacks against oil tankers.** *Ocean Development & International Law* 53(2–3). [link](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00908320.2022.2096158) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Explains the transit-passage rules for Hormuz and the 2019 tanker attacks and *Stena Impero* seizure. It argues states never proved beyond reasonable doubt that Iran laid the 2019 mines. Reliability: Specialist peer-reviewed law journal (Norwegian law-of-the-sea centre), written before 2026, and its verdict on Iran is contested by US/UK governments; abstract seen, not full text.
- **Richer, C. (2024). "No Sense in Military Terms": Reconsidering the Surprising Use of Force in a Gray Zone Conflict.** *Journal of Military and Strategic Studies* 23(3). [link](https://jmss.org/article/view/76863) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Argues US officials misjudged how far the 1987–88 Tanker War could escalate; the first escorted tanker, *Bridgeton*, hit a mine. Kuwait's 1987 request to reflag its tankers started US convoys. Reliability: Peer-reviewed open-access Canadian journal, but US-centred and built on US officials' words; conclusion seen only in a search summary.
- **Shepard, J.U. and Pratson, L.F. (2020). Maritime piracy in the Strait of Hormuz and implications of energy export security.** *Energy Policy* 140. [link](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S030142152030135X) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Models piracy and harassment as a "soft restriction" on Gulf exports, showing some exporters are hit harder than others depending on their fuel and risk sensitivity. Reliability: Duke University authors in a leading energy journal (DOI confirmed), but it is a model of a rare threat type, not observed 2026 data.
- **Lassoued [initial to check] (2026). Maritime chokepoint insecurity and systemic trade vulnerability: a transportation security assessment of the Strait of Hormuz.** *Journal of Transportation Security* 19(1). [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12198-026-00345-y) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Treats Hormuz as a problem of keeping a transport corridor secure, not just an oil-price shock, which fits questions about the likelihood as well as the severity of disruption. Reliability: Peer-reviewed Springer journal, but the author's name is unconfirmed and it was written before outcomes were known; abstract only.
- **Meza, A., Ari, I., Al Sada, M. and Koç, M. (2026). Implications of interrupting the Hormuz Strait in the LNG trade.** *Journal of Transportation Security*. [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12198-026-00350-1) — **Grade A2**. Summary: A simulation finds a one-month closure could be absorbed by switching to Pacific suppliers, but a closure of more than six months leaves LNG demand unmet. Reliability: Peer-reviewed, but by a Qatari state-funded university writing about Qatar's main export route, and the results come from a model, not real 2026 data.
- **Navias, M.S. and Hooton, E.R. (1996). Tanker Wars: The Assault on Merchant Shipping during the Iran–Iraq Conflict, 1980–1988.** I.B. Tauris (review in *Iranian Studies*). [link](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/iranian-studies/article/abs/tanker-wars-the-assault-on-merchant-shipping-during-the-iraniraq-conflict-19801988-martin-s-navias-and-e-r-horton-london-and-new-york-ib-tauris-publishers-1996-ix-244-pp-including-appendix-notes-index-of-ships-and-general-index-cloth/555128C86253A41B2A6257F098EC20C2) — **Grade A2**. Summary: The standard scholarly history of the Tanker War, with an index of ships attacked; Iraq began attacks in 1981 and Iran retaliated from 1984, and over 400 seafarers died. Reliability: Academic book reviewed in journals, but dated (1996), needs library access, and may duplicate plan link 8.
- **Walker, G.K. (2000). The Tanker War, 1980–88: Law and Policy.** US Naval War College, *International Law Studies* vol. 74. [link](https://digital-commons.usnwc.edu/ils/vol74/iss1/) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Covers the law of neutral shipping, reflagging, convoys and UN Resolution 598 during the Tanker War, with a free full text. Reliability: Authoritative legal study, but published by a US Navy institution, so it takes a US legal viewpoint.
- **López Martín, A.G. (n.d., c. 2012–13). El "cierre" del estrecho de Ormuz: un análisis desde el Derecho internacional.** Dialnet / Ágora. [link](https://agora.edu.es/descarga/articulo/4371328.pdf) — **Grade A2**. Summary: A Spanish legal article asking whether Iran may lawfully close Hormuz or charge fees under the law of the sea, and whether a closure could be a reprisal for sanctions. Reliability: Peer-reviewed article by a Complutense law professor, but probably written over a decade before the 2026 war and the date is unconfirmed.
- **[Authors to check] (2026). The price of passage: the legality of Iranian transit charges in the Strait of Hormuz under the Law of the Sea.** *Australian Journal of Maritime & Ocean Affairs* (to confirm). [link](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/18366503.2026.2701606) — **Grade A3**. Summary: Covers Iran's toll law and permit rules after the late-April 2026 partial reopening, and argues no state has the right to charge ships simply for passing through. Reliability: Peer-reviewed publisher, but the article and its authors were not found by search, so its existence is unconfirmed.
- **[Authors to check] (2026). Geopolitical disruptions in the Middle East and global supply chain resilience.** *Maritime Economics & Logistics*. [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41278-026-00366-3) — **Grade A3**. Summary: Studies how Middle East disruption hits supply chains and how firms can limit damage; a tracker counted 138 ships a day on 1 March 2026 and 12 on 2 April. Reliability: Leading journal in its field, but authors unknown and only a snippet seen; the 138→12 figure is from an unnamed tracker.
- **[Authors to check] (2012). The effect of transnational threats on the security of Persian Gulf maritime petroleum transportation.** *Journal of Transportation Security*. [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12198-012-0090-y) — **Grade A6**. Summary: Looks at how terrorism, piracy and other non-state threats affect Gulf oil tankers, useful background on types of threat. Reliability: Peer-reviewed Springer journal, but only the title was seen and it is 14 years old, so its content cannot be judged.
- **Saidi, M., Esmaeildoost, F., Khankan, R., Masarani, H. and Toyasaki, F. (2026). Logistical agility at the Strait of Hormuz [full title to check].** *Transport Policy*. [link](https://trid.trb.org/View/2694716) — **Grade A6**. Summary: Looks at how logistics systems adapt to disruption at Hormuz, which supports supply-chain design as an alternative to naval cooperation. Reliability: Strong Elsevier journal, but only a bibliographic record was seen and the title is incomplete.
- **Mobley, R.A. (2022). Deterrence Without Escalation: Fresh Insights into U.S. Decisionmaking During Operation Earnest Will.** *Joint Force Quarterly* 106 (NDU Press). [link](https://ndupress.ndu.edu/Media/News/News-Article-View/Article/3106465/) — **Grade B1**. Summary: Uses declassified documents to show US planners sent no minesweepers ahead, so *Bridgeton* was mined, and struggled to read Iran and avoid escalation. Reliability: US defence-university journal by a former US intelligence analyst, so it reflects a US view with no Iranian sources.
- **Mobley, R.A. (2021). London and Washington [full title to check] on the 1987 reflagging.** *Naval War College Review* 74(2). [link](https://digital-commons.usnwc.edu/nwc-review/vol74/iss2/9) — **Grade B2**. Summary: Uses declassified British records to show London had doubts about America's 1987 reflagging and escorts, so the "coalition" was not united. Reliability: Archive-based but published by the US Navy's college for officers and only editor-reviewed, so expect some bias towards US naval policy.
- **Mobley, R.A. (2016). Fighting Iran: intelligence support during Operation Earnest Will, 1987–88.** *Studies in Intelligence* 60(3), CIA. [link](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Fighting-Iran.pdf) — **Grade B2**. Summary: Describes how intelligence and information sharing supported the 1987–88 escort operation, which can be compared with modern ship-tracking tools. Reliability: Official CIA in-house journal that passed CIA review, so it is pro-US and somewhat self-congratulatory.
- **Robert S. Strauss Center, University of Texas (n.d.). Strait of Hormuz – Tanker War.** Strauss Center. [link](https://www.strausscenter.org/strait-of-hormuz-tanker-war/) — **Grade B2**. Summary: Says the Tanker War cut commercial shipping by 25% and raised crude prices early on, but at its peak disrupted no more than about 2% of ships in the Gulf. Reliability: Neutral university research summary, but undated, and its price claim is disputed by other sources.
- **American Society of International Law (2026). Charges for passage in the Strait of Hormuz.** *ASIL Insights* 30(10). [link](https://asil.org/insights/volume-30-issue-10/) — **Grade B2**. Summary: Explains that the law gives no power to charge ships just for passing through a strait; charges are allowed only for specific services. Reliability: Editorially reviewed briefing by a leading US law society, short and aimed at a US audience; matches other legal sources.
- **Lott, A. (2026). The legality of Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz.** *EJIL: Talk!* [link](https://www.ejiltalk.org/the-legality-of-irans-closure-of-the-strait-of-hormuz/) — **Grade C2**. Summary: Notes that the US, Israel and Iran are all non-parties to UNCLOS, and sets out Iran's argument that only suspendable innocent passage applies against the view that transit passage cannot be suspended. Reliability: Expert blog by a straits specialist, not peer-reviewed, and by the same author as the 2022 ODIL article.
- **Völkerrechtsblog (2026). Iran's Legal Strategy in Hormuz: Law of the Sea Considerations and Rules of Naval Warfare.** *Völkerrechtsblog*, 16 April. [link](https://voelkerrechtsblog.org/irans-legal-strategy-in-hormuz/) — **Grade C2**. Summary: Sets out the strongest legal defence of Iran: it signed but never ratified UNCLOS and objected to transit passage becoming customary law. Reliability: German academic law blog with no named author and no peer review, but useful for presenting Iran's view fairly.
- **Verfassungsblog (2026). More than a Trade Route: Hormuz.** *Verfassungsblog*, 21 May. [link](https://verfassungsblog.de/more-than-a-trade-route-hormuz/) — **Grade C2**. Summary: Says the strait normally carries 80–130 ships a day and about 20% of world oil and LNG, but traffic fell below 10 a day after the war began. Reliability: European academic legal blog, author not shown and not refereed; snippet only.
- **Libman, L. (2026). The U.S.-Iran MOU and the Future of Maritime Shipping Through the Strait of Hormuz.** *Lawfare*, 13 July. [link](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/the-u.s.-iran-mou-and-the-future-of-maritime-shipping-through-the-strait-of-hormuz) — **Grade C2**. Summary: Calls Article 5 of the June 2026 MoU "vague and unsatisfactory", for example on when 60 days of free passage start, and predicted another crisis. Reliability: Legal analysis by a former Israeli military lawyer, so flag an Israeli perspective; snippet only.
- **The Conversation (2026). Despite Its Best Efforts, Iran Won't Be Able to Toll the Strait of Hormuz.** *The Conversation*. [link](https://theconversation.com/despite-its-best-efforts-iran-wont-be-able-to-toll-the-strait-of-hormuz-heres-why-286241) — **Grade C2**. Summary: Argues Hormuz is too large to police like Suez or Panama, so a permanent toll is unenforceable, and Gulf states warn of a dangerous precedent. - **The Conversation (2026). The Strait of Hormuz Is Reopening, but Global Shipping Won't Return to Normal for Months.** *The Conversation*. Reliability: US legal-policy outlet; the author's credentials were not shown and only a snippet was seen.
