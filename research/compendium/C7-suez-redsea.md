### Peer-reviewed and academic

- **Treves, T. (2009). Piracy, law of the sea, and use of force: developments off the coast of Somalia.** *European Journal of International Law* 20(2). [link](https://doi.org/10.1093/ejil/chp027) — **Grade A1**. Summary: Explains how UN Resolution 1816 (2008) let navies enter Somali waters, and why navies were wary of detaining pirates, which led to transfer deals with Kenya. Reliability: A former law-of-the-sea judge in a top law journal, so very authoritative, but from 2009, missing later prosecutions.
- **Guilfoyle, D. (2008). Piracy off Somalia: UN Security Council Resolution 1816 and IMO regional counter-piracy efforts.** *International and Comparative Law Quarterly* 57(3). [link](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-and-comparative-law-quarterly/article/abs/ii-piracy-off-somalia-un-security-council-resolution-1816-and-imo-regional-counterpiracy-efforts/A7C877DE295732AD8D439457F9AA4978) — **Grade A1**. Summary: Covers Resolution 1816, the IMO-led regional cooperation that became the Djibouti Code of Conduct, and the rule that navies needed the Somali government's consent to enter its waters. Reliability: A UCL international lawyer in a leading journal, but written in 2008 before outcomes were known; DOI not confirmed.
- **Yap, W.Y. and Yang, D. (2024). Geopolitical tension and shipping network disruption: analysis of the Red Sea crisis on container port calls.** *Journal of Transport Geography* 121. [link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtrangeo.2024.104004) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Carriers switched to the Cape and added ships to keep port calls steady; daily Suez transits fell from about 75 to about 30 (December 2023–December 2024). Reliability: PolyU researchers in a strong journal; the 75→30 figure is snippet-only and covers year one.
- **Bedoya-Maya, F. et al. (2025). Container port competitiveness amid disruptions: insights from the European maritime network during the Red Sea crisis.** *Journal of Transport Geography* 128. [link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtrangeo.2025.104304) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Transport-chain costs to Europe rose 3–13% depending on the port; hubs near Gibraltar coped best, while feeder ports near Suez lost connections. Reliability: A strong University of Antwerp port-economics team with a free PDF and a confirmed abstract, but it covers only the first half of 2024.
- **[Authors to check] (2026). Mediterranean container port reconfiguration under geopolitical disruption: an AIS-based multi-scale analysis.** *Maritime Economics & Logistics*. [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41278-026-00356-5) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Ship-tracking data over 240 days show capacity, especially the biggest ships, shifting to western Mediterranean ports, so the crisis moved trade around as well as cutting it. Reliability: The leading journal in its field, but the authors are unconfirmed and only a snippet was seen.
- **Bensassi, S. and Martínez-Zarzoso, I. (2012). How costly is modern maritime piracy to the international community?** *Review of International Economics* 20(5). [link](https://doi.org/10.1111/roie.12000) — **Grade A2**. Summary: A gravity model of Europe–Asia trade (1999–2008) finds ten extra hijackings go with about an 11% fall in exports, costing about US$24.5 billion a year. Reliability: Peer-reviewed work by trade economists (one Spanish), but the data stop in 2008 and the cost figure conflicts with another estimate of US$18 billion.
- **Shortland, A. and Vothknecht, M. (2011). Combating "maritime terrorism" off the coast of Somalia.** *European Journal of Political Economy* 27(S1). [link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejpoleco.2011.03.004) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Finds the 2008–10 Gulf of Aden naval mission, including EU Atalanta, was only a partial success: attacks stayed high and moved out of the guarded corridor into the open ocean. Reliability: DIW Berlin and King's College economists in a peer-reviewed journal, but written before piracy collapsed in 2012.
- **Percy, S. and Shortland, A. (2013). The business of piracy in Somalia.** *Journal of Strategic Studies* 36(4). [link](https://doi.org/10.1080/01402390.2012.750242) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Argues Somali piracy is a business run from land that even gains from local stability, so naval patrols alone cannot end it. Reliability: Oxford and King's College academics in a leading strategic-studies journal, though the Somali case may not transfer to state threats.
- **Bueger, C. (2013). Practice, pirates and coast guards: the grand narrative of Somali piracy.** *Third World Quarterly* 34(10). [link](https://doi.org/10.1080/01436597.2013.851896) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Uses pirates' own interviews to show how they justify piracy as "coast guarding" against foreign fishing and dumping, which helps explain why it lasted. Reliability: A well-known maritime-security professor in a peer-reviewed journal, but the study is interpretive and covers Somalia only.
- **Kontorovich, E. and Art, S. (2010). An empirical examination of universal jurisdiction for piracy.** *American Journal of International Law* 104. [link](https://scholarlycommons.law.northwestern.edu/facultyworkingpapers/38) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Using piracy data for 1998–2009, finds that at most 1.47% of clearly punishable piracies were ever prosecuted internationally, so law on paper rarely meant enforcement. Reliability: A Northwestern law professor in a top journal, but pages and DOI are unconfirmed and the data end in 2009.
- **Sun, R., Abouarghoub, W., Demir, E. and Potter, A. (2025/26). Geopolitical disruptions and maritime transitions: environmental and economic costs of rerouting.** *Transportation Research Part A*. [link](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/181932) — **Grade A2**. Summary: Cape detours on three Eurasian routes raise emissions by at least 46% and fleet costs by at least 51%, and add 20–34 days per round trip. Reliability: Cardiff Business School authors in a strong journal, but volume and DOI are unconfirmed and results are modelled.
- **Yang, D., Yue, X. and Yap, W.Y. (2025). Geospatial resilience of shipping alliances: navigating the Red Sea crisis.** *Journal of Transport Geography* 126. Summary: Shipping alliances skipped more ports, especially Red Sea ones, to keep their schedules reliable; no headline figure was seen. Reliability: PolyU researchers in a strong journal, confirmed in search, but its focus on carrier scheduling is narrow.
