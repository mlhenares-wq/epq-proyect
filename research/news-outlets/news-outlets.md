## How to read this list

Each outlet has a grade from the same Admiralty Code used in the rest of the project: a letter for how reliable the producer usually is (A–F), and a number for how far its content is usually confirmed (1–6). The grades come from how each outlet scored when its articles were checked during the research. "Access" says whether you can read it free. The aim is a balance: international agencies for facts, regional papers for each country's point of view, trade press for shipping detail, and archives for the historical periods.

## International news agencies

1. **Reuters (B2, B1 when confirmed).** A global news agency that supplies facts to thousands of other outlets, with correspondents in the Gulf, Cairo and Singapore. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it has strict standards of accuracy and impartiality, and its figures were usually confirmed by official data during the research. I chose it because it reports on all three of my case studies and is free to read. However, its fast reporting sometimes gives early figures that are later revised, so I should check the date and, where possible, the official source it quotes.

2. **Associated Press (B1–B2).** A US-based, non-profit news cooperative that reports events as they happen. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it is one of the most widely trusted agencies in the world, and its archived stories from 1967–75 let me read reports written at the time. I chose it because it covers both my historical and present-day periods, such as the 1975 reopening of the Suez Canal and the 2026 Hormuz attacks. However, its stories are short and written quickly, so they give events rather than analysis, and its American base can shape which stories it covers.

3. **Agence France-Presse (B2).** France's international news agency, strong in the Middle East and Africa. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it is a major independent agency, and its counts of attacks and seafarer deaths matched IMO figures. I chose it because it gives a European, non-American angle, and Arab papers such as Ahram Online often carry its reports. However, I often read it through partner papers that may cut or edit its text, so I should cite AFP as the original source and check the partner's version.

4. **UPI Archives (B1–B2).** United Press International was a major US agency in the 1980s, and its archive is free online. It is reliable in terms of provenance because its stories were written on the day of each event, such as the mining of the *Bridgeton* in July 1987, and other records confirm them. I chose it because it is the best free source of reports from the time on the Tanker War, which is my weakest historical period for evidence. However, some of its claims repeat the US government's view, such as blaming Iran for mines, so I should treat those as claims rather than facts.

## Financial and shipping press

5. **Bloomberg (B1–B2).** A financial news service with specialist reporters on oil, shipping and insurance. It is reliable in terms of provenance because its market data is used by traders, and its figures were confirmed by other sources. I chose it because it reports prices, freight rates and ship-tracking counts that newspapers rarely give. However, most of it is paywalled, so I should read it through free reprints such as gCaptain, and remember that it writes for investors.

6. **CNBC (B1).** A US business broadcaster that reports market reactions as they happen. It is reliable in terms of provenance because its price figures come from live market data, such as Brent closing above $100 the day after the IEA stock release. I chose it because it shows how quickly markets reacted to cooperation. However, it focuses on daily price moves, so it says little about why a crisis happened.

7. **Lloyd's List (B2).** The world's oldest shipping newspaper, read across the insurance and shipping industry. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it has expert reporters and direct access to brokers and underwriters, and it is the main source for war-risk premiums. I chose it because premiums are one of the two likelihood indicators in my risk equation. However, it is behind a subscription, so I may need a school or public library, and it quotes brokers who earn more when rates rise.

8. **gCaptain (B2–C2).** A free shipping news site that reprints Reuters and Bloomberg stories and adds its own reporting. It is fairly reliable in terms of provenance because its best material comes from those agencies, while its own pieces are less checked. I chose it because it gives free access to agency stories on Hormuz transits and naval escorts. However, I should cite the original agency where gCaptain names one, and treat its own claims with more care.

9. **Seatrade Maritime and The Maritime Executive (C1–C2).** Specialist shipping magazines that report traffic, piracy and naval operations. They are fairly reliable in terms of provenance because they are written by maritime journalists, and their figures usually come from official bodies such as the IMO and ReCAAP. I chose them because they report details that general newspapers ignore, such as the July 2025 arrests of a pirate gang in the Riau Islands. However, figures sometimes differ between them (for example, 8 or 11 people arrested), so I should check against the official source.

10. **Splash247 and SAFETY4SEA (C1–C2).** Free shipping news sites that publish quick summaries of ReCAAP and IMB piracy reports. They are fairly reliable in terms of provenance because they repeat official data accurately, but they add little of their own. I chose them because they are the fastest free way to find the latest Malacca and Singapore figures for 2026. However, they are only second-hand, so I should cite the ReCAAP or IMB report itself.

## Historical archives

11. **Time magazine archive (B2).** A US news magazine whose archive from the 1960s and 1970s is free online. It is reliable in terms of provenance because it reported the Suez closure as it happened, such as the canal being "blocked at both ends by scuttled Egyptian ships" in 1967. I chose it because it gives me reports from the time for my historical Suez case, which has few other free sources. However, it wrote for American readers during the Cold War, and some figures (such as the number of trapped ships) differ between its own articles.

12. **Michigan Daily archive (B1).** A student newspaper whose digitised archive reprints AP wire stories from the 1970s. It is reliable in terms of provenance because the stories themselves come from AP, written on the day. I chose it because it holds the AP report of the canal's reopening on 5 June 1975. However, I should cite AP as the author, not the student paper.

13. **The Spectator archive (C2).** A British opinion magazine whose archive is free online. It is only fairly reliable because it publishes commentary rather than news. I chose it because a 1975 article called the reopening an "anti-climax", which shows how larger tankers had made the canal less important. However, it is one writer's opinion, so I should use it as a view, not as evidence of fact.

## Gulf and Middle East press

14. **Al Jazeera (B2).** A Qatari-funded international broadcaster with strong coverage of the Gulf, Yemen and Egypt. It is usually reliable for events, and its reporting on attacks and pipelines matched other sources. I chose it because it gives an Arab perspective that Western outlets can miss. However, it is funded by the Qatari state, so its political coverage can reflect Qatar's foreign policy.

15. **The National (C1–C2).** A UAE newspaper based in Abu Dhabi. It is fairly reliable for Gulf events, such as the UK–France naval mission still not having ships in the strait in October 2026. I chose it because it shows how the Gulf states, the countries most exposed to a Hormuz closure, saw the crisis. However, it is owned by an Abu Dhabi state-linked company and avoids criticising the UAE government.

16. **Arab News, Saudi Gazette and Asharq Al-Awsat (C2).** Saudi-owned papers that report Saudi oil exports, pipelines and Red Sea shipping. They are fairly reliable for Saudi facts, such as exports moving through Yanbu during the Hormuz closure. I chose them because Saudi Arabia was affected by both of my Middle East cases. However, they follow the Saudi government's line, so their figures should be checked against the IEA or Kpler data.

17. **Ahram Online and Egypt Today (D2).** Egyptian news sites close to the state. They are not usually reliable as independent sources, but they publish the Suez Canal Authority's official figures, such as revenue falling from $10.25bn to $3.99bn. I chose them because they are the only source for some canal figures and show Egypt's official view. However, they repeat the government's message, so I should compare their figures with UNCTAD and the IMF, and describe them as Egypt's official claims.

18. **Mada Masr (C1).** An independent Egyptian news site, often critical of the government. It is fairly reliable because its reporting is careful and confirmed by other sources, such as Egypt not publicly joining the Prosperity Guardian coalition. I chose it to balance the state-linked Egyptian media. However, it is blocked inside Egypt and has an opposition viewpoint, so it is one side of the story.

19. **Times of Israel (B2).** An Israeli news site with wide coverage of the Gaza war and the Houthis. It is usually reliable for events, such as the link between Gaza ceasefires and pauses in Houthi attacks. I chose it because Israel was the declared target of the Houthi campaign, so its view matters for my Red Sea case. However, it reports from one side of the conflict, so I should balance it with Arab and Yemeni sources.

20. **South24 (C2).** A Yemeni research and news centre based in Aden. It is fairly reliable for counts of Houthi attacks, which matched other trackers. I chose it because it gives a Yemeni, local view of the Red Sea crisis. However, it is linked to southern Yemeni groups opposed to the Houthis, so it is not neutral.

21. **Iran International (C1–C2).** A Persian-language channel based in London that reports on Iran. It is fairly reliable for events inside Iran, such as Kpler data on Iranian oil flows in October 2026. I chose it because it reports Iranian developments that Western media miss. However, it is critical of the Iranian government and its funding has been questioned, so I should balance it with other sources.

22. **Press TV (D2–D3).** Iran's state-run English-language broadcaster. It is not reliable as a source of fact, because it presents the Iranian government's position. I chose it only to show how Iran justified closing the strait, which is useful when I explain the state's motives. However, I must never use it for figures, and I should label it clearly as Iranian state media.

## Southeast and South Asian press

23. **The Straits Times (C1–C2).** Singapore's main newspaper, close to the government. It is fairly reliable for Singapore's policies and for piracy figures that it takes from ReCAAP. I chose it because Singapore is the centre of information sharing in the Malacca case and hosts the ReCAAP centre. However, it is partly paywalled, its older archive is only free inside Singapore's National Library, and it reflects the Singapore government's view.

24. **CNA, Channel NewsAsia (C1).** Singapore's international broadcaster, covering Southeast Asia. It is fairly reliable for regional security news, such as patrols and arrests in the straits. I chose it because its coverage is free and detailed for the 2025–26 Malacca period. However, it is state-owned, so it presents Singapore's efforts favourably.

25. **The Star and Malay Mail (C1).** Malaysian newspapers that report piracy figures and Malaysia's position. They are fairly reliable for ReCAAP figures and statements from Malaysian officials. I chose them because Malaysia is one of the three coastal states patrolling the Strait of Malacca, so its view is essential. However, they often repeat official statements, such as a minister saying there are "no pirates, only petty crooks", which are claims rather than facts.

26. **Kompas and The Jakarta Post (C2).** Indonesia's leading newspaper and its main English-language daily. They are fairly reliable for Indonesian events, such as police operations and the fall in oil flows through Malacca during the Hormuz crisis. I chose them because Indonesian enforcement was the largest single cause of the 2025–26 fall in robberies. However, Kompas is in Indonesian, and both can follow the government's line on sovereignty.

27. **Nikkei Asia (B2).** A Japanese business newspaper covering Asian economies. It is usually reliable for energy and trade data, since Japan imports most of its oil through Hormuz and Malacca. I chose it because it shows how the crises affected Asian importers. However, it is partly paywalled and writes from a business angle.

28. **South China Morning Post (C2).** A Hong Kong newspaper, owned by Alibaba, that covers China's foreign policy. It is fairly reliable for China's position, such as Beijing's direct deals with the Houthis for safe passage. I chose it because China is the largest buyer of Gulf oil and an important player outside the Western coalitions. However, its independence from Beijing has been questioned since its change of ownership in 2016, so I should treat its China coverage with care.

29. **Dawn (C2).** Pakistan's oldest English-language newspaper. It is fairly reliable, but some of its figures, such as seafarer deaths in the Red Sea, differed from other counts. I chose it because South Asia supplies many of the world's seafarers, so it shows the human cost of attacks. However, its figures should be checked against the IMO.

## European press

30. **Última Hora (C1).** A Spanish newspaper. It is fairly reliable for Spain's official decisions, such as declining to join the US-led Prosperity Guardian coalition. I chose it because it shows that even allies disagreed over how to cooperate, which supports my point that cooperation had gaps. However, it is a regional paper, so for Spanish policy I should also look for El País or official government statements.

## Outlets to avoid or use only for framing

- **Hellenic Shipping News (D1–D2):** it mostly reposts other outlets' articles without checking them. Find and cite the original.
- **Xinhua and Global Times (D2–D3):** Chinese state media. Use only to show China's official framing, never for figures.
- **Tehran Times and Kayhan (D2–D4):** Iranian state-aligned papers. As with Press TV, use only to show the government's view.
- **Unnamed blogs and aggregator sites:** they often copy figures without a source, as with the blog that quoted a 1967 New York Times article. Trace the figure back to the original.
