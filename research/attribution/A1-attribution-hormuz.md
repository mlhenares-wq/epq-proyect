# A1. Attribution test for the Strait of Hormuz: Tanker War (1980–88) vs the 2026 closure

Was multilateral cooperation the **main** reason risk changed at Hormuz between the two periods?

Method: the Chokepoint Disruption Risk Index (`research/risk-equation.md`). R = L × S, cooperation index C, and the Cause Contribution Score with process tracing.
Compiled 20 October 2026. The data runs to about 19 October 2026, and the 2026 crisis is still going on.

## Read this first: how reliable these numbers are

- Almost every figure comes from **search-result snippets** collected in research files 01–13, plus 11 extra web searches for this file. **No page was opened in full**, because page fetching is blocked here. Before a number goes into the EPQ, open the source and check it.
- Some indicators **have no data for the 1980s**. The clearest case is freight rates for the Tanker War, which I could not find at all. Following the method's rules, I left that indicator out and reduced n. I did **not** estimate it.
- A few context figures come from my own background knowledge, not from a source. They are marked **[BK]**. Do not cite them until you have checked them yourself.
- As the rubric requires, I used **peak-year** values for incidents and premiums and the **peak fall** for trade.

- **ΔR = +6.9 (+52%)** on the central scores. Risk **rose**. It moved from High to Very high.
- **The whole rise comes from severity.** L hardly changed (5.0 → 4.67). S went up from 2.67 to 4.33. In the 1980s ships were attacked often, but oil kept flowing. In 2026 the flow itself stopped.
- **Range check:** if you take the highest Tanker War scores and the lowest 2026 scores, ΔR runs from about **−0.7 to +8.5**. The negative end needs every disputed score to go against 2026 at the same time, which is unlikely. On any single change, ΔR stays positive.
- **Consistency check:** the 2026 freight score is the one indicator the Tanker War lacks. If you drop it from 2026 too, L = 4.5 and R = 19.5, so ΔR = +6.2. The conclusion does not change.
- **The key result:** cooperation **increased** (C went from 9 to 12) while risk **also increased**. On the face of it, more cooperation did not bring lower risk. The rest of this file tests why.

## 3. Cause Contribution table

**Sign convention** (method, section 4b): **+** means the factor **reduced** risk, or held it down. **−** means the factor **increased** risk. Cooperation that raised risk gets a minus. Shares are calculated on the unsigned totals, which measure how much causal weight each factor carries.

### Evidence for each score

**1. Multilateral cooperation (3).**
- **Change 1:** C rose by only 3 points, and naval cooperation did not change (2 → 2).
- **Timing 1:** the IEA release, Res. 2817 and the insurance facility all came *after* the closure, as reactions to it. The pre-existing frameworks (IMSC 2019, JMIC) were in place before.
- **Mechanism 1:** the IEA released about 330m barrels by 15 October (file 04 #38). But Brent closed above $100 **the day after** the announcement (file 04 #22). The IEA itself calls the release "short-term", and CFR calls reserve releases "insufficient" for a long closure (file 10 #10). No source measures how much the release lowered prices.
- **Counterfactual 0:** risk rose anyway. Bordoff and O'Sullivan credit the milder-than-expected outcome to oversupply and pipelines, **not** cooperation (file 10 #13).
- **Direction:** in 2026, multilateral cooperation worked *against* the rise (+), but weakly. In 1987–88, Earnest Will **raised** short-term risk (−): the *Bridgeton* was mined on the first convoy, the *Sea Isle City* was hit, and the clashes escalated (file 07 Debate 5, file 12 #31, #35). That escalation pushed the 1980s R *up*, so it makes ΔR look smaller than it otherwise would.

**2. Type of threat (8).**
- **Change 2:** in the 1980s, two belligerents attacked each other's trade. Iraq made about two-thirds of the attacks (file 13 #76). Iran needed the strait for its own exports, and neither side tried to close it. In 2026, Iran was directly at war with the US and Israel and *declared* the strait closed. It set up a permit and toll authority (file 10 #4) and blacklisted 45 tankers (file 09 H21).
- **Timing 2:** the strikes began on 28 February. Transits collapsed within days (129 → 6/day).
- **Mechanism 2:** each IRGC declaration matched a fall in transits in the tracking data. On 21 June, for example, transits fell from 26 to 5 (Kpler, file 04 #27).
- **Counterfactual 2:** sources say escorts cannot get ships past a state at war (file 07 Debate 6), and Chatham House says the strait "cannot be opened by force" (file 01 #9). Talmadge (2008) predicted that Iran could make the strait unusable (file 01 #6).

**3. Technology (6).**
- **Change 1:** mines were decisive in **both** periods (*Bridgeton* in 1987; about 80 mines in 2026). Iran also had anti-ship missiles in the 1980s (the Silkworm hit on *Sea Isle City*). What is new is cheap drones and precision missiles, and much larger stocks (Talmadge).
- **Timing 2:** these capabilities were built up before 2026.
- **Mechanism 2:** drone attacks shut the Saudi East–West pipeline on 13 September (file 13 #29). The *GFS Pride* sank after a projectile attack (file 09 H25).
- **Counterfactual 1:** mines alone could have closed the strait, so technology mattered only partly.

**4. Physical alternatives (6).**
- **Change 1:** bypasses existed in the 1980s too. Petroline was built during the Tanker War, the IPSA line was built in the 1980s, and Kirkuk–Ceyhan was running. The *CSM* (1987) said "most Saudi crude bypassed Hormuz via the Yanbu pipeline". In 2026, capacity was about **9 mb/d** against about **20 mb/d** through the strait (file 07 7a). Actual flow was lower: about 2–5.8 mb/d on the East–West line, depending on the date (file 13 #26, #28).
- **Timing 2:** ADCOP has run since 2012, and the East–West line was expanded in 2019.
- **Mechanism 2:** Kpler data show Fujairah exports rising from 1.17 to 1.62 mb/d.
- **Counterfactual 1:** the pipelines cushioned severity, so the rise in S would have been larger without them. They did not cause the change in R.
- **Data gap:** I could not verify capacities for the 1980s (figures such as 1.85 or 3.2 mb/d for Petroline are unconfirmed).

**5. Trade concentration (7).**
- **Change 2:** oil flow through the strait rose from over 7 mb/d in 1984 (USIP) to about **20 mb/d** in 2025, about 25% of seaborne oil (IEA, file 01 #4). Qatar's LNG is about 20% of the world total, and it has no bypass (files 10 #8, 13 #40). In 1984 the strait carried roughly 12% of world demand of about 59 mb/d **[BK, check in the Energy Institute *Statistical Review*]**.
- **Timing 2.**
- **Mechanism 2:** the World Bank calls it the "largest oil supply shock on record" (about −10 mb/d). The IEA reports 14.4 mb/d shut in during May (file 06 #18–19).
- **Counterfactual 1:** the same closure would have hurt less if the strait carried less, but the closure itself was the trigger.

**6. US unilateral power vs multilateral action (7).**
- **Change 2:** in 1987–88 the US acted as a **neutral protector**. Earnest Will was mostly unilateral, and Praying Mantis crippled Iran's navy. Iran then accepted Res. 598. In 2026 the US was a **belligerent**: its strikes on 28 February with Israel started the crisis. Its blockade of Iranian ports (from 13 April) led Iran to re-close the strait on 18 April (file 13, contradictions section). The IMO speaks of a "double blockade" (file 04 #39). Kardon argues the US has "lost command of the commons" (file 10 #15).
- **Timing 2.**
- **Mechanism 2:** the blockade and re-closure dates line up, and CENTCOM's escort counts are available.
- **Counterfactual 1.**
- **Overlap:** this factor partly overlaps with type of threat, which is a limitation.

**7. Oil market conditions and stocks (6).**
- **Change 1:** both periods began with surplus. The 1980s had a glut and the 1986 price collapse. The *CSM* (1987) cites "spare capacity and strategic reserves". In 2026 the market was oversupplied before the war (Brent $61 in January; Bordoff and O'Sullivan). But Gulf spare capacity was **trapped behind the strait**.
- **Timing 2.**
- **Mechanism 2:** IEA OMR data show world stocks fell about 250m barrels over March–April. Futures fell back to about $79 after July (file 10 #26).
- **Counterfactual 1.**
- **Note:** I counted the government stock release (IEA collective action) under cooperation, not here.

**8. Shale revolution (5).**
- **Change 2:** the US went from historic net importer to net exporter. 2020 was its first net-export year since at least 1949, and net exports were 1.64 mb/d in 2023 (EIA: https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=42340 ; https://eia.gov/energyexplained/oil-and-petroleum-products/imports-and-exports.php).
- **Timing 2.**
- **Mechanism 1:** Brookings says the US is "nonetheless exposed to the same price shock" and that its producers were "slow to increase production" (file 07 7c).
- **Counterfactual 0:** shale changed **who** is exposed, not the global price, so it does not explain ΔR.
- **Sign 0/±:** some argue lower exposure made Washington readier to risk war, but that is a claim with no data behind it.

**9. Connected crises (5).**
- **Change 2:** nothing comparable existed in the 1980s. In 2026, the Houthis declared a blockade of Saudi Arabia on 20 July, Red Sea premiums more than doubled, and drones from Iraqi territory hit the East–West pipeline (files 09, 13 #29, 06 ECFR).
- **Timing 1:** these came *after* the closure.
- **Mechanism 2:** the pipeline shutdown is documented.
- **Counterfactual 0:** R had already risen before these crises.

### Decision rule

- Multilateral cooperation: **5.7%**. That is **not** the largest share; it is the smallest. Under the rule, it is **a minor reason**.
- The largest share is type of threat at **15.1%**. It is only 1.14 times the next largest (13.2%), below the 1.5 threshold. So **no single factor is the main reason**. Type of threat is the leading reason among several, alongside trade concentration and the changed US role.

### Sensitivity tests

1. **Most generous scoring for cooperation** (Change 1, Timing 1, Mechanism 2, Counterfactual 2 = 6): share **10.7%** (6/56). Still not the largest, so still a minor reason.
2. **Dropping the two weakest alternatives** (shale and connected crises): cooperation **7.0%** (3/43), type of threat 18.6%. Same verdict.
3. **Doubling the weight of the price indicator** in S: Tanker War S = (2+5+2)/4 = 2.25, so R = 11.3. 2026 S = (5+4+8)/4 = 4.25, so R = 19.8. ΔR = **+8.5**. The rise gets bigger, and the conclusion is unchanged.
4. **Counting the US–Israel coalition as "cooperation":** cooperation would then carry a large **minus** sign, because that coalition's strikes started the crisis. That would make cooperation a cause of the *rise* in risk, which is still not the reduction the EPQ hypothesis expects.

## 4. Verdict

Between the Tanker War and 2026, the risk of disruption at Hormuz **rose**, from **R ≈ 13.3 (High)** to **R ≈ 20.2 (Very high)**. That is ΔR ≈ +6.9, or +52%. Nearly all of the rise came from **severity** (S: 2.67 → 4.33), not likelihood: attacks were frequent in both periods, but only in 2026 did the oil flow itself stop. Multilateral cooperation grew a little over the same period (C: 9 → 12), mostly through the IEA's record stock release, Resolution 2817, state-backed insurance and JMIC information sharing. Naval cooperation was no stronger than in 1987–88, and the UK–France mission had still not deployed after seven months. Risk rose despite this extra cooperation, so cooperation cannot be the main explanation for the change. It scores only **5.7%** of total causal weight, which makes it **a minor reason**. Its effect was mildly risk-*reducing*, through the IEA stocks, but too weak to offset the other factors. The change is best explained by **several factors together**: the **type of threat** (15.1%), meaning a state at war that *chose* to close the strait rather than two belligerents raiding each other's trade; **higher trade concentration** (13.2%), with about 20 mb/d plus Qatari LNG and no matching growth in bypasses; and the **changed US role** (13.2%), from neutral convoy protector in 1987 to belligerent and blockader in 2026. Pipelines and a pre-war oil surplus did more than cooperation to stop severity rising even further.

## Strongest counter-argument

**"The test is unfair to cooperation, because it compares very different threats. And in 2026, cooperation was the one thing that actually worked."** A defender of multilateralism could argue three things:
- In the 1980s, risk stayed lower **because** of the Earnest Will convoys and European minesweepers. Regional coverage says US-led escorts "kept oil flowing despite more than 450 attacks" (file 13).
- In 2026, Brent peaked at about $120 (futures) rather than the $150+ some forecasts expected, **because** 32 IEA states released over 400m barrels within 11 days.
- Without that release and the $40bn insurance facility, S could have hit 5 on every indicator.

On this reading, the counterfactual score for cooperation should be 1–2, not 0.

The response to this is that sources point elsewhere for both periods. In the 1980s, flows were never "seriously disrupted" mainly because **neither side tried to close the strait**, only about 2% of ships were hit, and most Saudi crude already bypassed Hormuz (*CSM*, 1987). The war also ended through Iran's exhaustion and Resolution 598, not through escorts (file 07 Debate 5). In 2026, Brent went above $100 the day after the IEA announcement. Bordoff and O'Sullivan explain the "far less severe" outcome by **pre-war oversupply and pipelines**. And the more generous scoring in sensitivity test 1 still leaves cooperation a minor reason. The counter-argument is best used to show that cooperation **reduced severity at the margin** (a + sign) even though it did not drive the overall change. That is a nuanced conclusion examiners reward.

## Limitations specific to this test

- The Tanker War has **no freight data** (n_L = 2). The 1980s trade-fall figures measure different things: 25% fewer ships against −45% oil flow, which was partly market-driven.
- The 2026 crisis is **ongoing**. Duration may go up to 5, and incident totals are not final.
- The process-tracing scores are judgements. Type of threat and US role overlap, and so do market conditions and IEA stocks.
- Almost all evidence comes from search snippets. Verify it before citing.
