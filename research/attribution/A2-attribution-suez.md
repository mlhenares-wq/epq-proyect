# A2: Attribution, Suez Canal / Red Sea (1967–75 closure vs 2023–26 Houthi crisis)

**Question:** Was multilateral cooperation the MAIN reason disruption risk on the Suez / Red Sea route changed between the 1967–75 closure and the 2023–26 Houthi crisis?

**Method:** Chokepoint Disruption Risk Index (`research/risk-equation.md`): R = L × S, cooperation index C (0–20), Cause Contribution Score (0–8 per cause) and the 1.5× decision rule.

**Short answer:** No. Using the rubric, **risk did not change in a measurable way.** The central estimate is R ≈ 14.7 (1967–75) and R ≈ 14.4 (2023–26), so ΔR ≈ −0.3. The ranges overlap almost completely. Cooperation rose sharply (C went from 5 to 13), yet risk stayed in the "High" band. Part of the risk profile did change: severity fell a little because trade was partly diverted rather than totally cut. That change is best explained by the **type of threat** and by **connected crises** (Gaza, Hormuz 2026). Multilateral cooperation comes **sixth of eight** causes with an 8.5% share, which makes it **a minor reason**.

**What this means:** ΔR is too small to measure. Its sign flips depending on which defensible scoring choice you make. Both central values fall in the **High** band. The only component that changes in the same direction under every choice is **severity, which falls a little** (trade 5 → 3/4). Likelihood cannot be compared reliably, because the 1967 measures do not fit a closure. It looks higher in 2023–26 mainly because the risk is now *probabilistic*: ships can choose to sail, and get attacked or pay premiums. In 1967 they simply could not sail.

**The UN's role, in summary:** the UN supplied the *framework* (242, 338) and the *peacekeeping conditions* (UNTSO, then the UNEF II buffer). It did **not** clear or reopen the canal. The clearance was a US-led ad hoc coalition (US, UK, France, with Egypt), made possible by US bilateral diplomacy (Kissinger's disengagement deal) and by Sadat's own decision. Time (June 1975) quotes a UN estimate that the closure cost the world about $10bn (https://time.com/archive/6846859/the-world-suez-the-seas-rejoined/).

### Evidence for each score

**1. Multilateral cooperation (4/8)**
- *Change 2:* C rose from 5 to 13 (section 2).
- *Timing 1:* the cooperation was **reactive**. OPG came 29 days *after* the first attack, and Maersk had already paused on 15 Dec 2023 (09 R1). Aspides came 3 months later. Risk was already set before cooperation arrived.
- *Mechanism 1:* the claim that cooperation lowered risk has only partial data behind it. There are Aspides escort counts, and IISS/Forces News say OPG "successfully prevented the loss of civilian lives" but "has not deterred Houthi aggression or restored pre-2023 levels of maritime traffic" (https://www.forcesnews.com/middle-east/red-sea-trade-declines-houthis-grow-strength-despite-western-airstrikes, 07 4a). There is **no data showing that premiums, freight or transits fell because of the naval missions.** Data actually shows the reverse in one case: premiums **tripled** (0.3% → 1%) right after the US/UK strikes of 11–12 Jan 2024 (Lloyd's List, 09 R2).
- *Counterfactual 0:* Tallis (2025, *War on the Rocks*, https://warontherocks.com/2025/04/how-the-biden-administration-won-tactically-but-failed-strategically-in-the-red-sea/) says OPG "won tactically but failed strategically". Transits were still 60% down 100 days after the *last* attack (09 R10), so the trade loss would have been the same without the missions. Atlantic Council and Energy Intelligence warned the strikes risked an escalation cycle (07 4b); see also *Foreign Affairs*, 'How Washington Emboldened the Houthis' (file 10). **Fair counter-point:** a Reuters-sourced report says Western and Israeli strikes "significantly limited" attacks (07, Debate 4). That supports giving 1 instead of 0 (total 5; see sensitivity).
- *1967 note:* the 1974 clearance **was** necessary to *end* Period 1. It does not explain why risk in 2023–26 differs from 1967–75. It also only became possible after the political settlement (1973 war → Jan 1974 disengagement). Cooperation followed politics; it did not cause the reopening on its own.
- **Sign ±:** lowered severity (lives saved; only about 4 ships sunk out of ~100 attacked) and raised likelihood (premium spike after strikes; possible escalation).

**2. Type of threat (8/8)**
- *Change 2:* in 1967 a sovereign state closed the canal with scuttled ships and mines (Time 1967, file 12 #2). In 2023–26 a non-state actor used selective missile, drone and USV attacks aimed at Israel-, US- or UK-linked (and in 2026 Saudi-linked) ships (Saba, file 14 Y1; AP 2026, file 05 #31).
- *Timing 2:* the type of threat was set from day one of each crisis.
- *Mechanism 2:* product tankers fell only 19% against −86% for container ships (09 R10). Chinese operators and car carriers returned (09 R20). China negotiated passage directly (14 S4). Ships "go dark" to transit (09 R18). This explains why trade loss was 50–76% and not 100%.
- *Counterfactual 2:* a state closure gives 100% trade loss whatever navies do (1967–75 shows this). A selective non-state threat lets traffic decide ship by ship. The textbook distinction is inter-state conflict vs terrorism vs "blue crime" (06, maritime security text; Cogent 2025, file 03 §3.11).

**3. Connected crises (8/8)**
- *Change 2 / Timing 2:* the Gaza war (Oct 2023) came before the attacks. The Houthis framed the campaign as solidarity with Gaza (14 Y1–Y2).
- *Mechanism 2:* the *Galaxy Leader* crew were released after the 19 Jan 2025 Gaza ceasefire (05 #9). ACLED counted **84% fewer attacks in 2025** (06 #13). Attacks paused after the 10 Oct 2025 ceasefire (07 4c). The **Hormuz 2026** closure made Saudi Arabia reroute oil through Yanbu and the Red Sea, which led to the Houthi blockade on Saudi Arabia (20 July 2026) and premiums of 0.3% → 0.75%, up to 3–7% for Saudi-linked ships (09 R11, R19; CSIS 2026, file 06 #9). In P1, the 1973 war both prolonged the closure and led to it ending (Time 1973, 12 #3).
- *Counterfactual 2:* file 07, Debate 4: "the Houthis' on/off switch was Gaza, not naval power".

**4. Technology (7/8)**
- The closure "undoubtedly" stimulated supertanker building (US Minerals Yearbook 1968, WS link above). By 1975 VLCCs were too big for the canal: the 1976 draught limit was 38 ft (WS: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-navigation/article/navigation-in-the-gulf-of-suez/5F3BD561F0F0C4C0C436221A3DB9E6F9). The *Spectator* called the 1975 reopening "one of the more significant anti-climaxes of our time" (https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/7th-june-1975/11/suez-canal, files 05, 12).
- In 2023–26, cheap drones, ballistic missiles and USVs (the *Tutor* was sunk by the first drone-boat strike, 05 #26) gave a non-state actor the reach to threaten shipping. That raised L.
- *Counterfactual 1:* technology matters, but it works through the type of threat and the Cape option.

**5. Supply chain design (7/8)**
- Carriers added ships to keep port-call frequency on the Cape route (Yap & Yang 2024). Transport-chain costs rose only 3–13% (Bedoya-Maya et al. 2025, *JTG* 128, 104304). Only 0.6% of the container fleet was idle by the end of 2024, because spare ships absorbed the diversion (Alphaliner via Lloyd's List, 07 7d). The ECB estimated at most +0.25 pp on inflation (06 #23). Kiel: no greater negative world effect expected by Feb 2024 (11 #24).
- *Counterfactual 1:* this kept the *price* score low, but price scored 1 in both periods, so its effect on ΔR is limited.

**6. Physical alternatives (6/8)**
- The Cape was the alternative in **both** periods, so it changed only a little (change 1). New since P1: **SUMED** (opened 1977, so not available in 1967–75; WS: https://www.sumed.org/?p=history), now used for Saudi crude (Lloyd's List Intelligence, Aug 2026, file 05 #35). Cape arrivals were up 89% (UNCTAD RMT 2024) and Cape traffic up ~74% (IMF, 06 #24).
- *Counterfactual 1:* the Cape route explains why goods still arrived, but it was also available in 1967.

**7. Trade concentration / cargo mix (4/8)**
- In 1966 the canal carried about 15% of world seaborne trade, oil-heavy (60% of Italy's oil; WS Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_of_the_Suez_Canal_(1967%E2%80%931975)). Today it carries mostly containers, and severity depends on *what* passes (06, key point 4). Weak causal evidence on its own.

**8. 2025 US–Houthi truce (3/8)**
- Oman brokered it and Trump announced it on 6 May 2025 (CBS, https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/trump-houthis-ceasefire-bombing-over-capitulated). The Houthis said it "does not include Israel in any way" (Reuters/Malay Mail, 05 #28). Two months later *Magic Seas* and *Eternity C* were sunk (Bloomberg, 05 #29). The truce was **bilateral, not multilateral**, and protected only US ships. It must **not** be counted as multilateral cooperation.

### Decision rule

- Largest shares: **Type of threat 17.0%** and **Connected crises 17.0%** (tied).
- Multilateral cooperation: **8.5%**, joint sixth of eight. It is **not the largest** and is half the size of the leaders.
- **Verdict under the rule: multilateral cooperation was a MINOR reason.**

**Sensitivity tests**
- Cooperation counterfactual 1 instead of 0 (accepting the "significantly limited" claim): 5/48 = **10.4%**. Still minor.
- Cooperation given the maximum 8/8, which the evidence does not support: 8/51 = 15.7%, tied with threat and connected crises (15.7%). Even then it is not 1.5× the next largest, so at most a "major reason among several". **The conclusion holds under every scoring choice.**
- Dropping the two weakest causes (7 and 8): cooperation 4/40 = 10%. Still minor.

## 4. Verdict and strongest counter-argument

**Verdict.** Multilateral cooperation was **not** the main reason disruption risk changed on the Suez / Red Sea route. The CDRI shows risk **barely changed at all**: R ≈ 14.7 in 1967–75 and ≈ 14.4 in 2023–26, both "High", with ranges (7.3–22.5 and 12.0–17.1) that overlap so much that the sign of ΔR depends on scoring choices. Meanwhile the Cooperation Index rose from 5 to 13. If much more cooperation coincides with almost unchanged risk, cooperation cannot be the main driver. What changed was the risk's *shape*. In 1967 disruption was certain and total, because a state shut the canal for eight years. In 2023–26 disruption was likely but partial, because a non-state actor with cheap missiles and drones attacked selected ships and switched its campaign on and off with the Gaza war and later the Hormuz crisis. Process tracing gives the largest shares to the **type of threat (17%)** and **connected crises (17%)**, with technology, supply-chain design and the Cape/SUMED alternatives behind them. Cooperation scores **8.5%**, a minor reason, and its sign is mixed. Escorts and air defence limited deaths and sinkings. But the January 2024 strikes were followed by a tripling of war-risk premiums, and traffic stayed about 60% down even 100 days after attacks stopped. The 2025 US–Houthi truce, often cited as a success, was bilateral and protected only US ships.

**Strongest counter-argument.** The rubric may undercount what cooperation achieved. First, the index measures trade diverted, not lives and ships saved. Of about 100 ships attacked, only about four sank, and Western navies destroyed hundreds of missiles and drones; without OPG and Aspides, severity in terms of deaths, sinkings and oil spills could have been far higher. Second, in Period 1 a multinational operation (US, UK and French minesweeping, plus training and salvage for Egypt, under a UN-supervised disengagement) was literally what **ended** the 1967 closure, so cooperation was decisive for *severity* there. Third, the duration score is capped at 5, so the rubric cannot show that 3 years (and counting) is much shorter than 8. The answer: these points are real, and you should discuss them. But the 1974 clearance only happened after Egypt's political decision following the 1973 war (cooperation followed politics). And in 2023–26 the sources agree that shipping lines' behaviour followed Gaza ceasefires and insurance prices, not naval presence (Tallis 2025; BIMCO; ACLED). So cooperation reduced the *harm per attack* more than it reduced the *likelihood or scale* of disruption.

**Limitations to state**
1. The 1967 likelihood indicators do not fit a full state closure. L₁ rests on one freight figure (n = 1), and that figure is second-hand (NYT 1967 via a blog). There are no 1967 premium or incident data.
2. No 1967 oil-price figure was verified in the files. The posted-price point comes from general knowledge.
3. Leaving out connected-crisis price shocks (1973 embargo, 2026 Hormuz) is a choice. Including the 1973 embargo raises S₁ to 5.0 and R₁ to 20.0 (central L), so ΔR would be about −5.6. Risk would then look *lower* in 2023–26, but cooperation's share of the cause table, and so the verdict, would not change.
4. Ceiling effects: duration and trade reduction both hit band 5, so real differences are hidden.
5. Period 2 is still going on (Oct 2026), so its values may rise.
