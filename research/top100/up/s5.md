## Theory, method and cross-case (86–100)

These 15 give the essay its theoretical frame, justify the risk equation and put all three straits on one scale. All are graded A1, A2 or B1.

86. **Marin-Ferrer, M., Vernaccini, L. and Poljansek, K. (2017). Index for Risk Management INFORM: Concept and Methodology, Version 2017.** EU Publications Office (EUR 28655 EN). [link](https://doi.org/10.2760/094023) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official methodology report

     Summary and analysis: This is the blueprint for the EPQ's equation. INFORM combines three dimensions, Hazard & Exposure, Vulnerability and Lack of Coping Capacity, by multiplying them and raising each to the power 1/3, a geometric mean. It keeps coping capacity separate because international actors can change it. The student's R = (L × S × K)^(1/3) copies this structure directly, with multilateral cooperation treated as institutional coping capacity (K = 5 − C/5). Cite it at the start of the methodology chapter to justify both the form of the equation and the decision to give cooperation its own term. Pair it with ISO 31000 for the likelihood × severity core, OECD/JRC (2008) for normalisation and sensitivity testing, and Cox (2008a) for the limits of scoring.

     Reliability: The official methodology of the EU Joint Research Centre, so it is authoritative for index construction, though it was designed for humanitarian and disaster risk rather than maritime security.

87. **Bueger, C. and Edmunds, T. (2017). Beyond seablindness: a new agenda for maritime security studies.** International Affairs 93(6). [link](https://academic.oup.com/ia/article/93/6/1293/4111108) — **Grade A1** · Type: Journal article (theory)

     Summary and analysis: Bueger and Edmunds argue that politicians and scholars long neglected the sea ("seablindness") and set out a research agenda drawn from counter-piracy, stressing information sharing, capacity-building and networked governance. This gives the literature review its core vocabulary and a theoretical reason to expect cooperation to lower risk, which is the hypothesis the EPQ tests. The student should use it to frame the five cooperation factors (frameworks, joint operations, information sharing, response speed, enforcement) as recognised mechanisms, then test them against the three cases. Its optimism should be weighed against Shortland and Vothknecht (2011), who find displacement, and refined with Bueger and Edmunds (2024), whose threat-type split explains why the agenda fits Malacca piracy better than Iran or the Houthis.

     Reliability: Copenhagen and Bristol academics in a top, highly cited international relations journal, but it is theory rather than data and was written before 2023.

88. **Verschuur, J., Lumma, J. and Hall, J.W. (2025). Systemic impacts of disruptions at maritime chokepoints.** Nature Communications 16. [link](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-65403-w) — **Grade A1** · Type: Journal article (quantitative)

     Summary and analysis: This model estimates that about US$192 billion of trade a year is at risk at 24 chokepoints, with roughly US$10.7 billion a year in losses plus US$3.4 billion in extra freight, counting delays, rerouting, insurance and freight costs. It is the only peer-reviewed source that puts Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb/Suez and Malacca on one scale, so it suits the introduction and the cross-case comparison. It also justifies a key method choice: because it treats freight costs as losses, the student moved freight rates from L to S. Use it alongside EIA (exposure) and UNCTAD (2024) (observed Red Sea losses), and stress that its figures are expected values, not measured outcomes.

     Reliability: An Oxford and TU Delft team in a top open-access journal, confirmed by several sources, though the US$192 billion is a modelled expected value produced before the 2026 Hormuz closure.

89. **George, A.L. and Bennett, A. (2005). Case Studies and Theory Development in the Social Sciences.** MIT Press. [link](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=George+Bennett+2005+Case+Studies+and+Theory+Development+in+the+Social+Sciences) — **Grade A2** · Type: Academic methods book

     Summary and analysis: The standard guide to structured, focused comparison and process tracing, the method for tracing whether a proposed cause really produced an outcome in a small number of cases. The equation shows how much risk changed; this book justifies how the EPQ tests why. It underpins the Cause Contribution Score (change, timing, mechanism and counterfactual tests) used to weigh cooperation against alternatives such as rerouting, threat type and connected crises, and feeds the "total cooperation share" that links process tracing to the log decomposition of R. Cite it in the methodology chapter beside Marin-Ferrer et al. (2017), and return to it in the evaluation, since process-tracing scores remain qualitative judgements.

     Reliability: A leading academic methods book from a major university press, but it was carried over from an earlier file without a fresh check.

90. **ISO (2018). ISO 31000:2018 Risk management — Guidelines.** International Organization for Standardization. [link](https://www.iso.org/standard/65694.html) — **Grade A1** · Type: International standard

     Summary and analysis: ISO 31000 defines risk as the "effect of uncertainty on objectives" and says it is usually expressed through events, their likelihood and their consequences. It is the internationally recognised justification for the L × S core of the equation and for the student's first, simpler version (R = L × S on a 1–5 grid). Use it early in the methodology to show that the two main dimensions follow an accepted standard, then explain that, because the standard is silent on cooperation, the EPQ borrows INFORM's coping-capacity dimension (Marin-Ferrer et al., 2017) to add K. Cox (2008a) then supplies the warning that simple likelihood × consequence scoring can mislead.

     Reliability: The official global risk-management standard, though it says nothing about cooperation.

91. **Shortland, A. and Vothknecht, M. (2011). Combating "maritime terrorism" off the coast of Somalia.** European Journal of Political Economy 27(S1). [link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejpoleco.2011.03.004) — **Grade A2** · Type: Journal article (mixed methods; counter-argument)

     Summary and analysis: Using statistics and interviews, the authors judge the 2008–10 multinational naval mission in the Gulf of Aden, including EU Atalanta, only a partial success: attacks stayed high and were pushed out of the guarded transit corridor into the open Indian Ocean. This is the EPQ's strongest peer-reviewed counter-argument, because cooperation may move a threat rather than remove it, lowering L locally while risk reappears elsewhere. Use it in the evaluation and in the Red Sea case to qualify claims about joint operations, and link it to the NRC and Cox point that adaptive adversaries break simple multiplication. Contrast it with Bueger and Edmunds (2017) and the 2024 Atalanta decision.

     Reliability: Peer-reviewed work by DIW Berlin and King's College London economists that mixes statistics with interviews, though it was written before piracy collapsed in 2012, so the long-run effect is missing.

92. **UNCTAD (2024). Review of Maritime Transport 2024: Navigating Maritime Chokepoints.** United Nations. [link](https://unctad.org/publication/review-maritime-transport-2024) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official UN report

     Summary and analysis: The whole 2024 edition is about chokepoints. By mid-2024, tonnage crossing the Gulf of Aden was down 76% and through Suez down 70%, while Cape of Good Hope arrivals rose 89%; small island states and the least developed countries suffered most from higher freight costs. These are ready-made figures for the trade-reduction indicator in S for the Suez/Red Sea case, and they show rerouting as a physical alternative to cooperation. Use it with PortWatch-based estimates and Verschuur et al. (2025) to cross-check severity, and with EIA for Hormuz and Malacca exposure. Note its developing-country lens when comparing who bears the costs.

     Reliability: An official, free UN report whose figures match IMF and Clarksons data, though its mandate leads it to stress costs to poorer states and its mid-2024 data miss 2025–26.

93. **Bueger, C. and Edmunds, T. (2024). Understanding Maritime Security.** Oxford University Press. [link](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/understanding-maritime-security/) — **Grade A2** · Type: Academic book (theory)

     Summary and analysis: This short textbook sorts maritime threats into inter-state conflict, terrorism and "blue crime" such as piracy and smuggling, and explains how states cooperate against each type. That split is the EPQ's most useful explanatory tool: it suggests why cooperation (ReCAAP, joint patrols) worked against criminal piracy in Malacca but struggles against a state such as Iran or a state-backed actor such as the Houthis. Use it in the literature review and the comparative conclusion, and score "type of threat" as an alternative factor in process tracing. It updates Bueger and Edmunds (2017) and complements Percy and Shortland's land-based view of piracy.

     Reliability: Leading maritime-security scholars with a top academic publisher, but it offers theory rather than case data and was written before 2026.

94. **Cox, L.A. (2008a). What's wrong with risk matrices?** Risk Analysis 28(2). [link](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.2008.01030.x) — **Grade A1** · Type: Journal article (methodological critique)

     Summary and analysis: Cox shows that risk matrices have poor resolution, giving very different risks the same score, and that subjective inputs let users rate the same risk in opposite ways; he says matrices should be used only with clear explanation of the judgements built into them. For this EPQ, a plain 1–5 × 1–5 L × S grid gives only 14 possible values. This is the key critique for the evaluation chapter. The student should show how averaging several indicators per dimension and using a geometric mean (Marin-Ferrer et al., 2017) reduce, but do not remove, the problem, and should use R to rank and compare, always showing indicator scores. Read with ISO 31000 and OECD/JRC (2008).

     Reliability: A highly cited peer-reviewed paper in the leading risk-analysis journal.

95. **OECD/JRC (Nardo, M. et al.) (2008). Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators: Methodology and User Guide.** OECD Publishing. [link](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Handbook+on+Constructing+Composite+Indicators+OECD+2008) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official methodology handbook

     Summary and analysis: The standard step-by-step guide to building an index: theoretical framework, data selection, missing data, normalisation, weighting, aggregation, and uncertainty and sensitivity analysis. It notes that a geometric mean is less compensatory than an average and treats equal weights as a defensible default only if tested. This supports three of the EPQ's design choices: banded 1–5 scores (categorical normalisation), equal exponents of 1/3, and the eight-test sensitivity procedure with its "robust in 6 of 8" rule. Cite it in the methodology to show the index was built to recognised standards, and in the evaluation when reporting sensitivity results. It turns INFORM's structure into a testable procedure.

     Reliability: An official, widely used OECD and EU handbook, although the link recorded is a search rather than a direct URL.

96. **United Nations (1982). UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), Part III, Articles 34–45 (transit passage).** UN Division for Ocean Affairs. [link](https://www.un.org/Depts/los/convention_agreements/texts/unclos/part3.htm) — **Grade A1** · Type: Treaty (primary legal text)

     Summary and analysis: Part III sets the legal regime for international straits, including the right of transit passage that coastal states may not suspend, and Article 43, which says user and coastal states "should by agreement co-operate" on navigation and safety. It is the shared legal baseline for all three cases and scores the "formal frameworks" factor in C. Use it to contrast cases: Malacca's coastal states operate within it, while Iran signed but never ratified it and in 2026 asserted its own strait authority and tolls. Article 43 is directly on the EPQ's theme, but pair it with evidence of compliance, since law on paper is not enforcement.

     Reliability: The primary treaty text, though Iran signed but never ratified it and a legal text does not prove compliance.

97. **EIA (2024, updated 2026). World Oil Transit Chokepoints.** US Energy Information Administration. [link](https://www.eia.gov/international/analysis/special-topics/World_Oil_Transit_Chokepoints) — **Grade A1** · Type: Official data report

     Summary and analysis: The standard reference for oil volumes through each chokepoint. Malacca carries the most oil (about 23.7 million barrels a day in 2023), Hormuz about 21 million, and Hormuz has very limited bypass pipelines while Malacca is the easiest to route around. This gives the EPQ its measure of structural exposure and physical alternatives, which the methodology holds fixed within each strait but must discuss when comparing between straits. Use it in the case introductions and as a possible optional exposure score in S. Combine it with Verschuur et al. (2025) for trade value and UNCTAD (2024) for observed Red Sea disruption.

     Reliability: Official, free US government data, though it relies on a commercial tanker-tracking firm and its core figures are 2023 data from before the 2026 war.

98. **Council of the European Union (2024). Council Decision (CFSP) 2024/3186 amending Decision 2008/851/CFSP (Operation Atalanta).** EUR-Lex. [link](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dec/2024/3186/oj) — **Grade A1** · Type: Primary EU legal document

     Summary and analysis: The decision extends the Spanish-led Operation Atalanta, headquartered at Rota, to 28 February 2027, widens its area to parts of the Red Sea, calls for "synergies" with the defensive Aspides mission, and renames MSC Horn of Africa as MSC Indian Ocean. It is direct evidence of an EU formal framework and information-sharing arrangement for the Suez/Red Sea case, and the clearest European and Spanish angle among the cross-case sources. Use it to score the frameworks and information-sharing factors of C, and pair it with EMAD for Spain's actual contribution and Shortland and Vothknecht (2011) for doubts about results.

     Reliability: A primary, free EU legal document, though it is only the formal text and says nothing about the mission's results.

99. **Bueger, C. (2015). What is maritime security?** Marine Policy 53. [link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpol.2014.12.005) — **Grade A1** · Type: Journal article (concept)

     Summary and analysis: Bueger argues that "maritime security" is a buzzword with no agreed meaning. That vagueness lets many actors coordinate without agreeing on everything, but it can hide political disagreement. This is the best source for the definitions section: the student should define "maritime security", "multilateral cooperation" and "disruption" precisely before scoring them. It also helps explain why formal frameworks can look strong on paper but rest on thin consensus, as with the 2026 Hormuz negotiations. Read it before Bueger and Edmunds (2017, 2024), which build on this conceptual groundwork, and use it to explain why the EPQ needs explicit scoring rubrics.

     Reliability: One of the most cited papers in the field and free to read, though it is conceptual and offers no data.

100. **Estado Mayor de la Defensa (2025). Operación Atalanta news pages.** EMAD, Spanish Ministry of Defence. [link](https://emad.defensa.gob.es/en/operaciones/operaciones-en-el-exterior/42-ATALANTA/Noticias/listado/250425-atalanta-visita-delegacion-europea-somali-fragata.html) — **Grade B1** · Type: Official government web pages (primary)

     Summary and analysis: These Spanish Defence Staff pages record that Atalanta's operational headquarters is at Rota (moved from Northwood after Brexit), that its mandate runs to February 2027, and that Spain contributes about 350 personnel, a frigate with a helicopter, a special operations team and a maritime patrol aircraft in Djibouti. Spain describes itself as the only EU state to contribute without a break since 2008. This gives the EPQ concrete evidence of Spain's role in European cooperation and supports scoring joint operations for the Red Sea case. Use it with the 2024 Council Decision, and treat its claims of success as self-presentation to be tested against independent data.

     Reliability: An official, primary record of what Spain did, but it is self-promoting and the "uninterrupted contributor" claim is self-reported.
