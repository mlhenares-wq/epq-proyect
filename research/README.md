# EPQ research: multilateral cooperation and maritime chokepoint disruption

**Question:** How far does multilateral cooperation reduce the likelihood and severity of disruption at maritime chokepoints?
**Case studies:** Strait of Hormuz (Tanker War vs 2026), Suez/Red Sea (1967–75 vs 2023–26), Strait of Malacca (piracy peak vs present).

## Headline findings

| Case | Risk, period 1 → period 2 (CCRI, 1–5) | Cooperation C (0–20) | Was cooperation the main reason risk changed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hormuz | 3.49 → 3.75 (rose) | 9 → 12 | **No: minor reason** (5.7% of causal weight). Iran's decision, trade concentration and the US role mattered more |
| Suez / Red Sea | 4.22 → 3.30 (fell) | 5 → 13 | **No: minor reason** (8.5%, partly negative). Type of threat and connected crises mattered more |
| Malacca | 2.37 → 1.53 (fell) | 6 → 13 | **Major reason among several** (15.8–23.7%), alongside the 2004 tsunami, the Aceh peace deal and national enforcement. It becomes the main reason only if national patrols count as cooperation |

**Overall:** cooperation softens the severity of disruption, but it was not the main driver of risk in any case. How well it works depends on the **type of threat**: it is most effective against small-scale crime and least effective against states and armed groups.

## Important limitations

1. **The research environment could not open web pages.** Every source was verified as existing through search results, but summaries and figures come from search snippets. **Open each source and check every figure before citing it.** Items marked [BK] or "to check" need particular care.
2. Some outlets (NYT, Guardian, BBC, FT, El País) were blocked from search, so other papers from the same countries stand in for them.
3. The 2026 crises are still going on.
