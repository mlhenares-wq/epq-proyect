# P03–11 – Provenance and reliability: peer-reviewed sources (files 03 and 11)

Assessed 20 October 2026 against `research/provenance-rubric.md` (Admiralty Code A–F / 1–6, OPVL, usefulness 1–5).
Files assessed: `sources/03-peer-reviewed.md` (43 items) and `sources/11-peer-reviewed-econ-law.md` (44 items).

## How the grades were applied

- **Checking method.** 15 web searches were used to check authors, journals and peer-review status for the least-verified items (results listed under "Verification log" at the end). Full texts were not opened (page fetching was blocked). As the rubric's project note says, credibility is therefore **capped at 2** unless several independent sources confirm the content or the item is itself widely-cited original data.
- **Source reliability (letter), as applied to academic work:**
  - **A** = an established journal with external peer review (e.g. *Nature Communications*, *Marine Policy*, *International Affairs*, *JTG*, *ODIL*), or an official intergovernmental body (UNCTAD, OECD/ITF, World Bank).
  - **B** = editorially reviewed professional journals (*Naval War College Review*, *Studies in Intelligence*, *RUSI Journal*), editor-written journal sections (AJIL Contemporary Practice, a journal editorial), high-volume "soundness-only" open-access journals (*Heliyon*, MDPI, *Frontiers*, *Cogent*, *Research in Globalization*), central-bank and top think-tank papers (Dallas Fed, ECB, Kiel, CEPII, CEPR/VoxEU, OIES, ASIL, NIDS, ELIAMEP).
  - **C** = expert blogs by named academics, NGOs, small national journals, and unrefereed working-paper archives.
  - **D** = low-tier open-access outlet whose peer review cannot be relied on.
  - **F** = the item itself could not be confirmed to exist as described.
- **Information credibility (number):** 1 = confirmed or widely-cited original data; 2 = abstract or key claim confirmed in search results; 3 = plausible but only the compiler's snippet, or authors/details unconfirmed; 4 = the file's summary conflicts with what was found; 6 = only a title or bibliographic record was seen, so the content cannot be judged.
- **Peer-reviewed?** Y = externally refereed article; N = not refereed (editorial, professional journal, blog, report); WP = working paper or policy brief.
- **Duplicates across the two files** are graded once in each table and marked "(dup)".

### OPVL notes – File 03

**1.1 Richer (2024).** *O:* Christian Richer (name confirmed by search), in a long-running Canadian peer-reviewed OA journal (Centre of Military and Strategic Studies, Calgary); secondary, written 36 years after the events. *P:* to explain why the US misjudged escalation with Iran in a "gray zone" conflict. *V:* the clearest academic treatment of the Tanker War escalation risk. *L:* the file calls it "American and Iranian perspectives", but the search shows a US-centred framing built on US officials' quotes; mid-prestige journal. Fix the citation (author and title are now known).

**1.2 Mobley (2021).** *O:* a former US intelligence analyst, published by the US Naval War College; based on declassified British archives. *P:* professional education for US naval officers. *V:* primary archival evidence that the 1987 coalition was not united. *L:* editorial review, not double-blind; a navy-affiliated outlet, so there is institutional bias towards US naval policy.

**1.3 Mobley (2016).** *O:* same author, in the CIA's in-house journal. *P:* institutional history and lessons for intelligence officers. *V:* the only source here on information-sharing during Earnest Will. *L:* an official government publication that passed CIA review; it is pro-US and self-congratulatory, and the file used extracts only.

**1.4 Lott & Kawagishi (2022).** *O:* authors confirmed: Alexander Lott (Norwegian Centre for the Law of the Sea, UiT Tromsø) and Shin Kawagishi, *ODIL* 53(2–3): 123–146; open access via UiT Munin. *P:* legal analysis of the 2019 tanker incidents. *V:* high-quality specialist law journal; it links transit passage to grey-zone attacks. *L:* written before 2026; Lott also writes item 11-#34, so the two are not independent. Its "insufficient evidence" verdict on Iranian responsibility is contested by US/UK government attributions.

**1.5 Shepard & Pratson (2020).** *O:* Duke University (Nicholas School) authors in *Energy Policy*, a leading Elsevier energy journal; the DOI was confirmed via RePEc. *P:* to model piracy as a "soft restriction" on Gulf exports. *V:* explains why exporters are unequally exposed. *L:* a model, not observed data; piracy in Hormuz is rare, so the threat type differs from 2026 state action.

**1.6 JTS (2012).** *O:* unknown authors, in a Springer peer-reviewed security journal (mid-tier). *P:* threat assessment. *V:* background on non-state threats. *L:* only the title was seen; 14 years old; content cannot be judged.

(2026).** *O:* the title and abstract were confirmed on TRID, but the author name was **not** confirmed; *JTS* 19(1). *P:* to frame Hormuz as corridor insecurity rather than an oil shock. *V:* a conceptual frame for likelihood plus severity. *L:* written during or just after the events, so outcomes are unknown; a framing paper with no clear data; the author is unverified.
