# Provenance and reliability rubric

Every source in the research files is assessed with the same two tools, so ratings can be compared.

## 1. The Admiralty Code (source reliability + information credibility)

The grading system used by NATO and UK intelligence (NATO AJP-2.1; also called the "Admiralty System"). It rates **who** produced the source separately from **what** it says.

### Source reliability (A–F): who produced it?

| Grade | Meaning | Typical examples in this EPQ |
|---|---|---|
| **A** Completely reliable | Peer-reviewed journal, official statistics agency, or primary legal document | *International Security*, *Marine Policy*, UNCTAD, IEA, IMF PortWatch, UNSC resolutions, IMB/ReCAAP data |
| **B** Usually reliable | Major international news agency or paper of record; top think tank; central bank working paper; official government statement (for what the government did, not its claims about enemies) | Reuters, AP, AFP, BBC, FT, Bloomberg, Lloyd's List, Chatham House, IISS, CSIS, BIS working papers, Real Instituto Elcano |
| **C** Fairly reliable | National or regional newspaper with editorial standards; trade press; specialist blogs by named experts | Arab News, The National, The Straits Times, Jakarta Post, gCaptain, Splash247, War on the Rocks, The Conversation |
| **D** Not usually reliable | State-controlled media on topics touching their government; partisan outlets; aggregators and reposts with no original reporting | Press TV, Tasnim, Global Times, TASS, RT, Saba (Houthi), Hellenic Shipping News reposts |
| **E** Unreliable | Anonymous sites, content farms, social media posts with no sourcing | — |
| **F** Cannot be judged | Not enough information about the producer | Pages that could not be checked |

### Information credibility (1–6): is the content confirmed?

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **1** Confirmed | Confirmed by other independent sources, or is itself the original data |
| **2** Probably true | Consistent with other sources, not independently confirmed |
| **3** Possibly true | Plausible, little corroboration |
| **4** Doubtful | Conflicts with other sources |
| **5** Improbable | Contradicted by better sources |
| **6** Cannot be judged | Only seen as a search snippet, figure not visible |

A source is written as e.g. **B2** (usually reliable producer, probably true content).

> **Note for this project:** most sources were only seen in search results because the research environment could not open web pages. Where an agent could not confirm a figure, credibility is capped at **2** unless several independent sources give the same figure.

## 2. OPVL notes (origin, purpose, value, limitations)

The standard history-source analysis, which examiners expect to see in an EPQ.

| Heading | Question |
|---|---|
| **Origin** | Who made it, when, where? Primary (from the time/participants) or secondary? |
| **Purpose** | Why was it made? To inform, persuade, sell, justify a policy, propaganda? Who is the audience? |
| **Value** | What does it uniquely offer this EPQ? Which case study, factor or measure? |
| **Limitations** | Bias, gaps, date (too early to know outcomes?), paywall, only a snippet seen, translation |

## 3. Usefulness score (1–5)

How much the source helps answer *this* EPQ question (not how good it is in general).

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 5 | Essential: hard data or a key argument for a case study |
| 4 | Strong supporting evidence |
| 3 | Useful context or one figure |
| 2 | Marginal |
| 1 | Not useful; drop it |

## How to use this in the EPQ

- Prefer **A1–B2** sources for figures in your risk equation.
- Use **C** sources for national perspectives and local detail.
- Use **D** sources only as evidence of what a government *claims* or how it frames events (for example, Iranian state media's framing of the closure), never as evidence of facts.
- When two sources disagree, cite the higher-graded one and mention the disagreement.
