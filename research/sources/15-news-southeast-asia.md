# 15. News from Southeast Asia: Strait of Malacca and Singapore Strait

Case study 3 (Malacca), with links to case studies 1 (Hormuz) and 2 (Red Sea).
Compiled 19 October 2026.

## How these sources were checked (please read first)

- **Every article below appeared in live web search results** with the headline and URL shown. I did not make up any titles or links.
- **I could not open the full pages.** The page-fetching tool could not reach any news site during this session (DNS / proxy block). So each summary is based on the search-engine excerpt of the article, not the full text. Before quoting a figure in the EPQ, open the link and check it.
- **The Straits Times, CNA, Today and The Business Times block the search tool** for their current websites. For Singapore I used three workarounds instead:
  1. the National Library Board's **NewspaperSG archive** (eresources.nlb.gov.sg), which holds scanned Straits Times / Business Times / Today pages from 2004–2006. These links go to the **whole day's newspaper issue**, not to a single article. The headline or quote given is what the search excerpt showed for that issue. Search the issue page for "Malacca" to find the article.
  2. Straits Times articles **republished by Asia News Network** (asianews.network) or by The Star;
  3. **Singapore government and ReCAAP ISC primary documents** (MINDEF, the National Archives of Singapore, ReCAAP press releases).
- **Dates:** where the search result gave no exact day, the date comes from the URL (for example `/2026/04/` = April 2026) and is marked "(from URL)". "Date not shown" means I could not confirm it.
- **The search budget ran out** before I could do the planned extra searches for the Philippines (Inquirer), Vietnam (VnExpress) and the Indonesian police arrests of 2025. Those sections are thinner, as noted.
- **Already in the student's plan:** Malay Mail, 26 Aug 2025 (link 34). It is not repeated below.

**Key to the "Supports" line:** FF = formal frameworks, JO = joint operations, IS = information sharing, RS = response speed, EL = enforcement of laws; INC = incident numbers, INS = insurance premiums / war risk, FR = freight rates, TR = trade reduction / traffic; ALT = alternative factors (connected crises, physical alternatives, type of threat, etc.).

## MALAYSIA

### M1. "Seaborne robbery cases in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore highest since 2007"
- **Outlet / country:** The Star (Asean+ section), Malaysia
- **Date:** 10 January 2026 (from URL)
- **URL:** https://www.thestar.com.my/aseanplus/aseanplus-news/2026/01/10/seaborne-robbery-cases-in-the-straits-of-malacca-and-singapore-highest-since-2007
- **Summary:** Reports ReCAAP's 2025 annual figures: 108 incidents in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore, the most since 2007 and 74% more than in 2024. ReCAAP said the rise was mainly petty theft, with nearly half the cases involving nothing stolen. It said the rise did not mean a bigger threat to shipping.
- **National perspective:** Malaysian readers are told the threat is low-level and mostly in the Singapore Strait, not in Malaysian waters.
- **Bias note:** The Asean+ section often reprints partner papers (often The Straits Times), so the original author may be Singaporean. It relies on one source (ReCAAP).
- **Supports:** INC (present day), IS (ReCAAP), type of threat (ALT).

### M2. "No pirates in Malaysian waters, only petty crooks, Dewan Rakyat told"
- **Outlet / country:** The Star, Malaysia
- **Date:** 28 October 2025 (from URL)
- **URL:** https://www.thestar.com.my/news/nation/2025/10/28/no-pirates-in-malaysian-waters-only-petty-crooks-dewan-rakyat-told
- **Summary:** Malaysia's Home Minister told Parliament that the country has no pirates in its waters, only small-scale thieves and smugglers. Under UNCLOS, "piracy" only happens on the high seas, so crimes inside national waters count as "armed robbery at sea". The government credited joint patrols and enforcement for keeping its waters safe.
- **National perspective:** A defensive, reputation-protecting line: the 2025 surge is presented as someone else's problem (mainly the Singapore Strait off Indonesia's Riau Islands).
- **Bias note:** A government statement in Parliament, reported without challenge. Shows how *definitions* can shape incident numbers.
- **Supports:** EL, JO, INC (definition problem), type of threat.

## Contradictions and flags for the student's plan

1. **Was the 2025 "surge" a real threat?** ReCAAP, The Star, FMT and MINDEF all say the 2025 rise (108 incidents, the most since 2007) was mainly petty theft, with no incidents in the most serious category, and that it does not threaten trade. Malaysia's minister even says there are "no pirates". So the 2025 incident count is not like the 1999–2005 peak, which included hijackings and kidnappings. Compare severity, not just numbers.
2. **The 2025 surge was almost all in the Singapore Strait** (107 of 108 incidents), off Indonesia's Riau Islands, not in the Malacca Strait proper.
3. **The 2025 fall was caused by enforcement (Indonesian arrests in July–August 2025 using ships' CCTV and IFC information), not by new frameworks.** This supports "enforcement of laws" and "information sharing" as the factors that worked.
4. **Hormuz did not simply boost Malacca traffic.** Ship arrivals in Singapore rose a little, but Kompas (EIA data) shows oil flows through Malacca fell from about 23.2 to 16.6 million barrels a day in Q2 2026, because less Gulf oil was moving east.
5. **The war-risk timeline:** listed June 2005, kept on the list January 2006, removed August 2006 (Business Times). This lag is useful evidence that cooperation reduces insurance premiums slowly.

## Comparing the national narratives

Each country tells the Malacca story in a way that fits its own interests. **Malaysia's** press (The Star, NST, Bernama, and the minister's "no pirates, only petty crooks" line) is defensive about sovereignty and reputation. In 2004 it rejected US patrols under the RMSI, in 2005 it attacked the Lloyd's war-risk listing as unfair, and in 2026 it rejected both "external control" and Indonesia's unilateral toll idea. Throughout, it credits littoral-state patrols (MALSINDO, Eyes in the Sky, the MMEA) for security. **Singapore**, seen through ST/BT archives and government releases, is the most internationalist of the three. In 2004 it was grouped with the US for wanting outside help. Its outlets stress information sharing (ReCAAP ISC, the IFC) and open access under UNCLOS, and in 2025 it, too, insisted that no incidents happened in its own waters. **Indonesia's** media (Jakarta Post, Antara, Kompas, Jakarta Globe) carry the strongest sovereignty theme: the RMSI was "within our own sovereign rights". They also show a new economic nationalism (Purbaya's toll idea, later dropped because of UNCLOS) and pride that Oman suggested the "Malacca model" for Hormuz. They say less about the fact that most 2025 robberies took place off Indonesian islands, though the 2023 Jakarta Post editorial is an exception. **Thailand's** press treats Malacca mainly as a commercial opportunity: land bridges and the Kra Canal, with cooperation presented as inadequate, although Thailand joined the patrols in 2008. **The Philippines and Vietnam** write as user states worried about energy supply. In 2026 they focused on bilateral safe-passage deals with Iran and on freedom of navigation, linking it to the South China Sea. Overall, the littoral states' story is that "regional cooperation, without outsiders, keeps Malacca safe". Outsider and user-state outlets, and the 2025 data, suggest that cooperation works best when it comes with real enforcement (arrests) and information sharing. They also suggest that Malacca's calm in 2026 depends more on the absence of a hostile coastal state than on the frameworks alone.
