## PART A – Think-tank analysis

### A1. Strait of Hormuz (2026 closure)

**1. Childs, N. (2026) 'Navigating the Strait of Hormuz', *Survival Online*, IISS, June.**
URL: https://www.iiss.org/online-analysis/survival-online/2026/06/navigating-the-strait-of-hormuz/ (PDF: https://www.iiss.org/globalassets/media-library---content--migration/files/online-analysis/survival-online/2026/06/iiss_navigating-the-strait-of-hormuz_12062026.pdf)
Type: think-tank analysis (IISS's journal-linked online series; not peer-reviewed)
Summary: Argues there is "no plausible military option" for reopening the strait. The tight waters and short warning times cancel out many of the advantages US warships normally have. Many allied navies are "much diminished" and short of the right ships, so they will only join a coalition to deal with leftover threats once a ceasefire holds. It calls for a high-level initiative on what cooperative military means can and cannot do to protect waterways.
Supports: Hormuz 2026 – joint operations, response speed. A strong source for the *limits* of multilateral cooperation.
Reliability: IISS is a respected, independent London think tank. Free to read.
Related IISS pieces seen in the same search: 'A bad peace: the Arab Gulf states and the US–Iran memorandum of understanding' (June 2026), https://www.iiss.org/online-analysis/online-analysis/2026/06/a-bad-peace-the-arab-gulf-states-and-the-usiran-memorandum-of-understanding/ (on the 17 June 2026 Islamabad MoU, with Gulf states except Oman resisting Iranian transit fees), and 'Steel Traps: America's Fraught Quest for Maritime Dominance' (Sept 2026), https://www.iiss.org/online-analysis/survival-online/2026/09/steel-traps-americas-fraught-quest-for-maritime-dominance/ (says the US Navy cannot yet keep Hormuz open "at an acceptable cost" and calls the result "sea denial via reciprocal blockade").

### A3. Malacca and Tanker War background

**14. Chalk, P. (2008) *The Maritime Dimension of International Security: Terrorism, Piracy, and Challenges for the United States*. Santa Monica, CA: RAND Corporation (MG-697).**
URL (open-access record): https://library.mumbai.bristol.ac.uk/Record/doab-20.500.12854ir-114734 (open access via DOAB; also on rand.org as MG-697)
Type: think-tank report / book (RAND)
Summary: Explains why piracy and sea-borne terrorism rose after 2000. Chalk points to lax port security, weak coastal surveillance and busy chokepoints such as Malacca, and he reviews US-led maritime security initiatives. It is written from the time just after the 2004 MALSINDO patrols began.
Supports: Malacca 1990s–2005 – formal frameworks, enforcement. Alternative factor: type of threat.
Reliability: RAND is US-government-linked. Free (open access).

## PART B – Official and institutional sources

### B1. Energy and oil (mainly Hormuz)

**17. U.S. Energy Information Administration (2024) *World Oil Transit Chokepoints*. Washington, DC: EIA (updated 25 June 2024).**
URL: https://www.eia.gov/international/analysis/special-topics/World_Oil_Transit_Chokepoints (PDF: https://www.eia.gov/international/content/analysis/special_topics/World_Oil_Transit_Chokepoints/wotc.pdf)
Type: official data report
Summary: The standard reference for oil volumes through each chokepoint, based on Vortexa tanker tracking. It ranks Hormuz and Malacca as the most important chokepoints. **Malacca is the largest by oil volume (23.7 million b/d in 2023).** About 76% of world liquids supply moved by sea in 2023. It treats the Cape of Good Hope as an alternative route, not a chokepoint.
Supports: all three cases – trade concentration, physical alternatives.
Reliability: high (US government). Free. The search said the next update was due June 2026, but I could not confirm whether it has been published.

**18. International Energy Agency (2026) *Oil Market Report* – May 2026 and August 2026. Paris: IEA.**
URLs: https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-may-2026 ; https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-august-2026 ; April: https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-april-2026
Type: official report (intergovernmental)
Summary: Monthly tracking of the closure. **May:** Gulf output hit by the closure was 14.4 mb/d below pre-war levels, and cumulative losses already exceeded 1 billion barrels. **April:** loadings through the strait averaged about 3.8 mb/d, against more than 20 mb/d in February. **August:** 2026 world supply is forecast to fall 4.3 mb/d to 102 mb/d, with 8.3 mb/d of Gulf output still shut in during July. Demand is expected to fall 1.6 mb/d, and a deal on Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb was "still elusive". Global stocks fell by about 250 million barrels across March and April.
Supports: Hormuz 2026 – severity (trade reduction, length). Multilateral response: IEA stock release (link 4 in the plan).
Reliability: very high. The summary page is free, but the full report may need a subscription. Check whether September and October 2026 reports exist.

**19. World Bank (2026) *Commodity Markets Outlook*, April 2026. Washington, DC: World Bank.**
URL: the World Bank report page was not in the results. Verified coverage: https://www.kitco.com/news/off-the-wire/2026-04-28/world-bank-forecasts-24-surge-energy-prices-2026-due-middle-east-war (find the report itself on worldbank.org under "Commodity Markets Outlook")
Type: official report
Summary: Calls the disruption the largest oil supply shock on record, with supply down about 10 mb/d at the peak. It notes that Hormuz carries about 35% of seaborne crude trade. Brent is forecast to average $86/bbl in 2026 (against $69 in 2025), or as high as $115 if facilities suffer more damage. Energy prices are forecast to rise 24%, fertilisers 31%. The baseline assumed Hormuz traffic would be back near normal by October 2026, **which did not happen**. A later World Bank *Global Economic Prospects* (June 2026) reportedly raised the Brent forecast to $94.
Supports: Hormuz 2026 – severity (Brent, general price level).
Reliability: very high. I verified only secondary coverage, so cite the primary PDF.

**20. OECD (2026) *OECD Economic Outlook, Interim Report*, March 2026. Paris: OECD.**
URL: verified mirror https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/462159 ; Swedish delegation summary https://www.swedenabroad.se/en/embassies/oecd-unesco/current/news/oecd-economic-outlook-interim-report-march-2026/ (the original is on oecd.org)

### B2. Shipping, trade and inflation (mainly Red Sea)

### B3. Legal and political frameworks

**25. United Nations Security Council (2024) Resolution 2722 (2024), S/RES/2722, 10 January.**
URL: https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4033392/files/S_RES_2722_%282024%29-EN.pdf (IMO copy: https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/MediaCentre/HotTopics/Documents/Red%20Sea/SCR%202722(2024)%20on%20the%20Red%20Sea%20adopted%20(E)%201.pdf)

## Points that may contradict or complicate the student's plan

1. **"2025 surge in robberies" in Malacca.** ReCAAP's director says the record 108 incidents "does not signal a matching rise in threat to shipping": they were mostly petty theft, there were no CAT 1 cases, and 107 of the 108 were in the **Singapore** Strait, not the Malacca Strait. The drop in late 2025 came from **national** enforcement (Indonesian arrests), not a new multilateral operation. IMB counts 80 incidents and ReCAAP counts 108, so say which source you use.
2. **Malacca carries more oil than Hormuz** by volume (EIA: 23.7 mb/d in 2023). Do not describe Hormuz as "the biggest" without saying which measure you mean.
3. **Military cooperation has limits at Hormuz.** IISS (Childs 2026) says there is no plausible military option and allies lack the ships. ECFR says EU missions are "fragmented and under-resourced". This supports a "cooperation has limits" conclusion and works against any assumption that coalitions fix chokepoints.
4. **Red Sea inflation was small.** ECB estimates were about +0.25 percentage points, and Elcano found the oil-market effect limited. Hormuz 2026 was much larger (OECD: G20 inflation 4.0%). So severity depends on *what* passes through the chokepoint (containers vs oil), not only on cooperation.
5. **Forecasts dated by events.** The World Bank's April 2026 baseline assumed Hormuz would be back to normal by October 2026, and the IEA's May report assumed flows would resume from June. Neither happened, so present these as forecasts, not outcomes.
6. **PortWatch numbers vary widely** between write-ups. Download the data yourself rather than quoting secondary figures.
