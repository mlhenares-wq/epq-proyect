# 16. World press on the chokepoints: views from the big importers and the rest of the world

Compiled 19 October 2026 for Manuela López-Henares's EPQ.
Focus: Hormuz 2026 and Red Sea 2023–26, plus some Malacca and China "Malacca dilemma" items.

## How to read this file (please read first)

- **How I checked these:** every article below appeared in live web-search results with the headline and URL shown. I could not open the full articles: the page fetcher failed with DNS errors on every news site I tried (globaltimes.cn, caixinglobal.com, cnn.com, france24.com) and refused clarin.com and folha.uol.com.br. **Summaries are built from the search-result snippets, not from reading each full article.** Before quoting a figure in your EPQ, open the link and check it.
- **Dates:** where the date is in the URL, I give it exactly. Where it is not (SCMP, Korea Herald, TASS, RT), I give the month/year implied by the search result and mark it "approx.". Check these.
- **Gaps against the brief (important):**
  - The search tool would not search The Hindu, Times of India, Indian Express, Hindustan Times, BBC, Guardian, Telegraph, FT, NYT, WSJ, Le Monde, Der Spiegel, DW, Corriere della Sera or Kathimerini. Their sites block the crawler. General searches naming those papers also returned no articles from them. **So none of those 15 outlets appears below.** I used other papers from the same countries instead: Business Standard, Tribune, Deccan Herald and All India Radio for India; Forces News and Lloyd's List for the UK; ANSA for Italy; France 24 for France.
  - Clarín (Argentina), Folha (Brazil) and El Universal (Mexico) were also unreachable. Latin America is covered by Infobae, iProfesional, Diario de Cuyo, Rosario3, El Imparcial, BM&C News and CNN en Español.
  - Yonhap and NHK did not come up directly. Korean coverage comes from the Korea Herald, Korea Times and JoongAng Daily; Japanese coverage from the Japan Times, Nikkei Asia and Jiji (via Nippon.com).
  - I hit the shared limit of 200 web searches before running every query I planned, so some outlets got fewer searches than intended.
- **Key for the "Supports" line:** C = case study (H = Hormuz, RS = Red Sea/Suez, M = Malacca). Cooperation factors: formal frameworks, joint operations, information sharing, response speed, enforcement. Severity measures: trade reduction, length of disruption, price level / Brent. Likelihood measures: incidents, freight rates, war-risk insurance. Alternative factors: supply-chain design, trade concentration, threat type, technology, connected crises, physical alternatives, regional growth.

## 1. CHINA (mainland state media + Hong Kong + Caixin)

**State-control note:** Global Times, China Daily and Xinhua are owned and controlled by the Chinese Communist Party and state. Global Times (a People's Daily offshoot) is the most nationalist and openly anti-US. Xinhua is the official state news agency. Caixin is privately run and does real business journalism, but it still works under Chinese censorship. SCMP is Hong Kong-based and owned by Alibaba. It is more independent than mainland outlets and often quotes Western analysts, but it is cautious on Beijing's red lines.

1. **"Strait of Hormuz faces volatility amid US President's blockade order; Chinese FM notes root cause is military conflict, urges restraint"**. Global Times, China, April 2026. https://www.globaltimes.cn/page/202604/1358847.shtml
   - Summary: Reports on the US naval blockade of Iranian ports announced in April 2026. It quotes China's Foreign Ministry saying the root cause of the disruption is the military conflict itself, and that all parties should stay calm and show restraint.
   - Perspective: China puts the blame on the war (and implicitly on the US and Israel), not on Iran's closure, and presents itself as the calm voice calling for de-escalation.
   - Bias: Party-state outlet. It repeats the official line and offers no criticism of Iran.
   - Supports: C-H. Cooperation (China stays outside any coalition); length of disruption.

2. **"Chinese expert weighs in on impact of Strait of Hormuz blockade, Iran's capability of controlling the strait"**. Global Times, China, March 2026. https://www.globaltimes.cn/page/202603/1356150.shtml
   - Summary: A Chinese expert assesses how far Iran can really control the strait and what a blockade means for shipping and oil. Search coverage linked to it quotes a Chinese official saying international law protects innocent passage and freedom of navigation through international straits.
   - Perspective: Beijing supports freedom of navigation in principle (it needs Gulf oil) while avoiding open criticism of Tehran.
   - Bias: State media. The "experts" used are usually aligned with official policy.
   - Supports: C-H. Formal frameworks (UNCLOS transit passage); threat type.

## Comparing the national narratives

The same events read very differently depending on who is importing what and whose navy is at sea.

**China's** state media (Global Times, Xinhua, China Daily) tell one story for both crises: the "root cause" is the war (Gaza in 2023–24, the US–Israeli strikes on Iran in 2026), US military coalitions make things worse, and China is the calm, fair voice of the UN and international law. In practice Beijing protected its own ships through bilateral understandings with the Houthis and the IRGC rather than joining Operation Prosperity Guardian, Aspides or the France–UK mission. The Hong Kong and business press (SCMP, Caixin) show the cracks in that story: a Chinese-owned tanker was hit in 2024, Gulf imports fell 25% in March 2026, and the "Malacca dilemma" has become a two-chokepoint problem.

**India, Japan and South Korea** are all heavily exposed: about 90% of India's LPG, 93% of Japan's crude and about 70% of Korea's crude normally come through Hormuz. Each chose national action plus quiet deals with Tehran over joining a US-led coalition. India's press celebrates a capable, autonomous navy (Sankalp in 2024, Urja Suraksha in 2026). Japan's press is dominated by the legal limits of its constitution, so a ceasefire must come first. Korea's press is about how to stay "not involved in war" while protecting its ships.

**Russian** state media (TASS, RT) present a world short of oil, with Russia as the reliable supplier and its ships allowed through as one of Iran's "friendly nations". Iranian threats are amplified and the US blockade is framed as the aggressor.

**European** coverage (France 24, ANSA, Euronews, German outlets) is about building rules-based, "purely defensive" multilateral missions (Aspides, the France–UK Hormuz coalition). It also shows how slow these are: every contributor (Germany, Italy, Japan, Australia) demanded a ceasefire, a mandate or a parliamentary vote first. **US** outlets (CNN, the Washington Post, CNBC) focus on American firepower, US-guided transits and sinkings.

Distant importers (**Australia, Argentina, Mexico, Brazil**) barely mention navies at all. For them the crisis is about pump prices, rationing and state subsidies, which shows that price-level severity reaches countries with no role in the cooperation.

For the EPQ, the most useful pattern is this: the biggest importers mostly relied on **bilateral, selective passage** rather than **multilateral cooperation**. Multilateral missions were designed to act only after the fighting stopped, so in 2026 they did more to shorten recovery than to prevent disruption. That fits the plan's hypothesis that cooperation reduces severity more than likelihood. Freight, insurance and connected crises (the Red Sea route being hit just when it was the Hormuz bypass) did much of the damage.
