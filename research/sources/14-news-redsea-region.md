# 14 — Regional newspaper coverage: Suez Canal / Red Sea / Bab el-Mandeb

Case study 2 (Suez/Red Sea): the 1967–75 closure compared with the 2023–26 Houthi crisis.
This file covers newspapers from the countries around the chokepoint, grouped by country.

## How these were checked (please read)

- **Every article below appeared in live web-search results on 19 October 2026.** For each one I saw the headline, the outlet and the exact URL, plus a summary of its content. Nothing has been invented.
- **I could not open the pages directly.** The research environment's network proxy blocked direct page fetches (Ahram Online, Egypt Independent and Times of Israel all returned "connection refused"). Summaries and figures therefore come from the search engine's reading of each page. Before quoting a figure in the EPQ, open the link and check it.
- **Dates.** Where the date is in the URL or was stated in the search result, it is given plainly. Where I worked it out from the content (for example, "FY23/24 figures" means about July 2024), it is marked *approx.* Where I could not find it, it says *date not confirmed*.
- **Search limit reached.** The shared web-search budget for this session ran out near the end, so a few dates (mostly for the Turkish and Djiboutian items) could not be pinned down. A follow-up session could fill them in.
- **State-control key:** **State** = owned or controlled by a government or armed group. **State-aligned** = private, but close to the government. **Independent** = generally free of government control. Press-freedom context: Egypt, Eritrea, Djibouti, Saudi Arabia and Houthi-held Yemen all have heavily restricted media.
- **Codes for "supports":** *Severity* = trade reduction / length of disruption / prices. *Likelihood* = incidents / freight rates / insurance. *Cooperation* = frameworks / joint operations / information sharing / response speed / enforcement. *Alt* = alternative factors (Cape route, pipelines, connected crises, type of threat, and so on).

## 1. EGYPT

Egypt owns the canal and loses money when ships avoid the Red Sea, so Egyptian outlets mostly report Suez Canal Authority (SCA) figures and statements by its chairman, Admiral Osama Rabie.
**Bias note for all state outlets:** Ahram Online (Al-Ahram) is state-owned. Egypt Today is close to the state. Daily News Egypt and Egypt Independent are private but work under tight censorship. Mada Masr is the main independent outlet; it is blocked inside Egypt.

**E1. "Suez Canal revenue rises 23% to $4.6 billion in FY 2025/26: Chairman"**
- Outlet: Ahram Online (Egypt) · Date: 28 June 2026 · URL: https://english.ahram.org.eg/News/571808.aspx
- Summary: Rabie said dollar revenue rose 23% in FY2025/26 (to about $4.67bn) as ships returned after the Red Sea disruption. Transits rose 10% and tonnage 22%, but both are still below pre-crisis targets. He said falling insurance premiums are now tipping shipowners back towards the shorter Suez route.
- National perspective: recovery is under way and Egypt is a reliable route again.
- Bias: state-owned; reports SCA figures without challenge.
- Supports: Severity (trade reduction, length of disruption) and Likelihood (insurance premiums drive route choice).

- National perspective: Egypt presents itself as a victim of a conflict it did not start.
- Bias: state-owned.
- Supports: Severity (trade reduction). This is the clearest single figure for the 2024 collapse.

## 2. YEMEN

Yemen is split. The Houthis (Ansar Allah) control Sanaa and the Red Sea coast and run Saba. The internationally recognised government and the southern separatists (STC) have their own media.

- National perspective (Houthi): the attacks are framed as solidarity with Gaza and a legal "naval ban".
- Bias: **propaganda organ of an armed group**. Its claims are not independently verified.

## Points that contradict or complicate the student's plan

1. **Revenue figures differ depending on the period.** Fiscal-year (July–June) figures show −23% (FY23/24), while calendar-year figures show −61% (2024). Baselines also vary: $8.6bn, $9.4bn or $10.25bn. Always say which period a figure covers.
2. **The Houthi "blockade on Saudi Arabia" date.** The formal declaration was **20 July 2026**, which matches the plan, but some sources date attacks on Saudi Arabia to 13 July.
3. **Eilat "collapse".** The port was declared "in a state of bankruptcy" (July 2024) and had near-zero ship calls, but the government kept it open. Partial car imports now arrive via Aqaba.
4. **Control of Bab el-Mandeb in October 2026 is contested.** The Houthis took Mokha, Perim and the Hanish Islands in September 2026, while the Yemeni government claims to have recaptured positions from 4 October. Traffic counts also differ (Kpler about 32–41 a day, the Africa Center about 25 a week).
5. **The 15% Suez discount** (May 2025) was reportedly suspended in April 2026. This comes from a single source and is unconfirmed.
6. **Littoral states reject outside coalitions.** Egypt, Eritrea, Sudan and Somalia (El-Alamein Declaration, October 2026) insist that Red Sea security is for coastal states only. Djibouti refused US strike operations but helped EU Aspides. This complicates any simple "multilateral cooperation = good" argument: *who* cooperates matters.

## Comparing the national narratives

Each country's newspapers tell the crisis through its own economic stake. **Egypt's** mostly state-controlled press is a story of loss and recovery told through Suez Canal Authority numbers. Egypt appears as an innocent victim, the Cape route is called "unsustainable", and recovery is credited to diplomacy (the Gaza deal) rather than to US or EU warships. Only independent Mada Masr exposes the political bargaining behind US protection. **Houthi-run Saba** presents the attacks as a lawful "naval ban" in solidarity with Gaza, and later as retaliation for a Saudi "siege". The anti-Houthi Yemeni outlets (South24, Yemen Monitor, Yemen Online) stress war crimes, insurance shocks and the Houthis' march on Bab el-Mandeb. **Saudi** Arab News, echoed by Jordan, Sudan and Turkey, frames the July 2026 blockade as an illegal attack on freedom of navigation, answered by a new Saudi-led coalition and defence pacts. **Eritrea** and, increasingly, Egypt promote a "littoral states only" doctrine that rejects outside navies, while **Djibouti** quietly balances its crowded foreign bases and casts itself as a regional "sentinel" that profits from transshipment. **Israel's** independent press gives the most detailed picture of direct economic damage (Eilat's 85% drop, near-bankruptcy and survival on state aid), framed as an Iranian-proxy threat. **Somali** outlets show a side-effect: piracy returned once navies were drawn north, and federal–Puntland rivalry weakens enforcement. **Kenya, South Africa, Ethiopia and Turkey** treat the crisis as an opportunity as well as a cost: busier ports, nearshoring orders and arguments for new corridors. South African and Ethiopian writers admit that infrastructure gaps limit the gains. For the EPQ, the key point is that the regional press shows cooperation as split rather than united: Western-led operations (OPG, Aspides), Saudi-led coalitions, littoral-state declarations and bilateral deals (China–Houthis) overlap and compete. That may help explain why the 2023–26 disruption has lasted so long. By contrast, the 1967–75 closure ended through a state-to-state diplomatic settlement (the 1974–75 disengagement agreements) followed by international mine clearance.
