## Read this first: how these were checked

- **Network limits during this search:** the Crossref, OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar APIs, doi.org and the publisher websites could not be reached from this research environment (DNS or proxy blocked). The web-search budget also ran out before I finished. So every paper below was **verified by appearing in web-search results** (the publisher page, RePEc/IDEAS, TRID, or a university repository), which the brief allows. **I did not open the full texts.** The summaries come from the abstracts and snippets shown in search results.
- **DOIs:** a DOI is given only where I saw it, either written out in a result or as part of the publisher URL (for example `link.springer.com/article/10.1007/...` or `tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/...`). Where I only saw a ScienceDirect or Cambridge URL, the DOI is marked **"not seen – look it up"**. Do not guess it.
- **Author names:** where no result showed the authors, the citation says "[authors to check]". Please fill these in from the publisher page before you cite the paper. Do not cite anything with a gap still in it.
- Labels: **OA** = free full text seen or very likely (open-access journal). **Paywall** = abstract only; try your school library, Google Scholar "All versions", or email the author.

## 1. Strait of Hormuz: the Tanker War (1980–88)

**Gap warning:** I found very few true peer-reviewed journal articles specifically on Earnest Will or convoy effectiveness. Most of the literature is in books (Crist, *The Twilight War*; Duffy on Earnest Will), professional journals (USNI *Proceedings*, *Naval War College Review*) and think-tank pieces. The items below are the best I could verify. Be open about this limitation in your EPQ.

### 1.1 Journal of Military and Strategic Studies (2024): Tanker War perspectives
- **Citation:** [Author to check] (2024) '[Title to check: article on American and Iranian perspectives in the Tanker War]', *Journal of Military and Strategic Studies*, 23(3).
- **URL:** https://jmss.org/article/download/76863/57806/258112
- **DOI:** not seen. **Type:** peer-reviewed (an open-access Canadian journal from the Centre of Military and Strategic Studies). **OA**
- **Summary:** The article looks at how both Washington and Tehran saw the escalation of the late-1980s Tanker War. It argues that American policymakers misjudged how far the conflict with Iran could escalate. It also notes that Kuwait formally asked the US to reflag its tankers in early 1987.
- **Case study / factor:** Hormuz 1980–88. It covers joint operations, response speed, and the risk that escorting ships escalates the conflict.
- **Key point:** Kuwait's 1987 request to reflag tankers is what started US convoying.
- **Reliability:** The journal is peer-reviewed and free. Because it is written from a US and Western viewpoint, compare it with Iranian-perspective sources.

### 1.2 Mobley (2021): British misgivings about the 1987 reflagging
- **Citation:** Mobley, R.A. (2021) 'London and Washington [full title to check]', *Naval War College Review*, 74(2), Article 9.
- **URL:** https://digital-commons.usnwc.edu/nwc-review/vol74/iss2/9
- **DOI:** none seen. **Type:** professional journal with editorial review (not a standard academic double-blind journal). **OA**
- **Summary:** The article uses recently declassified British records to show that London had doubts about America's 1987 reflagging and escorting of Kuwaiti tankers. It is useful for showing that the "coalition" was not fully united, which matters when you judge how well multilateral cooperation worked.
- **Case study / factor:** Hormuz 1980–88. It covers formal frameworks, coalition cohesion and joint operations.
- **Reliability:** It is based on primary archives and written by a former US intelligence analyst. It is good evidence but not neutral.

### 1.3 Mobley (2016): intelligence support during Earnest Will
- **Citation:** Mobley, R.A. (2016) 'Fighting Iran: intelligence support during Operation Earnest Will, 1987–88', *Studies in Intelligence*, 60(3) (extracts).
- **URL:** https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Fighting-Iran.pdf
- **DOI:** none. **Type:** professional or government journal (CIA Center for the Study of Intelligence). It is not academic peer review. **OA**
- **Summary:** The article describes how intelligence supported the 1987–88 escort operation. Use it for the **information-sharing** factor in the Tanker War period, which you can compare with modern AIS and maritime-domain-awareness tools.
- **Case study / factor:** Hormuz 1980–88. It covers information sharing and response speed.
- **Reliability:** It is an official US publication, so expect a pro-US framing.

### 1.4 Ocean Development & International Law (2022): the legal regime of Hormuz and attacks on tankers
- **Citation:** [Authors to check] (2022) 'The legal regime of the Strait of Hormuz and attacks against oil tankers: law of the sea and law on the use of force perspectives', *Ocean Development & International Law*.
- **URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00908320.2022.2096158
- **DOI:** 10.1080/00908320.2022.2096158. **Type:** peer-reviewed. **Paywall** (a PDF link appeared in the search results and may be open).
- **Summary:** The article explains the transit-passage rules for Hormuz and analyses tanker attacks, including the 2019 limpet-mine attacks and Iran's seizure of the *Stena Impero*. It argues that states did not provide enough evidence to prove beyond reasonable doubt that Iran was responsible for the 2019 mine attacks.
- **Case study / factor:** Hormuz, linking the two periods. It covers enforcement of laws and the type of threat (deniable "grey-zone" attacks).
- **Reliability:** A specialist law journal of high quality.

### 1.5 Shepard and Pratson (2020): piracy in the Strait of Hormuz and energy exports
- **Citation:** Shepard, J.U. and Pratson, L.F. (2020) 'Maritime piracy in the Strait of Hormuz and implications of energy export security', *Energy Policy*, 140, 111379.
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S030142152030135X (accepted manuscript: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S030142152030135X)
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.enpol.2020.111379 (confirmed through RePEc and Duke's faculty listing). **Type:** peer-reviewed. **Paywall**, though the "am" (author manuscript) link may be free.
- **Summary:** The authors model piracy and harassment in Hormuz as a "soft restriction" on Gulf fuel exports. How badly a country is hit depends on how sensitive it is to risk and on what fuel it exports. This helps explain why the same threat hurts some exporters more than others.
- **Case study / factor:** Hormuz. It covers severity (trade reduction) and the alternative factor of trade concentration.
- **Reliability:** Duke University authors in a top energy-policy journal.

### 1.6 Journal of Transportation Security (2012): transnational threats to Gulf oil shipping
- **Citation:** [Authors to check] (2012) 'The effect of transnational threats on the security of Persian Gulf maritime petroleum transportation', *Journal of Transportation Security*.
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12198-012-0090-y
- **DOI:** 10.1007/s12198-012-0090-y. **Type:** peer-reviewed. **Paywall**
- **Summary:** The article examines how terrorism, piracy and other non-state threats affect oil tanker transport in the Gulf. It is useful background on the type of threat, from state-led attacks (the Tanker War) to non-state ones. I only saw the title, so read the abstract before relying on it.

## 2. Strait of Hormuz: the 2026 closure

### 2.1 Lassoued (2026): chokepoint insecurity and trade vulnerability at Hormuz
- **Citation:** Lassoued, [initial to check] (2026) 'Maritime chokepoint insecurity and systemic trade vulnerability: a transportation security assessment of the Strait of Hormuz', *Journal of Transportation Security*, 19(1).
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12198-026-00345-y (RePEc: https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/jtrsec/v19y2026i1d10.1007_s12198-026-00345-y.html)
- **DOI:** 10.1007/s12198-026-00345-y. **Type:** peer-reviewed. **Paywall** (check for OA)
- **Summary:** The article treats Hormuz as a problem of keeping a transport corridor secure, not just as a cause of oil-price shocks. That framing fits your question about whether cooperation reduces the *likelihood* of disruption as well as its *severity*.
- **Case study / factor:** Hormuz 2026. It covers likelihood and severity, plus physical alternatives.

### 2.2 Meza, Ari, Al Sada and Koç (2026): what a Hormuz closure does to the LNG trade
- **Citation:** Meza, A., Ari, I., Al Sada, M. and Koç, M. (2026) 'Implications of interrupting the Hormuz Strait in the LNG trade', *Journal of Transportation Security*, article 28.
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12198-026-00350-1 (HBKU repository: https://elmi.hbku.edu.qa/en/publications/implications-of-interrupting-the-hormuz-strait-in-the-lng-trade/)
- **DOI:** 10.1007/s12198-026-00350-1. **Type:** peer-reviewed. **Paywall**
- **Summary:** The authors use an agent-based simulation with geographic data, checked against past LNG trade. A one-month closure could probably be absorbed because importers can switch to Pacific suppliers. A closure longer than six months leaves the market unable to supply every importer. There is no alternative sea route, so the fixes are long-term: military deterrence, pipelines, strategic gas reserves and a faster energy transition.
- **Case study / factor:** Hormuz 2026. It covers severity (length of disruption) and physical alternatives.
- **Key figures:** about 1 month can be absorbed; more than 6 months means unmet demand.
- **Reliability:** The authors are at a Qatari university, and Qatar is the biggest LNG exporter through Hormuz, so there is a possible regional interest. The results come from a model, not from observed 2026 data.

### 2.3 Australian Journal of Maritime & Ocean Affairs (2026): Iranian transit charges
- **Citation:** [Authors to check] (2026) 'The price of passage: the legality of Iranian transit charges in the Strait of Hormuz under the Law of the Sea', [journal: the DOI prefix 10.1080/18366503 points to the *Australian Journal of Maritime & Ocean Affairs*; confirm this].
- **URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/18366503.2026.2701606
- **DOI:** 10.1080/18366503.2026.2701606. **Type:** peer-reviewed. **Paywall**
- **Summary:** The article covers the period after the partial reopening in late April 2026. Iran said foreign ships would need Iranian approval, would face inspection and would pay transit fees, and its parliament passed a toll law. The article argues that no state currently has the right to levy such a charge under the law of the sea.
- **Case study / factor:** Hormuz 2026. It covers enforcement of laws and formal frameworks (UNCLOS transit passage).
- **Flag for the plan:** The student's timeline has the conflict starting on 28 Feb, a ceasefire, and the strait shut again in June. This source adds a **"partial reopening with Iranian conditions"** in late April 2026, which is worth adding to the timeline. One search result (Wikipedia) dated the start to 4 March rather than 28 February. Check the start date against a primary source.

## 3. Suez 1967–75 and the Red Sea / Houthi crisis (2023–26)

**Gap warning (Suez 1967–75):** apart from Feyrer (2021), which she already has, I did not find another peer-reviewed article on the 1967–75 closure. Two working papers (not peer-reviewed) are useful:
- *Growth volatility and trade: evidence from the 1967–1975 closure of the Suez Canal*, MPRA paper 39040: https://ideas.repec.org/p/pra/mprapa/39040.html
- CEPII working paper 2016-29, *Trade costs and the Suez and Panama Canals*: https://www.cepii.fr/PDF_PUB/wp/2016/wp2016-29.pdf

**This contradicts Feyrer.** The CEPII paper estimates a much smaller effect of distance on trade (an elasticity of about −0.15) than Feyrer does. So the size of the 1967 trade shock is **contested**. Say this in your EPQ rather than quoting Feyrer as settled fact.

### 3.1 Verschuur, Lumma and Hall (2025): systemic impacts of chokepoint disruptions
- **Citation:** Verschuur, J., Lumma, [initial to check] and Hall, J.W. [check initials] (2025) 'Systemic impacts of disruptions at maritime chokepoints', *Nature Communications*.
- **URL:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-65403-w
- **DOI:** 10.1038/s41467-025-65403-w. **Type:** peer-reviewed. **OA** (*Nature Communications* is fully open access.)
- **Summary:** The paper estimates that about **US$192 billion a year** of trade is at risk of disruption at chokepoints. Most of that comes from geopolitical risk at the Taiwan Strait and Suez Canal, and from a mix of hazards at Bab el-Mandeb. It is very strong for comparing all three of your chokepoints on the same scale.
- **Case study / factor:** all three. It covers chokepoint vulnerability, trade concentration and severity.
- **Reliability:** an Oxford and TU Delft team in a top journal. Highly reliable.

### 3.2 Journal of Transport Geography (2024): Red Sea crisis and container port calls
- **Citation:** [Authors to check, Hong Kong PolyU] (2024) 'Geopolitical tension and shipping network disruption: analysis of the Red Sea crisis on container port calls', *Journal of Transport Geography*, 121, 104004.
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966692324002138 (PolyU record: https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/geopolitical-tension-and-shipping-network-disruption-analysis-of-/)

The snippets reported that average daily Suez transits fell from about 75 to about 30 between December 2023 and December 2024.

### 3.3 Bedoya-Maya et al. (2025): European port competitiveness in the Red Sea crisis

### 3.5 Maritime Economics & Logistics (2026): Mediterranean port shifts measured with AIS data

## 4. Strait of Malacca: piracy and cooperation

### 4.1 Marine Policy (2023): from RMSI to ReCAAP
- **Citation:** [Authors to check] (2023) 'Multilateral cooperation against maritime piracy in the Straits of Malacca: from the RMSI to ReCAAP', *Marine Policy*.
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X23001550 (abstract also at https://straits.univ-littoral.fr/?p=2201)
- **DOI:** not seen. Look it up. **Type:** peer-reviewed. **Paywall**
- **Summary:** The US-backed Regional Maritime Security Initiative (RMSI, proposed 2003–04) failed because Indonesia and Malaysia saw it as a threat to their sovereignty and did not rank piracy as a priority. ReCAAP learned from that failure. Its main strength is that it is **inclusive** and based on information-sharing rather than joint patrols.
- **Case study / factor:** Malacca. It covers formal frameworks, information sharing and littoral sovereignty.
- **Key point:** Malaysia and Indonesia still have not signed ReCAAP, although they take part in many of its activities.

### 4.2 Marine Policy (2026): governance, sovereignty and Eyes in the Sky
- **Citation:** [Authors to check] (2026) 'Complexities of governance and sovereignty in maritime security', *Marine Policy*.
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0308597X26000369
- **DOI:** not seen. **Type:** peer-reviewed. **OA likely** (the URL has no "abs", which usually means open access).
- **Summary:** This is the strongest critique of Eyes in the Sky I found. Only about **8 air patrols a week** were flown, against an estimated **70** needed, and flights were in daytime while pirates attacked at night. It warns that piracy falling *at the same time* as these measures came in makes them look effective without proving they caused the fall.
- **Case study / factor:** Malacca. It covers joint operations, enforcement and causation versus correlation.
- **Flag for the plan:** this **challenges** any claim that MSP/Eyes in the Sky alone caused the post-2005 fall in piracy. Use it in your evaluation.

### 4.4 Marine Policy (2022): interstate hostility and maritime crime
- **Citation:** [Authors to check] (2022) 'Interstate hostility and maritime crime: evidence from South East Asia', *Marine Policy*.
- **URL (free accepted manuscript):** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S0308597X22001816

## Points that contradict or complicate the plan
1. **Feyrer's 1967 trade effect is contested.** The CEPII 2016 working paper finds a much smaller distance effect.
2. **Eyes in the Sky may have been much weaker than official accounts say** (Marine Policy 2026: 8 weekly patrols against about 70 needed, daytime only). The fall in Malacca piracy may also reflect governance on land and the displacement of piracy elsewhere (Heliyon 2023; *Asia-Pacific Journal*).
3. **There was a late-April 2026 partial reopening of Hormuz with Iranian approval, inspection and toll conditions.** Add it to the timeline. One source gives the war's start as 4 March rather than 28 February, so verify the date.
4. **Naval escorts in the Red Sea reduced but did not stop attacks** (Frontiers 2025; RUSI Journal 2024). That supports a "reduces severity more than likelihood" type of conclusion.
