## Read this first: how these were checked, and what is missing

- **How checked:** Every article below came up in web search results, with its headline, URL and dated snippets. The page-fetch tool failed for every host during this session (DNS errors on upi.com, time.com, cia.gov and others), so **no page was opened in full**. Key figures come from the search-result snippets. Check each one against the page before you quote it.

- **The search budget ran out partway through the work.** Because of that, **the Malacca case (1999–2006) has no articles in this file.** Searches still to do: the Alondra Rainbow hijack (Oct–Nov 1999), Lloyd's JWC adding Malacca as a war-risk area (June 2005), the MALSINDO launch (20 July 2004), Eyes in the Sky (13 Sept 2005) and ReCAAP (2006). Good free places to try are the upi.com Archives (they run into the 2000s), apnews.com and news.bbc.co.uk.
- **The Washington Post archive, AP News archive, CIA CREST and ABC hemeroteca were not searched**, because the budget ran out first. One CIA reading-room item did appear in a search: https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/print/1622271 (Tanker War related). It could not be opened, so it is not counted.

**How to cite UPI wire stories (Harvard):** UPI (year) 'Headline as shown', *United Press International*, day Month year. Available at: URL (Accessed: 19 October 2026). Many UPI archive pages use the first words of the story as the headline. That is normal for this archive.

## CASE STUDY 2 – Suez Canal, 1967–75 closure

### 1. Time (c. late 1969) 'World: The Suez Canal's Bleak Centennial'
- URL: https://time.com/archive/6635732/world-the-suez-canals-bleak-centennial/ (subscriber copy: https://content.time.com/time/subscriber/article/0,33009,841641-1,00.html)
- Type: news (weekly magazine).
- Summary: Published at the canal's 100th anniversary (November 1969). It reports that the only ships left in the canal are 15 vessels trapped since the June 1967 war, 14 of them bunched together in the Great Bitter Lake. Skeleton crews are rotated every three months and pass the time with lifeboat races, soccer, church services and films. This is the "Yellow Fleet" / Great Bitter Lake Association.
- Supports: Suez 1967–75 – **length of disruption**. It is also a human-interest primary account of the stranded ships.
- Key figures: "15 vessels… trapped… 30 months", "14… in the Great Bitter Lake".
- Reliability: Reputable magazine, written at the time. The exact issue date was not shown in the search results. The "30 months" figure puts it around late 1969, but that is inferred. Check the date on the page.

### 2. Time (June 1967) 'Middle East: The Quickest War' and 'Essay: Running From Defeat'
- URLs: https://time.com/archive/6890621/middle-east-the-quickest-war/ ; https://time.com/archive/6633612/essay-running-from-defeat/
- Type: news / opinion essay (magazine).
- Summary: Time's coverage of the Six-Day War. According to the search summary, one of these June 1967 pieces reports that the canal was "blocked at both ends by scuttled Egyptian ships". This is the start of the closure.
- Supports: Suez 1967 – the start and **type of threat** (deliberate blocking by a state at war).
- Reliability: Contemporary, but the exact issue dates were not confirmed, and it is not certain which of the two pieces contains the "scuttled ships" line. Open both pages to check.

### 3. Time (October 1973) 'Middle East: The War of the Day of Judgment' and 'The World 1973: Black October – Old Enemies at War Again'
- URLs: https://time.com/archive/6841881/middle-east-the-war-of-the-day-of-judgment/ ; https://time.com/archive/6697820/the-world-1973-black-october-old-enemies-at-war-again-yom-kippur-war/
- Type: news (magazine).
- Summary: Coverage of the October 1973 war. Egypt crossed the canal with up to 100,000 men on the east bank by the end of the first week. Arab producers cut output and banned oil exports to the US and the Netherlands, and US oil prices roughly quadrupled.
- Supports: Suez – **connected crises** (the 1973 war and oil embargo). Severity: **oil price / general price level**. It also explains why the canal stayed shut until 1975.
- Key figures: about 100,000 Egyptian troops across the canal; prices "quadrupled".
- Reliability: Contemporary. The search tool warned that the dates in its results might be unreliable, so check the issue date on each page. The "quadrupled" line may come from a later retrospective piece, '40 Years After the 1973 Oil Embargo', https://time.com/archive/7162970/… , which is not contemporary.

### 4. Time (May 1974) issue of 6 May 1974 / 'Egypt: Clear the Canal' / 'Egypt: Salvaging Suez'
- URLs: https://time.com/magazine/us/3566907/may-6th-1974-vol-103-no-18-u-s/ ; https://time.com/archive/6611227/egypt-clear-the-canal/ ; https://time.com/archive/6842859/egypt-salvaging-suez/
- Type: news (magazine).
- Summary: May 1974 reporting on the start of the US-led clearance (Nimbus Star / Nimrod Spar). US Navy Sea Stallion helicopters were to work with British minesweepers to find "thousands of shells, rockets and bombs from the 1967 and 1973 wars", sweeping for magnetic, acoustic and pressure mines. US Rear Admiral Brian McCauley is named as commander. The US cost share is put at about $15 million.
- Supports: Suez – **joint operations / multilateral cooperation** (US, UK, France, Egypt), **response speed**.
- Reliability: Contemporary. It is not confirmed which of the three URLs holds which figure. The 6 May 1974 issue page is the dated anchor.

### 5. Time (December 1974) 'The World: Clean Sweep of the Canal'
- URL: https://time.com/archive/6876758/the-world-clean-sweep-of-the-canal/
- Type: news (magazine).
- Summary: Reports the end of the clearance. More than 700,000 explosives, "ranging in size from hand grenades to rockets and aerial bombs", had been disarmed. The task force was headed by US Admiral Kent Carroll. It also says 16 more ships with skeleton crews were "rusting but still afloat in the Great Bitter Lake".
- Supports: Suez – **joint operations**, scale of the clearance task, Yellow Fleet.
- Key figures: 700,000+ explosives. A later Time piece gives 686,000, so the figures **conflict**; the Proceedings article in the plan (link 23) is the better source for exact numbers. Note the ship count too: Time says 16 more ships, while most sources say 15 (14 in the Bitter Lake plus 1 in Lake Timsah).
- Reliability: Contemporary. The December 1974 date comes from the search summary and has not been checked on the page.

### 6. Time (9 June 1975) 'The World: Suez: The Seas Rejoined'
- URL: https://time.com/archive/6846859/the-world-suez-the-seas-rejoined/
- Type: news (magazine).
- Summary: The reopening issue. A multinational team spent more than a year clearing the waterway, and US Navy Sea Stallions towed minesweeping sleds along its whole length. The first convoy was due to start north from Suez city at the end of that week. It gives a UN estimate that the closure cost the world economy about $10 billion in higher shipping costs and lost trade. Egypt's spending is given as $288 million plus a further US $20 million.
- Supports: Suez – **severity (trade reduction / cost)**, **length of disruption** (8 years), **multilateral clearance**.
- Key figures: about $10 billion global cost (UN estimate); more than one year of clearance.
- Reliability: Contemporary. The $10bn figure is a UN estimate quoted by Time, and the search summary may have drawn some of these figures from neighbouring Time pieces. Check them on the page before citing.

### 7. Time (June 1975) 'Middle East: A Watershed Week for Egypt's Sadat' and 'Egypt: The Canal Reborn'
- URLs: https://time.com/archive/6846857/middle-east-a-watershed-week-for-egypts-sadat/ ; https://time.com/archive/6842291/egypt-the-canal-reborn/
- Type: news (magazine).
- Summary: Sadat timed the reopening to the anniversary of the 1967 closure. It was "one of the most jubilant moments of his presidency", coming straight after the Salzburg talks with President Ford. Ships flying the Israeli flag were still barred. About 275,000 people had returned to Port Said, and Sadat planned to make it a free-trade zone.
- Supports: Suez – **diplomacy as cooperation** (the US-brokered disengagement made the reopening possible), **regional economic growth**.
- Reliability: Contemporary. The exact issue dates are not confirmed (June 1975 is likely).

### 8. *The Spectator* (7 June 1975) 'Suez Canal', p. 11
- URL: https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/7th-june-1975/11/suez-canal
- Type: news / opinion (UK weekly).
- Summary: A skeptical British view written two days after the reopening. It calls it "one of the more significant anti-climaxes of our time" and argues that Egypt presented it as a military victory.
- Supports: Suez 1975 – a counter-view. By 1975 supertankers too large for the canal, and the Cape route, had reduced the canal's importance (**alternative factors: technology / physical alternatives**).
- Reliability: An opinion piece from a right-leaning UK weekly. The archive is free.

### 9. Michigan Daily (6 June 1975) AP report on the reopening
- URL: https://digital.bentley.umich.edu/midaily/mdp.39015071754639/265 (text: …/265/download_text)
- Type: news (student newspaper reprinting an Associated Press wire story).
- Summary: Reports that Sadat led the ceremonial convoy aboard an Egyptian destroyer on 5 June 1975, a date chosen to match the anniversary of the 1967 war.
- Supports: Suez – the exact **date the disruption ended**.
- Reliability: AP wire copy, written at the time. Useful as a free stand-in for the NYT archive.

### 10. Arndt, R. (1975) 'Suez: The Reopening', *Aramco World*, 26(5), September/October 1975
- URL: https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/197505/suez-the.reopening.htm
- Type: news feature (company magazine).
- Summary: An eyewitness account. On 5 June 1975, after a 21-gun salute, a seven-ship flotilla passed through ceremonial gates off Port Said and steamed south to Ismailia in five hours, "exactly eight years" after the closure. Sadat signed the canal over from military to civilian control. It covers the clearance in depth.
- Supports: Suez – **length of disruption**, clearance cooperation.
- Reliability: Contemporary and detailed, but published by Aramco (the Saudi oil company), so it has an industry viewpoint. Free archive.

### 11. UPI (1975) 'Foreign Affairs: Peace in the Middle East, Conflict in Ireland, Death of Franco' (UPI year-end audio review)
- URL: https://www.upi.com/Archives/Audio/1975/Foreign-Affaris-Peace-in-the-Middle-East-Conflict-in-Ireland-Death-of-Franco/
- Type: news (audio review of 1975; "Affaris" is a typo in the URL itself).
- Summary: UPI's review of 1975. It notes that no Israeli ships were allowed through the reopened canal, although other ships carrying supplies to Israel passed without incident.
- Supports: Suez – limits on how far cooperation went after the reopening.
- Reliability: Contemporary wire service. It is audio, so the summary comes from the page description.

### D. 1987: European minesweepers

**38. UPI (1987) 'Britain, France sending mine sweepers to Persian, Oman gulfs', 11 August 1987.** https://www.upi.com/Archives/1987/08/11/Britain-France-sending-mine-sweepers-to-Persian-Oman-gulfs/5251555652800/

## Things that contradict or complicate the plan
1. **European minesweepers in 1987–88 cleared "only a few dozen mines"** (UPI, 2 April 1989, item 43). Multilateral naval cooperation worked more through deterrence and keeping shipping moving than through physically clearing mines.
2. **Convoys did not stop attacks:** the Bridgeton was mined on the very first convoy (item 31), and the *Sea Isle City* was hit in Kuwaiti waters (item 35). Iraqi attacks went on into 1988 (items 44 and 48). Cooperation reduced the risk *for protected ships* but not overall incident numbers.
3. **The Suez reopening was seen by some as an anti-climax** (*Spectator*, item 8). Supertankers and the Cape route had partly replaced the canal, which is an "alternative factors" point.
4. **Clearance figures conflict:** Time gives 700,000+ explosives (Dec 1974) in one piece and 686,000 in another. The number of Yellow Fleet ships is also given as 14, 15 or 16 in different sources. Say which figure you use and where it comes from.
5. **Wire-report casualty figures changed:** the Stark's dead went from 28 to 37, and Saudi Arabia was first said to have shot down two Iranian jets, later corrected to one. Use the final official figures, and treat early wire reports as evidence of what people believed *at the time*.
