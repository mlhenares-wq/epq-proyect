## Flags: things that contradict or refine the plan

1. **Spain is not part of Aspides.** The brief mentions "the Armada's role in Atalanta and Aspides". Spain refused to join both the US-led Prosperity Guardian (December 2023) and the EU's Aspides (February 2024). The Armada's role in the region is **Atalanta**, which Spain commands from its operational headquarters at **Rota**. See items 33–37.
2. **Spain refused US use of Rota and Morón for the 2026 strikes on Iran** (March 2026). This is a good "cooperation limits" example for the Hormuz case. See item 38.
3. **Start dates for the 2026 crisis differ between sources.** The brief says the war began on 28 February 2026, and Elcano agrees. The Spanish Wikipedia gives 1 March. Infobae reports Iran's closure announcement on 2 March. Motor.es says Iran closed the strait on 4 March. The phases of the closure need to be dated carefully.
4. **Two very different figures measure how exposed Spain is to Hormuz.**
   - About 5% of Spain's 2025 oil imports physically passed through Hormuz (CORES data, via Diario de Tarragona and Esdiario).
   - About 28–35% of Spain's crude came from Gulf countries. Some of that leaves through Saudi Red Sea terminals or pipelines, so it never crosses Hormuz.
   - Use "origin" and "transit" consistently. Elcano (item 2) calls Spain's direct exposure "relatively low".
5. **The 2025 Malacca "surge" was mostly petty theft.** ReCAAP's 2025 figures, as reported in Spanish and English summaries, show 108 incidents in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore, up 74%. ReCAAP says most were minor theft, with no incidents in the most serious category. The number of incidents rose sharply, but the threat to trade did not. This matters for your likelihood and severity measures.
6. **The June 2026 re-closure was a sequence, not one event.**
   - 10 June: Iran declares a "complete" closure.
   - 14 June: Trump announces a deal to reopen the strait.
   - 20 June: Iran closes it again.
   - 23 June: Iran reopens it under a 60-day memorandum of understanding.
   - July: Iran closes it again after firing on a ship.

### Real Instituto Elcano

**1. Arteaga, F., Escribano, G. and Urbasos, I. (2024) 'Crisis del mar Rojo: inseguridad marítima y energética', *ARI* 77/2024. Madrid: Real Instituto Elcano, 10 June.**
- URL: https://www.realinstitutoelcano.org/analisis/crisis-del-mar-rojo-inseguridad-maritima-y-energetica/ (PDF: https://media.realinstitutoelcano.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/ari77-2024-arteaga-escribano-urbasos-crisis-del-mar-rojo-inseguridad-maritima-y-energetica.pdf)
- Type: think-tank report (expert, not peer-reviewed).
- Summary: Houthi insecurity in the Red Sea will keep putting pressure on shipping logistics, but its effect on oil and gas markets "seems limited, for now". The report notes that Saudi pipelines to Red Sea terminals let some oil avoid Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb.
- Key point: the shock hit logistics, not energy prices.
- Supports: S, and the severity measure (price level vs trade). Alternative factor: physical alternatives (pipelines).
- Reliability: high. Elcano is Spain's leading foreign-policy think tank and is partly government-funded.

**2. Hidalgo Pérez, M. (2026) 'Vulnerabilidades sistémicas ante la disrupción del estrecho de Ormuz', *Comentario Elcano*. Madrid: Real Instituto Elcano, 11 March.**

- Supports: H and ES. Alternative factors: supply chain design and trade concentration.
- Reliability: high. A recent and quantitative source.
