## Read this first: how these sources were checked

- **The Crossref, OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar APIs, and doi.org itself, could not be reached from this research session.** The network proxy blocked them, and page fetching was also blocked. Every item below was therefore checked through web-search results instead: university repositories, RePEc/IDEAS, publisher pages and DOAJ.
- **A DOI is only given when it appeared in a search result.** No DOI below has been typed from memory. Where a search confirmed that a paper exists but showed no DOI, the paper goes in Part B ("verified, but no DOI confirmed"). Please click each DOI link (https://doi.org/...) once yourself before you hand in.
- **The target was 30+ items with DOIs. I reached 21.** The shared web-search limit ran out partway through. Part B adds 22 more sources that were verified but have no confirmed DOI: working papers, policy briefs and expert law blogs. That makes 43 sources in all.
- **FREE** means a legal free copy turned up (open access, an author repository or a working-paper version). **PAYWALL** means you'll probably need a library login. Your school library or Google Scholar's "All versions" link may still find a free copy.

### A1. Economics: Red Sea / Suez shock, freight, trade, inflation

**1. Notteboom, T., Haralambides, H. and Cullinane, K. (2024) 'The Red Sea crisis: ramifications for vessel operations, shipping networks, and maritime supply chains', *Maritime Economics & Logistics*, 26(1), pp. 1–20.**
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1057/s41278-024-00287-z
- Type: peer-reviewed journal (written as a long editorial, not a full research article). Access: the Springer page is subscription-only. PortEconomics (https://www.porteconomics.eu/the-red-sea-crisis-ramifications-for-vessel-operations-shipping-networks-and-maritime-supply-chains/) has a summary page, and your plan's link 26 (marineinfo.org/doc/publication/391353) is the record for this same paper.
- Summary: Three leading port economists describe how the Houthi attacks, which began in mid-November 2023, pushed carriers onto the Cape of Good Hope route. They cover what that did to vessel operations, network design and supply chains.
- Supports: Case 2 (Red Sea 2023–26). Measures: freight rates, length of disruption. Alternative factors: Cape route, supply chain design.
- Reliability: top authors in a respected journal. As an editorial, it summarises more than it tests.

**2. Haralambides, H. (2024) 'The Red Sea crisis and chokepoints to trade and international shipping', *Maritime Economics & Logistics*, 26(3), pp. 367–390.**
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1057/s41278-024-00296-y
- Type: peer-reviewed. PAYWALL.
- Summary: A longer analysis of the Red Sea crisis that puts it alongside other chokepoints to world trade and shipping. I could only see the bibliographic record, not the text.
- Supports: Case 2, plus the general chokepoint comparison. Measures: trade reduction, freight rates.
- Reliability: a senior figure in maritime economics. I haven't checked its findings myself.

**3. Feyrer, J. (2021) 'Distance, trade, and income – The 1967 to 1975 closing of the Suez Canal as a natural experiment', *Journal of Development Economics*, 153, 102708.**
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2021.102708
- Type: peer-reviewed. PAYWALL, but the earlier version, NBER Working Paper 15557 (https://www.nber.org/papers/w15557), has the same title and abstract and is FREE.
- Summary: Feyrer uses the eight-year Suez closure as a "natural experiment", meaning a real-world shock that works like a controlled test. The closure suddenly made sea routes longer for some country pairs but not others. He finds that distance cuts trade (the effect is about half the size of standard estimates) and that the lost trade cut national income.
- Key figures (from the VoxEU column summarising it, your plan's link 25): a 10% fall in sea distance raises trade by about 5%, and each extra dollar of trade raises income by about 25 cents.
- Supports: Case 2 (1967–75). Measures: trade reduction, length of disruption.
- **Flag for your plan:** your link 24 (ideas.repec.org/p/red/sed011/1438.html) is a 2011 conference version of this paper. Cite the 2021 journal version, because the published numbers may differ from the working paper's.

**4. Carrière-Swallow, Y., Deb, P., Furceri, D., Jiménez, D. and Ostry, J.D. (2023) 'Shipping costs and inflation', *Journal of International Money and Finance*, 130, 102771.**
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jimonfin.2022.102771
- Type: peer-reviewed. FREE as IMF Working Paper WP/22/61 (https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2022/03/25/shipping-costs-and-inflation-515144) and as CEPR Discussion Paper DP17259.
- Summary: The authors use the Baltic Dry Index (a measure of bulk shipping costs) for 1992–2021. They find that spikes in shipping costs push up import prices, producer prices, and headline and core inflation, and that the effect lasts longer than oil or food price shocks. The effect is weaker in countries that import less, and in those whose central banks target inflation and have well-anchored inflation expectations. They also use Suez Canal closures as an "instrument" (a way of isolating cause and effect), and the result holds up.
- Supports: all cases. Measure: rise in the general price level. This is the key link from chokepoint shocks, through freight costs, to inflation.
- Reliability: IMF economists in a strong journal. Highly cited.

**5. Yap, W.Y. and Yang, D. (2024) 'Geopolitical tension and shipping network disruption: Analysis of the Red Sea crisis on container port calls', *Journal of Transport Geography*, 121, 104004.**
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtrangeo.2024.104004
- Type: peer-reviewed. PAYWALL.
- Summary: The paper uses AIS ship-tracking data on Asia–Europe and Asia–Mediterranean services. Carriers switched to the Cape route and added extra ships to keep the same number of port calls. Transshipment cargo at Eastern Mediterranean and Red Sea ports moved easily to other hubs.
- Supports: Case 2 (2023–26). Measure: length of disruption. Alternative factors: Cape route, supply chain design. Shows how the industry adapts without any state cooperation.
- Reliability: peer-reviewed, quantitative.

**6. Wang, Z., Fang, Z., Yu, J., Hu, X. and Gong, J. (2024) 'Red Sea crisis impacts on maritime shipping networks', *Heliyon*, 10(22), e40384.**
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e40384 (one search result gave this DOI. A second search confirmed the article and its number e40384 but did not show the DOI, so check it.)
- Type: peer-reviewed, open access. FREE (DOAJ: https://doaj.org/article/35fff86ef53947a095efa8024b70a39e).
- Summary: AIS data show that both the number of ships and their speeds fell after the crisis began, and that many ships sailed past the Red Sea rather than calling there. Network analysis suggests the global shipping network changed shape but stayed resilient.
- Supports: Case 2. Measures: incident and traffic numbers, length of disruption.
- Reliability: Heliyon is a broad, high-volume open-access journal, so treat it as solid but not top-tier.

**7. Bedoya-Maya, F., Beckers, J., Cant, J., Martínez-Moya, J., van Hassel, E. and Vanelslander, T. (2025) 'Container port competitiveness amid disruptions: Insights from the European maritime network during the Red Sea crisis', *Journal of Transport Geography*, 128, 104304.**

- Summary: The study covers the first half of 2024. Estimated transport-chain costs to Europe rose 3% to 13%, depending on the port. Hubs near Gibraltar kept their connections and saw the smallest cost rises, while feeder ports near Suez lost connections.
- Key figure: transport-chain costs up 3–13%.
- Supports: Case 2. Measures: freight costs, trade reduction (by region). Alternative factor: supply chain design.
- Reliability: a strong University of Antwerp port-economics team.

**8. Rodríguez-Díaz, E., Alcaide, J.I. and García-Llave, R. (2024) 'Challenges and security risks in the Red Sea: Impact of Houthi attacks on maritime traffic', *Journal of Marine Science and Engineering*, 12(11), 1900.**

### A2. Economics: piracy and trade (Somalia comparison)

**12. Bensassi, S. and Martínez-Zarzoso, I. (2012) 'How costly is modern maritime piracy to the international community?', *Review of International Economics*, 20(5), pp. 869–883.**
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/roie.12000
- Type: peer-reviewed. FREE via the Universitat Jaume I repository (https://repositori.uji.es/items/95edfdcf-6579-46f5-80a3-09dc8e840d60/full).
- Summary: A gravity model of Europe–Asia trade for 1999–2008. (A gravity model predicts trade between two countries from their size and distance.) The paper finds that hijackings cut trade because they make delivery times less reliable.
- Key figures: ten extra hijacked ships go with roughly an 11% drop in exports, and piracy's trade cost is estimated at about US$24.5 billion a year.
- Supports: the Somalia comparison and Case 3 (Malacca piracy). Measure: trade reduction. It shows that piracy, not just war, has measurable trade costs.
- Reliability: peer-reviewed. Co-author Martínez-Zarzoso is a Spanish trade economist.

**13. Shortland, A. and Vothknecht, M. (2011) 'Combating "maritime terrorism" off the coast of Somalia', *European Journal of Political Economy*, 27(S1), pp. 133–151.**
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejpoleco.2011.03.004
- Type: peer-reviewed. FREE as DIW Berlin Discussion Paper 1079 (https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.455066.de/publikationen/diskussionspapiere/2010_1079/combating__maritime_terrorism__off_the_coast_of_somalia.html).
- Summary: Assesses the international naval mission in the Gulf of Aden in 2008–10, including EU Atalanta, using statistics and interviews. It calls the counter-piracy effort only a partial success: attacks stayed high and were pushed out of the guarded transit corridor into the open ocean.
- Supports: the Somalia comparison, testing whether joint naval operations work. **This is a key counter-argument:** in the short run, cooperation moved the threat elsewhere rather than removing it.
- Reliability: peer-reviewed, a mix of numbers and interviews.

**14. Percy, S. and Shortland, A. (2013) 'The business of piracy in Somalia', *Journal of Strategic Studies*, 36(4).**
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/01402390.2012.750242 (shown in one source, a REF impact case study)
- Type: peer-reviewed. PAYWALL. FREE as DIW Discussion Paper 1033 (2010) (https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.454730.de/publikationen/diskussionspapiere/2010_1033/the_business_of_piracy_in_somalia.html).
- Summary: Argues that Somali piracy is a land-based business, which actually benefits from local stability, so naval patrols alone can't remove it.
- Supports: the Somalia comparison. Alternative factor: type of threat. A useful limit on the "cooperation works" argument.
- Reliability: peer-reviewed in a leading strategic-studies journal.

### A3. Somali piracy, Operation Atalanta and maritime security cooperation (Bueger and others)

**15. Bueger, C. (2015) 'What is maritime security?', *Marine Policy*, 53, pp. 159–164.**
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpol.2014.12.005

**17. Bueger, C. and Edmunds, T. (2017) 'Beyond seablindness: a new agenda for maritime security studies', *International Affairs*, 93(6), pp. 1293–1311.**

### A4. International law

**18. Treves, T. (2009) 'Piracy, law of the sea, and use of force: developments off the coast of Somalia', *European Journal of International Law*, 20(2), pp. 399–414.**
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/ejil/chp027
- Type: peer-reviewed. FREE PDF (Roma Tre repository: https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/6144).
- Summary: A former judge of the law-of-the-sea tribunal (ITLOS) explains how UN Security Council Resolution 1816 (2008) and later resolutions widened anti-piracy powers, notably by letting navies enter Somali waters. The resolutions also say they create no new customary law. He shows that navies hesitated to detain pirates because of human-rights concerns, which led to transfer deals with Kenya.
- Supports: the Somalia comparison. Cooperation factors: enforcement of laws, formal frameworks. It also explains why few pirates were prosecuted.
- Reliability: a very authoritative author.

**19. 'The United States and partners use force against the Houthis to protect freedom of navigation in the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden' (2024), Contemporary Practice of the United States, *American Journal of International Law*, 118(2), pp. 366–373.**

- Type: peer-reviewed journal section (the journal's editors record and analyse US legal practice). Access: Cambridge Core. Check whether it's free; it may be paywalled.
- Summary: Records the January 2024 US–UK strikes on Houthi targets and the legal reasons given for them: self-defence under Article 51 of the UN Charter, and the protection of freedom of navigation.
- Supports: Case 2 (2023–26), on the legal status of the Houthi attacks and the response. Cooperation factors: joint operations, enforcement.
- Reliability: AJIL is the leading US international-law journal. This section describes the US government's position; it isn't a neutral verdict.

### B1. Economics working papers and policy briefs

**22. Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas (2026) *Working Paper 2609*, April 2026 (on a closure of the Strait of Hormuz).** https://www.dallasfed.org/~/media/documents/research/papers/2026/wp2609.pdf. Companion note (20 March 2026): https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0320

### B2. International law: Hormuz, transit passage, the Houthis

**34. Lott, A. (2026) 'The legality of Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz', *EJIL: Talk!* (blog of the European Journal of International Law).** https://www.ejiltalk.org/the-legality-of-irans-closure-of-the-strait-of-hormuz/
- Type: expert blog (not peer-reviewed, but written by an academic specialist in straits). FREE.
- Summary: Analyses the closure under the law of naval warfare and neutral states' rights. Notes that the belligerents (the US, Israel and Iran) are all **non-parties to UNCLOS**. Iran argues that transit passage is not customary law and binds only UNCLOS parties, so only innocent passage applies, and innocent passage can be suspended. The opposing view, also reported, is that transit passage is customary and cannot be suspended, even in war.
- Supports: Case 1 (2026). Cooperation factors: formal frameworks, enforcement of laws.

**35. Opinio Juris (2026) 'The closure of the Strait of Hormuz by Iran: considerations over the law of the sea and law of naval warfare', 6 May 2026.** http://opiniojuris.org/2026/05/06/the-closure-of-the-strait-of-hormuz-by-iran-considerations-over-the-law-of-the-sea-and-law-of-naval-warfare/
- Type: expert blog. FREE. Supports: Case 1, legal framework.

**36. American Society of International Law (2026) 'Charges for passage in the Strait of Hormuz: the law governing tolls and service fees in straits used for international navigation', *ASIL Insights*, 30(10).** https://asil.org/insights/volume-30-issue-10/
- Type: expert legal briefing. FREE.
- Summary: Neither legal regime gives a power to charge simply for passing through a strait. UNCLOS allows charges only for specific services provided to a ship. Relevant to Iran's 2026 "coordination" and permit demands.

**37. Völkerrechtsblog (2026) 'Iran's legal strategy in Hormuz'.** https://voelkerrechtsblog.org/irans-legal-strategy-in-hormuz/

## Points that affect the student's plan

1. **Red Sea 2023–24 had a smaller effect on inflation than you might expect.** The ECB (27), VoxEU (28) and Kiel (24) all describe the effect on prices and trade as limited or muted, apart from Europe-bound routes. Report this honestly: severity was high for traffic numbers and freight rates but low for the general price level. Carrière-Swallow et al. (4) explain the time lag and why it varies by country.
2. **Joint naval operations can move a threat rather than remove it.** Shortland & Vothknecht (13) found that the 2008–10 Gulf of Aden mission pushed pirates into the open ocean. Percy & Shortland (14) argue that piracy is solved on land. Use both as counter-arguments to "cooperation reduces likelihood".
3. **Law on paper does not equal enforcement.** Kontorovich & Art (43) found that at most 1.47% of piracies were prosecuted. Treves (18) shows that navies hesitated to detain pirates. Both are useful for your "enforcement of laws" factor.
4. **Hormuz 2026: the legal framework is contested.** Iran, the US and Israel are all non-parties to UNCLOS (34, 37). So the "formal framework" for Hormuz is weaker than for Malacca, where the coastal states are UNCLOS parties. That could help explain why cooperation achieved less there.
5. **Forecasts of 2026 oil prices differ a lot.** Kiel (23) estimates about +12%, while the Dallas Fed (22) has WTI at $110–167 depending on how long the closure lasts. Both are scenarios. Use actual Brent data for what really happened.
6. **Plan link 24** is an older conference version of Feyrer, and **plan link 26** is the Notteboom et al. editorial. Cite the journal versions (items 3 and 1).
